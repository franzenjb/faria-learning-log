See why turning both directions preserves the angle

Set a latitude above, then rotate both directions together. The angle between them stays the same.

What makes the two angles match?

The line from Earth's center through your feet is your local straight up. Your level horizon is perpendicular to it. The polar axis is perpendicular to the equatorial plane.

Turn both sides of an angle through the same 90°, and their separation stays the same. The latitude angle becomes the angle from your horizon to the pole.

The reference matters: at 39° N, the pole is 39° above your northern horizon and 51° away from straight up. Straight up itself is always 90° above the horizon.

Why use the pole instead of Polaris exactly?

Polaris is close to the north celestial pole, but it is slightly offset. This experiment places the star exactly at the pole to isolate the geometry. It uses a spherical Earth and a level astronomical horizon. Precise observations also account for the real Earth's shape, Polaris's offset, refraction, instrument error and horizon dip.
