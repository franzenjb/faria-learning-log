Day 1 · How big is the world? · c. 240 BC

A well, a stick, and the size of the Earth

At noon on the summer solstice the Sun shone straight down a well at Syene. The same moment in Alexandria, a vertical stick, a gnomon, cast a shadow of about 7.2°, one fiftieth of a circle.

The Sun is so far away its rays arrive parallel. If the ground were flat, both sticks would cast the same shadow. They did not, so the ground curves, and by Euclid’s rule for parallel lines the shadow angle at Alexandria equals the angle between the two cities at the center of the Earth. Fifty times the distance between them, measured in stadia, is the whole way around.

Day 1 · A first look · full story on Day 3

Every flat map tells a lie on purpose.

Peel a globe and something has to stretch. A projection is a choice about what to keep true and what to give up.

Mercator kept angles and shapes true — a compass line stays straight — and paid with size. On his map Greenland looks the size of Africa; Africa is really fourteen Greenlands. You met this trap in the opening quiz. On Day 3 we take projections apart properly.

Kept: shapes and compass lines.Given up: size — and the sacrifice grows toward the poles.
One Greenland, two maps

Day 1 · Chapter 1 · 1 of 10 · Latitude is the easy one

Walk north. Polaris climbs by the same angle.

Drag the person along the globe, or move the latitude bar. One angle is your latitude at Earth’s centre. The other is Polaris’s height above your horizon.

40° latitude = 40° star altitudeAt 40° N, Polaris stands 40° above the horizon.

Why must they match? Euclid, not Pythagoras. Your horizon is perpendicular to the radial line from Earth’s centre to you. The equatorial plane is perpendicular to Earth’s axis. Your sightline to Polaris is effectively parallel to that axis. Rotate both sides of an angle by 90° and its measure stays the same.

The model uses the exact north celestial pole. Polaris is about 0.7° away from it, so a real navigation sight needs a small correction.

Day 1 · Chapter 1 · 2 of 10 · Longitude has no landmarks

Two ships, same latitude, hundreds of miles apart.

Latitude comes free from the sky: the equator and the poles are real places, a natural north–south scale. Longitude has no such landmarks. The Earth spins, so every meridian looks exactly like every other.

One degree of latitude is 60 arcminutes anywhere on Earth, and each arcminute is one nautical mile — fixed at 1,852 meters in 1929. A degree of longitude is 60 nautical miles only at the equator, and shrinks to nothing at the poles. East–west distance takes more than an angle.

Same parallel, unknown gap.Both ships read Polaris at 42° N. Neither can say how far east or west the other is.
One parallel, two ships, no east–west landmarks

Day 1 · Chapter 1 · 3 of 10 · The cost of not knowing

October 22, 1707. Four warships hit the wrong rocks.

Admiral Clowdesley Shovell's fleet believed it was safely west of England, in open Atlantic. It was in the Isles of Scilly. Four Royal Navy ships wrecked at night; about 2,000 sailors died — some historical counts put it between 1,400 and 2,000.

They knew their latitude. They did not know their longitude.

One number missing.A north–south fix was routine. The east–west fix did not exist.
A night fleet, a wrong longitude

Day 1 · Chapter 1 · 4 of 10 · 1714

Parliament puts a price on the answer.

Seven years after Scilly, the Longitude Act offered £20,000 — millions in today's money — for a practical way to find longitude at sea to within half a degree.

Half a degree of longitude at the equator is 30 nautical miles. That was the bar: cross an ocean and land within 30 miles of the truth.

£20,000 for half a degree.One of the first great science prizes — open to anyone, won by a carpenter.
The prize that started a fifty-year race

Day 1 · Chapter 1 · 5 of 10 · Longitude is a time

Latitude is an angle. Longitude is a time.

Spin the Earth. Pinch or scroll to zoom. Tap anywhere on it to stand there.

The Earth's axis points at Polaris. Stand anywhere in the north and the star's height above your horizon, measured with a quadrant or sextant, is your latitude. Spin to the south and there is no star at the pole, only the Southern Cross pointing to an empty spot in the sky.

Tap the globe to place yourself.Drag to spin · pinch or scroll to zoom

The Earth turns 15° every hour. If you know the time back home when the Sun is highest where you are, you know how far around the world you have gone.

Drag the ship. Then slide the Greenwich chronometer until the Sun stands highest over the ship (local noon). Read the clock. Every hour of difference is 15° of longitude. Without a clock that keeps home time at sea you need the lunar distance method instead, an hour of arithmetic for every fix. That is why Harrison's chronometer mattered.

Day 1 · Chapter 1 · 6 of 10 · There was a star method

The Moon is a clock — if you have an hour to read it.

The Moon crosses its own width against the stars in about an hour. Measure the gap to a bright star with a sextant, look it up in printed tables, compute, and you have the time back home — and with it, longitude.

On a rolling deck the sight was hard and the arithmetic took more than an hour, with a dozen corrections. A slip anywhere spoiled the fix. It worked, barely, for patient experts — not for every ship, every night.

Right in principle, brutal in practice.The method was real. The hour of math on a heaving deck was the problem.
Moon to star: one angle, one hour of math

Day 1 · Chapter 1 · 7 of 10 · Galileo's offer

Jupiter's moons eclipse on schedule, like clockwork.

Galileo's idea: publish the eclipse timetable for Jupiter's moons. Any observer who watches Io vanish knows the time at the observatory — compare with local noon, and you have longitude.

It worked well on land, and kings used it to remap their coasts. On a pitching ship, no telescope could hold a moon hundreds of millions of miles away. The clock in the sky stayed on land.

Land: yes. Sea: no.Day 2 opens with this telescope — and what else those moons revealed.
A public clock, 600 million miles away

Day 1 · Chapter 1 · 9 of 10 · Why not just bring a clock?

A pendulum keeps time. A ship keeps moving.

The best clocks of 1700 swung a pendulum: superb in a hallway, useless in a swell. Every roll and pitch stole seconds or gifted them.

Four seconds of clock error is one nautical mile of longitude. A pendulum at sea could lose that in a single wave.

4 seconds = 1 nautical mile.The sea eats pendulums. The clock had to be reinvented, not packed carefully.
A hallway clock meets the Atlantic

Day 1 · Chapter 1 · 10 of 10 · The clock that could travel

Make a clock the sea cannot shake.

John Harrison, a self-taught carpenter, spent 31 years building sea clocks of wood and brass with no pendulum at all. His fourth, H4, crossed to Barbados in 1764 and kept time to about 5 seconds in 81 days at sea.

Five seconds is about a mile of longitude. The prize bar was 30 miles. The longitude problem was over — and Cook carried Harrison's design on his second voyage. Day 2 picks up there.

H4: ~5 seconds in 81 days.Chapter closed. The rest of the course is what we built on top of that answer.
Springs and balances instead of a pendulum

Day 1 · 1761 and 1769 · The whole world looks up

Venus crosses the Sun, and the solar system gets a size

Halley's idea, parallax: two observers far apart on Earth see Venus cross the Sun on slightly different lines. Time both crossings. The difference gives the distance to the Sun.

Tap the globe to post one observer, tap again for the second, then play the transit. Their stopwatches disagree, and that disagreement is a ruler for the solar system.

Venus passes between us and the Sun every 584 days. Almost every time it slips above or below the Sun, because its orbit is tilted 3.4°.

Only when the lineup falls where the two orbits cross does Venus show on the Sun's face: a pair eight years apart, then more than a century of nothing. The dates below are computed live from the orbits, not typed in.

Day 1 · A first look · full story on Day 3

A map is either lines or cells.

Vector: the world as points, lines, and polygons. A road is a line with a name; a shelter is a point with an address; a flood zone is a polygon.

Raster: the world as a grid of cells. A satellite photo, a rainfall surface, an elevation model — every cell holds one number.

Same coast, two recordings. Every GIS layer you will ever touch is one or the other — Day 3 opens with this choice.

Vector asks "what and where." Raster asks "how much, everywhere."Lines for boundaries. Cells for surfaces.

Day 2 · Galileo · January 1610 · Padua

Four little stars that would not stay still

Galileo pointed a new telescope at Jupiter and saw three, then four, small stars in a line beside it. Night after night they changed sides. They were moons, and they circled Jupiter, not the Earth.

This was the first thing anyone had seen orbiting something other than the Earth. Aristotle and Ptolemy had the whole sky turning about us. Here was a small system turning about a planet, and a planet that carried its moons along as it moved. If Jupiter could do that, so could the Earth.

  • Io · 1.77 days · 5.9 Jupiter radii
  • Europa · 3.55 days · 9.4 radii
  • Ganymede · 7.15 days · 15.0 radii
  • Callisto · 16.69 days · 26.3 radii

Drag the picture to tilt it: edge-on is what Galileo saw, from above is what was really happening. Periods and distances are real; starting positions are an illustration, not an ephemeris.

Io vanishes into Jupiter’s shadow about every 42 hours, and the whole world sees it at the same instant. Galileo saw a clock in the sky: predict the eclipse, compare the predicted time with your local time, and the difference is your longitude.

It worked on land and fixed the map of France. On a rolling deck nobody could hold Jupiter in the eyepiece. And in 1676 Ole Rømer noticed the eclipses ran late when Earth was far from Jupiter: light itself takes time to arrive.

Press the buttonand watch the light leave Io. It reaches Earth 35 to 52 minutes later, depending where Earth is.

One eclipse, two clocks: where are you?

Florence, at 11.25° E, records Io’s eclipse at 9:14 pm local mean time. Your clock, set to your own local noon, reads 7:14 pm at the same instant. Where are you?

Day 2 · Galileo · Sidereus Nuncius · 1610

How tall is a mountain on the Moon?

Galileo saw bright points on the dark side of the Moon, well past the terminator, the line between day and night. They were peaks, still catching sunlight while the ground around them was in shadow.
