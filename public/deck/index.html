1 · Where am I?

Latitude is an angle. Longitude is a time.

Polaris sits almost exactly over the North Pole. Its height above your horizon, in degrees, is your latitude.

Drag the observer along the Earth. The horizon tilts, the star does not move, and the angle between them is the angle you are north of the equator. Sailors measured it with a quadrant or sextant for a thousand years.

The Earth turns 15° every hour. If you know the time back home when the Sun is highest where you are, you know how far around the world you have gone.

Drag the ship. Then slide the Greenwich chronometer until the Sun stands highest over the ship (local noon). Read the clock. Every hour of difference is 15° of longitude. Without a clock that keeps home time at sea, this is impossible, which is why Harrison's chronometer mattered.

2 · How big is the world? · c. 240 BC

A well, a stick, and the size of the Earth

At noon on the summer solstice the Sun shone straight down a well at Syene. The same moment in Alexandria, a vertical stick cast a shadow of about 7.2°, one fiftieth of a circle.

The Sun is so far away its rays arrive parallel. If the ground were flat, both sticks would cast the same shadow. They did not, so the ground curves, and the shadow angle at Alexandria equals the angle between the two cities at the center of the Earth. Fifty times the distance between them is the whole way around.

3 · January 1610 · Padua

Four little stars that would not stay still

Galileo pointed a new telescope at Jupiter and saw three, then four, small stars in a line beside it. Night after night they changed sides. They were moons, and they circled Jupiter, not the Earth.

This was the first thing anyone had seen orbiting something other than the Earth. Aristotle and Ptolemy had the whole sky turning about us. Here was a small system turning about a planet, and a planet that carried its moons along as it moved. If Jupiter could do that, so could the Earth.

  • Io · 1.77 days · 5.9 Jupiter radii
  • Europa · 3.55 days · 9.4 radii
  • Ganymede · 7.15 days · 15.0 radii
  • Callisto · 16.69 days · 26.3 radii

4 · Sidereus Nuncius · 1610

How tall is a mountain on the Moon?

Galileo saw bright points on the dark side of the Moon, well past the line between day and night. They were peaks, still catching sunlight while the ground around them was in shadow.

A peak lit from across the terminator must poke above the Sun's grazing ray. If the peak is a distance d into the dark side on a Moon of radius R, Pythagoras gives its height: h = √(R² + d²) − R. Galileo measured d as about one twentieth of the Moon's width and got more than four miles, higher than any mountain he knew on Earth.
