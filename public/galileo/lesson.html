How Tall Is That Mountain?

A bright peak in darkness gave Galileo a ruler. Move the mountain, make it taller, or change the sunlight.

Or drag the mountain's round peak handle.

Side View · Height Exaggerated For Clarity · Sun Angle Measured At The Center Marker

What Galileo Saw

Through his telescope, he saw little bright points beyond the edge of the sunlit Moon. These were high peaks catching the Sun before the lower ground. A more distant bright peak needs a taller mountain.

From A Bright Spot To Four Miles

He compared the gap to the Moon's full width. Knowing roughly how big the Moon was, he could work out how tall a peak must be to reach the sunlight. In this modern example, a peak about 93 miles beyond the daylight edge needs to stand about 4 miles high — 21,120 feet.

Try lowering the mountain to 2 miles. Its peak goes dark. Raise it back to 4 miles, or raise the Sun: the light reaches it again.

Sources And What This Model Simplifies

Galileo wrote more than four Italian miles in The Starry Messenger (1610). Those historical units are not identical to today's miles. The four-mile setting here is a modern teaching example, not a reproduction of an exact historical measurement.

This model uses a smooth spherical Moon about 2,160 modern miles across, parallel sunlight, and one radial mountain. Distance is measured along the ground from the fixed center marker; the result also shows distance from the moving daylight edge. The Sun angle is its height above the local horizon at that center marker. The real Moon has rough terrain, and a telescope's projected view needs additional geometry. This is a side view, not a telescope photograph. Height is stretched to make a small mountain visible; the Sun's drawn slope is stretched by the same amount. Reflections, the Sun's apparent size, and shadows cast by other mountains are omitted.
