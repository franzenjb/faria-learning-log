# Superpowers

One move per slide. The notes carry the script. The screen gets one short line per step. Cream, ink, one red. Dark only for Scilly and Jupiter. Back restarts the move.

| Slide | Story beat | The move | Words on screen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Where Am I? (cover) | The question, before any map. | The meridian draws, then stars wheel around Polaris and leave a long-exposure trail. | Where Am I? |
| The Isles of Scilly | 22 October 1707. Shovell is sure. The rocks are not. | Slow push-in on the engraving, fog rolling, lanterns flickering, one ship drifting onto rocks that turn red. | One line a step: the date, then “They were sure.”, then “The rocks.”, then “Where am I?” |
| Where Am I? (Havana) | Due south of Havana is not a country. | Question fades. The globe dollies in, a red dot pulses on Havana, and the meridian drops like a plumb line while latitude ticks 23° N to 20° S. South America slides east. A 1.1° tick at Punta Pariñas. The line falls into empty ocean. | Question and options, then only “None. Open Pacific.” |
| Rome’s Parallel | Boston, not New York, shares Rome’s latitude. The Gulf Stream is why Italy is warm. | The same globe flies from Havana to the Atlantic. The 42° N parallel races east from Boston and lights Rome. A warm current animates across the ocean. | Question and options, then “Boston. Same parallel.” |
| Reno or Los Angeles | The coast lies. Reno is farther west. | The camera dives from the globe into California and Nevada. Both meridians fall. The coast rotates until its southeast slant is obvious and the 1.6° gap opens. | Question and options, then “Reno, by 1.6°.” |
| Call a Square | A stranger needs one cell, not a story. | The graticule grows on the globe. Cells snap into a bingo card. Tap any square on the iPad and it is called. | One line a step, ending “G-4.” A tap replaces it with the called square. |
| The Mile | Whoever holds power sets the ruler. The sea uses the Earth. | Dots march a Roman mile, morph into a furlong field, then the view zooms until one minute of latitude fills the screen. | “1,000 paces.” then “5,280 feet.” then “One minute of Earth.” |
| A Well at Syene | The librarian reads a report and the Earth curves. | Sunlight falls straight down the well and sparkles on the water. A scroll unrolls. Then the ground itself bends. | “The well is bright.” then “His stick still shadows.” then “The Earth curves.” |
| A Stick and a Shadow | 7.2° at Alexandria is the same angle at the center of the Earth. | The shadow sweeps as the sun climbs to noon. The camera pulls out through the Earth until the 7.2° wedge is the whole picture. | “Straight down the well.” then “7.2°, north.” then “The same wedge.” |
| The Whole Earth | Fifty slices. About 25,000 miles. | The 50 slices spin into place around the globe, the walked slice staying red. | “50 slices.” then “5,000 stadia.” then “About 25,000 miles.” |
| The Easy Number | Two captains, one star, one latitude. East is invisible. | Drag either ship. Polaris stays put. The miles between them tick live. | “Same star. Same latitude.” then “The star cannot say east.” |
| Fists and the Pole Star | A fist is a rough sextant. | Drag the person from the equator toward the pole. Polaris climbs, and the fist-count matches the latitude live. | “One fist, about 10°.” then “The star’s height is the latitude.” |
| Twelve Knuckles | Babylon counted 12, then 60, on a hand. | Knuckles light and snap to degrees: 3, then 12, then 60. | “Twelve.” then “Sixty.” |
| Not Always the Pole Star | Hipparchus, about 130 BC. The pole wanders. | Drag to scrub 26,000 years. The pole traces its circle through Thuban, Polaris, and Vega. | The year and the gap, one line, live. |
| A Rolling Deck | A lieutenant tries to hold the star. The deck will not let him. | The horizon pitches and rolls. The plumb swings. The star will not stay on the wire. | “Hold the star.” |
| One Circle, One Day | Greenwich, noon. A full turn is 24 hours, 15° an hour. | The Earth turns under the sun. The terminator sweeps. Twenty-four slices light hour by hour. A ship two slices west flashes noon. | One line a step, from the question to “2 hours = 30°.” |
| Was 1900 a Leap Year? | No. Power also sets the calendar. | The year-circle fails to close. Each reform shrinks the gap. Calendar pages rip away: 10 days in 1582, 11 in 1752. | “No.” then “A degree a day, likely.” then “10 days, then 11.” |
| Two Clocks | Local noon is easy. Greenwich is the hard clock. | Two clocks tick. The ship’s noon flashes. The gap converts live into degrees as you drag Greenwich. | “4 hours × 15° = 60° west.” |
| A Clock in the Sky | Padua, 7 January 1610. Io is a clock the whole Earth shares. | Io glides into Jupiter’s shadow in real time. An Earth clock and a ship clock both stop on that instant and disagree. | “What will you see tomorrow night?” then “One instant. Two clocks.” |
| Light Takes Time | Paris, 1676. Rømer was timing a clock and found that light travels. | Pulses leave Jupiter and arrive late when Earth is on the far side. About 22 minutes. We now say nearer 17. | “Late, when Earth is far.” |
| The Moon as a Clock | Maskelyne, 1767. The Moon is the astronomers’ clock, and it is hard work. | The Moon slides against a star. The lunar-distance arc measures itself, live, while the deck heaves. | “Half a degree an hour.” |
| The Longitude Act | London, 1714. Up to £20,000. Newton says the watch has not been made. | The prize counter climbs to £20,000. Newton’s sentence types itself out. | “£20,000.” then the quote. |
| A Watch That Could Travel | A carpenter from Barrow. About forty years. He never officially wins. | H1 shrinks through H2 and H3 into H4 in the palm. The decades fill. Money trickles in. His age ticks to 80. | “About 5 seconds.” then “Not the prize.” |
| The Prime Meridian | Washington, October 1884. Charts and the navy had already chosen. | Twenty-five dots cast votes on a ticker. The count lands 22 to 1. Greenwich’s zero line snaps onto the globe. | “22 to 1.” |
| The Date Line | Same noon, two dates. Politics bends the line. | The sun sweeps the globe until it is noon on both sides at once. The calendars flip. The jagged line draws the Kiribati and Samoa jogs. | “Thursday noon. Wednesday noon.” |
| Where Am I? (close) | The phone answers the way the ships could not. | Satellites orbit. Timing pulses converge on one dot. The question answers itself. | “Where am I?” then “Clocks in the sky.” |
| Appendix | After the questions, if they want the star day. | A quiet divider. No new trick. | “Not in the 28 minutes.” |
| One Degree More | The stars come back 4 minutes early. | The Earth steps along its orbit and the extra degree swings into the sun, not to scale. | “23 h 56 m.” then “+1° = 4 minutes.” |
