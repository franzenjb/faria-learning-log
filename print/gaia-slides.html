What Gaia was built to measure

As Earth goes around the Sun, a nearby star seems to shift a little against the far stars.

That shift is the star's parallax.

Measure the shift, and you have the distance.

A ladder of angles

Each rung is sixty times finer: 1° = 60′, and 1′ = 60″. The sixty is Babylonian, like the sixty minutes in an hour.

1′
one arcminute

A dime, seen face-on, at about 200 feet (about 60 meters).

1′ = 1/60 of a degree.

Face-on means looking at the face of the dime, its full 18 mm width, never its edge. The path is a picture of the distance; at 200 feet a real dime is almost too small to see.

One arcsecond already leaves the solar system

A star with a parallax of 1″ is one parsec away, about 3.26 light-years. Even the nearest star shifts by less than that.

It watched for ten and a half

The spacecraft and the science stayed healthy, so the mission was extended, until the cold gas that kept it spinning and pointing ran out on 15 January 2025.

Switched off on 27 March 2025, and left in a retirement orbit around the Sun.

Not every star in the Milky Way

The Galaxy holds a few hundred billion stars. Gaia measured nearly two billion, roughly one in a hundred.

Many others are too faint, or hidden behind dust.

A huge census of the brighter stars, not a complete count.

Gaia-Enceladus: a galaxy inside ours

In 2018 Gaia showed that much of the inner halo came from a galaxy that merged with ours about 10 billion years ago. Its stars still move like debris from the crash.

The snail-shell wobble

Also in 2018: sort nearby stars by height and up-and-down speed, and they curl into a spiral.

The disk is still ringing, like a pond after a stone, probably because the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy passed through it.

Sleeping black holes

Gaia saw stars sway around partners that give off no light.

  • BH1 and BH2: found in Data Release 3, confirmed in 2023.
  • BH3, announced 16 April 2024: about 33 times the Sun's mass, about 1,926 light-years away.
  • The most massive star-sized black hole then known in our galaxy.
