A short lesson on what noon was before the railroads: [Before Time Zones: Every Town Its Own Noon](/topics/117-before-time-zones-every-town-its-own-noon/). Every Greek and Roman town kept its own sun time. Daylight was split into 12 hours, so a summer hour in Rome was about 75 minutes and a winter hour about 45. Hipparchus saw that timing the same lunar eclipse in two cities would give their longitude, and in Book 8 of the *Geography* Ptolemy listed noteworthy cities as hours east or west of Alexandria. Shared clocks waited for the train and the telegraph. U.S. railroads adopted standard zones on 18 November 1883, the Day of Two Noons — 143 years before this page was written. Greenwich became the world's zero the next year.

A second lesson from this day: [Pytheas of Massalia: Tin, Latitude, and the Round Earth](/topics/118-pytheas-of-massalia/). About 325 BC, Pytheas measured Massalia's latitude with a noon gnomon, sailed to the tin coasts of Britain, and described the long northern days, the tides and the Moon, and a pole with no star on it. His book is lost; Strabo called him a liar; later readers have mostly sided with Pytheas. [Lesson PDF](/downloads/Pytheas_of_Massalia_Tin_Latitude_and_the_Round_Earth_Faria_2026-11-18.pdf).
