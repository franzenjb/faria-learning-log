## Polaris
- A **triple** (Aa F7Ib Cepheid, Ab close dwarf imaged by Hubble in 2006, B found by Herschel in 1779), not a single star.
- **Altitude of Polaris = latitude**; it sits ~0.66° from the true pole.
- **Precession** (~26,000 y): Thuban in the pyramid age, Pytheas saw an empty pole, Polaris closest ~2100, then Gamma Cephei, then Vega ~14,500.
- **Cepheids** (SEF-ee-id): Leavitt's 1912 period–luminosity law; Polaris period ~3.97 days, amplitude and period both misbehaving.
- Distance: Hipparcos ~432 ly vs Turner ~323 ly vs Gaia ~446.5 ly; the nearest Cepheid calibrates the cosmic distance ladder.
- Names: cynosure, lodestar, Stella Maris, Dhruva, Arabic axle/needle, Old English Tir.
- **Sonnet 116** line by line: "the star to every wandering bark, / Whose worth's unknown, although his height be taken" — bark = ship; height taken = measuring altitude for latitude. The GIS talk in one couplet.

## Bowditch
- Salem, left school at ~10, indentured to a ship chandler at 12; taught himself Latin for Newton and French for Laplace.
- Five voyages 1795–1803; found **more than 8,000** errors in Moore's *Practical Navigator*.
- *The New American Practical Navigator* (1802), still **NGA Pub. No. 9**; sailors just say **"Bowditch."**
- Taught a whole twelve-man crew lunars (cook included, by the traditional telling).
- Polaris correction via Big Dipper clock; translated Laplace; early American actuary.
- **Bowditch curves** (1815) = Lissajous figures = oscilloscope XY shapes — Jeff's image.

## Sidereal day and latitude sailing
- **Sidereal** (sy-DEER-ee-ul) from Latin *sidus* "star"; day = **23 h 56 m 4.09 s** because Earth must turn ~1° extra for the Sun after each 360° star-turn.
- ≈ 366.24 sidereal days per solar year; stars rise ~4 min earlier each night.
- Polaris's 0.7° circle → up to **±42 nmi** latitude error; Jeff: uncorrected track weaves **north–south** like an oscilloscope; tables flatten it.
- **Latitude sailing / "running down the latitude"**: hold a parallel because longitude at sea was unsolved. Columbus 1492: Canaries ~28°N, then mostly west; Bahamas landfall ~24°N.
- Rhumb line (constant bearing) vs great circle (shortest).

## Harvard Computers
- **Computer** = person who computes (Brathwaite 1613); machine sense mid-20th c. Nautical Almanac human computers (Mary Edwards) tie to Bowditch.
- Pickering's Harvard College Observatory: women computers from early 1880s; ~25¢/hr; ~500,000 glass plates; "Pickering's Harem."
- **Fleming** (housemaid→curator): Horsehead Nebula 1888. **Maury**: finer spectra. **Cannon**: O B A F G K M, ~225k in HD Catalogue / ~350k lifetime. **Leavitt**: 1908 notice, 1912 Circular 173 period–luminosity law; Mittag-Leffler Nobel letter 1925 too late. **Payne** 1925: stars mostly hydrogen.
- Later: Sobel's *The Glass Universe*; DASCH; Leavitt crater.

## From a Venus transit to the Cepheids
The distance ladder under Leavitt's law and under the Polaris yardstick. Kepler's third law (a^3/T^2) gives **relative** orbits: Venus a = 0.723 AU, and a simplified circular gap at inferior conjunction of about **0.277 AU**. That is a semimajor axis, not today's distance. The 1761 and 1769 Venus transits, Halley's **durations** and Delisle's **contact times**, turn the ratio into a length by way of the **differential** parallax (Venus minus the Sun), not one angle and not latitude alone. Tahiti: Cook and Green, 3 June 1769. Siberia in 1769 is **Yakutsk** (Isleniev); Tobolsk was Chappe in **1761**. The black drop blurred the contacts. Encke's 1824 parallax was still about 2.5 percent off. The modern au is defined: **149,597,870,700 m**, about **92.96 million miles**. Earth's orbit is then the baseline for stellar parallax: the full shift is **2p**, and parsecs = 1/p. Bessel, 1838, used a **heliometer**, before photography. Leavitt 1908/1912 fixed the **slope** from a common-distance sample, not the zero point. Later parallaxes pinned the luminosity. Cepheids reach across the Milky Way and into other galaxies.
