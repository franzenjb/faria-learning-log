A conversation on predictive modeling for disaster response. The right test is temporal validation with holdout years: train on earlier years, then test on later ones, never the reverse. Ordinary random cross-validation can leak future information through time-ordered data. That is data leakage, and it can make a model look better than it is. Weather-alert and blood-drive timestamps have to be aligned on the same grain before training, or even a one-day offset can masquerade as a real weather effect. Regularization shrinks coefficients that only fit noise, which is the bias-variance tradeoff in practice. Overfitting memorizes noise instead of the underlying pattern, so a model that looks perfect on the training years can collapse when the next season arrives.

[Eratosthenes](/topics/18-eratosthenes-measures-the-earth/) is the model for what to trust. He relied on structural principles — parallel sunlight, and 7.2° as one-fiftieth of a circle — rather than any isolated measurement. The same idea applies to Red Cross disaster-response data: a coefficient that stays stable across decades is worth more than one that spikes on last year's hurricane. Invariants last; ephemeral noise does not. Resting a model on those invariants is what turns a forecast into infrastructure.
