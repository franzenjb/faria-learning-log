This page was written to join surveys that already have their own lessons. It is a reconstructed account of the method, not a transcript.

A baseline is a line on the ground whose length you actually measure. A triangle has three sides and three angles. If you know one side and the angles, the law of sines gives the other sides: a side divided by the sine of the opposite angle is the same number all the way around. A network is many triangles that share sides. Measure one base carefully, observe the angles, and the shared sides carry the scale across a country. A bad angle early in the chain is inherited by everything beyond it. That is why the surveys close triangles and measure more bases than pride requires.

Three historical uses sit in order, and they must not be melted together.

Willebrord Snell, in 1615, triangulated an arc in the Netherlands and published *Eratosthenes Batavus*. The [Snell lesson](/topics/81-willebrord-snell/) keeps his life and the separate optical law. The survey is the ancestor, on this site, of the later European chains.

Jean Picard, in 1669 and 1670, chained triangles from Malvoisine to Sourdon and checked the latitude at Amiens. One degree came out 57,060 toises. The plate and the arithmetic are the [Picard lesson](/topics/73-picard-triangulation-1669/). The 1671 preparatory drawing is that network. It does not include Orléans, and it is not a map of the French coast.

Giovanni Domenico Cassini and Philippe de La Hire then used two different extensions. Eclipses of Jupiter's moons, predicted for Paris, gave longitudes of ports and shifted the coast on the map published in 1693. In 1683 a meridian survey started south with Cassini and north with La Hire, to carry a line through the kingdom. Those are told on the [Cassini lesson](/topics/79-cassini-paris-and-jupiter/). A coastline from satellite eclipses is not a chain of roadside triangles, even when the same academy paid for both.

The eighteenth century kept going. Delambre and Méchain, from 1792 to 1799, triangulated from Dunkirk to Barcelona so that a quarter of a meridian could define a metre. That campaign is the [metric lesson](/topics/83-the-metre-from-a-meridian/). It inherits the method. It does not inherit Picard's 57,060 as the final length.

A classroom triangle, not a historical station: base 8 kilometres, angles 45 degrees, 60 degrees, and therefore 75 degrees. The side opposite 45 degrees is 8 times the sine of 45 degrees, divided by the sine of 75 degrees. The sine of 45 degrees is about 0.7071. The sine of 75 degrees is about 0.9659. So the side is 8 times 0.7071, divided by 0.9659, about 5.86 kilometres. The figure is for the rule. It is not a side from Snell's book or Picard's.

No presentation deck is part of this lesson. The triangles are the lesson.

## Key facts
- One measured base and the angles of a triangle fix the other sides by the law of sines. A network shares those sides.
- Snell 1615, Picard 1669-1670, and the Cassini-La Hire extensions are three campaigns. The 1671 plate, the 1693 coast, and the metre survey are three products.
- An early angle error travels to every later triangle. Extra bases are how a survey argues with itself.
