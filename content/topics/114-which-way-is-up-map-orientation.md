A globe has no "up." The page does. Which way a mapmaker points the top of the sheet is a choice, and for a long time people made different ones. North at the top feels inevitable only because we grew up with it.

![Four compass roses. The rust point marks the direction placed at the top of the map: east on medieval European mappae mundi, south on many Islamic world maps, and north on the Yu Ji Tu of 1136 and on printed atlases after Ptolemy.](../../img/Map_Orientation_Four_Ways_East_South_North_Tufte.svg)
*Drawn for this lesson. The rust point is "up" on the page. The letters around each rose are the four directions once that choice is made.*

## East, Toward Paradise

Many medieval European world maps, the *mappae mundi*, put **east at the top**. The Hereford map and the Ebstorf map do this, as do the simple T-O diagrams that lie behind them. East was where the Garden of Eden, or Paradise, was thought to lie. Jerusalem often sat at the center. These were maps of a story as much as of a coastline.

The word **orient** is the Latin *oriens*, "the rising," that is, the east, where the sun comes up. To *orient* a building or a map was, originally, to turn it toward that east. Churches were oriented that way. So were these maps. The English words *orient* and *orientation* keep that eastward root. They do not come from the later habit of putting north at the top.

Not every medieval European map followed the rule. From the thirteenth century, Mediterranean portolan charts, drawn for the magnetic compass, generally had north at the top, sometimes tilted a little by magnetic declination. A few world maps, including Fra Mauro's of about 1450, put south at the top.

## South, on Many Islamic Maps

In 1154 the geographer Muhammad al-Idrisi finished a great atlas in Palermo for Roger II of Sicily, later known as the Tabula Rogeriana. Its world is drawn with **south at the top**. That was a common convention for medieval Islamic world maps, not a quirk of one book.

It is often said that the maps point south because they point toward Mecca. The habit is real; that explanation is probably too neat. Mecca is not south of every place in the Islamic world, and scholars of these maps treat the south-up layout as a convention, not a single religious rule.

## North in China, but Not Only North

China had north-up maps early. The *Yu Ji Tu*, "Map of the Tracks of Yu," was carved in stone in **1136**. A rubbing is in the Forest of Stone Steles in Xi'an. It has north at the top, a regular grid, and coasts and rivers that still look familiar. Joseph Needham called it the most outstanding map of its time.

That is not the whole Chinese story. Other maps put south or east at the top. The emperor traditionally faced south, and later writers sometimes tied a map's "up" to whether it was hung on a wall or laid on a table. North-up is old and well attested in China. It was not the only rule for centuries.

## How North-Up Won in Print

[Ptolemy's *Geography*](../104-hipparchus-ptolemy-and-the-grid/), in the second century, already put north at the top. He was drawing the world from a globe, and he lived in the northern hemisphere. When that book was printed with maps in the late 1400s, north-up traveled with the plates. Atlas makers in the next century, Ortelius among them, copied Ptolemy and copied one another.

The compass helped the same habit along at sea. Portolan charts had already made north a practical top for sailors. Printing, the compass, and a widely copied Ptolemaic model together made one choice look like the only one. It isn't. It is just the choice that stuck.

## Key facts
- A map's "up" is a convention. The globe has none.
- Many medieval European *mappae mundi* put east at the top, toward Paradise; Jerusalem often sat at the center.
- *Orient* is Latin *oriens*, "the rising," the east. To orient something was originally to turn it eastward. The words *orient* and *orientation* keep that root.
- Portolan charts, from the thirteenth century, were generally north-up, for the magnetic compass. Fra Mauro's world map, about 1450, is south-up.
- Al-Idrisi's 1154 atlas for Roger II of Sicily, the Tabula Rogeriana, puts south at the top, a common convention on medieval Islamic world maps, not clearly a rule about facing Mecca.
- The *Yu Ji Tu* of 1136 is north-up, with a grid, and is among the finest surviving Chinese maps. Other Chinese maps used south or east at the top; the tradition was mixed.
- Ptolemy's *Geography* was already north-up. Printed editions in the late 1400s, and the atlases that copied them, made north-up the standard European, then global, habit.
