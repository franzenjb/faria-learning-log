This page was written from the historical account of the French national metrology laboratory. It is a reconstructed lesson, not a transcript.

Until the eighteenth century France had hundreds of local units. The French metrology laboratory's history counts more than seven hundred in 1795. A foot in one town was not a foot in the next. Area units and length units did not convert by a single rule. Trade and science both paid for the confusion.

On 16 February 1791 a commission of Borda, Condorcet, Laplace, Lagrange, and Monge was asked for one system. Three natural lengths were on the table: a pendulum that beats seconds at latitude 45 degrees, a quarter of the equator, and the distance from the North Pole to the equator, a quarter of a meridian. The seconds pendulum mixes length with time and with the local pull of gravity, so it changes from place to place. The quarter meridian was chosen.

On 26 March 1791 the metre was defined as one ten-millionth of that quarter meridian. A full meridian circle would then be 40,000,000 metres, because four quarters make the circle, and each quarter is 10,000,000 metres. Later geographical speech sometimes calls a meridian a semicircle. The 1791 definition used the astronomer's full circle. The laboratory's note says so. Do not "correct" the 10,000,000 into 20,000,000.

The length still had to be measured. Jean-Baptiste Delambre and Pierre Méchain triangulated from Dunkirk to Barcelona. The laboratory says the work lasted almost seven years. The dates usually given for the campaign are 1792 to 1799. They used the same idea as [Picard's degree](/topics/73-picard-triangulation-1669/): a measured base, triangles, latitudes. Picard's 57,060 toises is an earlier result for one degree near Paris. It is not the number Delambre and Méchain were sent to replace with a definition. The [network lesson](/topics/82-geodetic-networks/) is the method. This page is the unit.

The law of 7 April 1795, 18 germinal year III, set the decimal system: metre, square metre, cubic metre, litre, gram. The kilogram was the mass of a cubic decimetre of water at a stated temperature. Moving between units is then a shift of the decimal point, two places for area and three for volume, instead of a thicket of local ratios.

In 1799 standards of the metre and the kilogram were deposited in the Archives of the Republic. Those bars and weights are objects. The definition was the meridian. A bar can be scratched, copied badly, or lost. The idea was a length any nation could, in principle, remeasure.

Adoption was slow. The laboratory records the system required in the Netherlands from 1816, chosen by Spain in 1849, and made exclusive in France by the law of 4 July 1837. The Metre Convention of 20 May 1875 created the International Bureau of Weights and Measures. Seventeen states signed. The International System of 1960 is the successor. The definitions of the base units have been rewritten since, for the sake of precision. The 1791 sentence is the historical seed, not the current laboratory definition.

## Key facts
- More than seven hundred French units were still in use in 1795, by the laboratory's count.
- The metre was defined on 26 March 1791 as one ten-millionth of the quarter meridian from pole to equator. A pendulum beating seconds was rejected because it depends on gravity.
- Delambre and Méchain triangulated from Dunkirk to Barcelona. The campaign is the one that lasted nearly seven years, commonly dated 1792 to 1799.
- Archive standards were deposited in 1799. The Metre Convention is 20 May 1875. Picard's 57,060 toises is an earlier degree, not this definition.
