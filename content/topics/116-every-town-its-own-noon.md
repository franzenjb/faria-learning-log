One fact does almost all the work. Earth turns 360° in 24 hours, so **one hour of time is 15° of longitude**. Everything else in this lesson — the noon that walks west across the United States, the railroad time zones, the fight over Greenwich — is that fact, lived out on land and at sea.

## One Hour, 15 Degrees

The arithmetic is a set of paired scales, the same ones as in [time to longitude](../70-time-to-longitude/):

- 1 hour = 15°
- 4 minutes = 1°
- 1 minute = 15′
- 4 seconds = 1′
- 1 second = 15″

At the equator, 1′ of longitude is about 1 nautical mile. The distance shrinks as the cosine of latitude. At Greenwich, about 51.5° N, a minute of longitude is only about 62 percent of that, a little over half a nautical mile. [Harrison's](../84-harrison-and-the-marine-chronometer/) H4 was about 5 seconds off on its first sea trial. Five seconds is 75″, or 1.25′ of longitude: about a mile and a quarter at the equator, and less as you go north or south.

## Noon Walks West

Fix a moment: solar noon in New York, 40.71° N, 74.01° W, when the Sun stands highest over that meridian. Noon is already finished everywhere to the east, and it has not yet arrived anywhere to the west. It walks west at a steady rate, 15° in an hour.

*Every meridian has its own noon. The rust times are the rounded talking points; the exact solar times sit under them.*

How fast does that line move? At New York's latitude, cos(40.71°) is about 0.758. A degree of longitude is then about 69.2 × 0.758 ≈ 52.4 miles. Noon covers one degree every 4 minutes, so it moves about **13 miles a minute**, or about **790 mph**. Philadelphia's noon is about 5 minutes after New York's. Pittsburgh's is about 24. Memphis, at +64 minutes, sits almost on 90° W.

The 15° steps are only convenient checkpoints. Between New York and Memphis there are endless noons, one for every step west. Even the east and west ends of Manhattan have noons a few seconds apart.

## Carry Home's Time With You

On land, you can watch that line arrive. At sea you cannot, not if the sky is overcast and the last good sight is days behind you. Local noon, when you can see it, tells you the time *here*. It does not tell you how far you are from home. For that you also need home's clock. The difference, in hours, times 15, is your longitude. That is the whole problem that sank Shovell's fleet at the [Scilly Isles in 1707](../102-scilly-1707/) and that the [Longitude Act of 1714](../103-the-longitude-act-of-1714/) tried to buy a solution for.

One early answer was a clock in the sky: the eclipses of [Jupiter's moon Io](../96-jupiters-moons-as-a-measuring-stick/). Everyone on Earth sees the same eclipse at the same instant, so if you compare the local time you see it with the time predicted for Paris or Greenwich, you have your longitude. Cassini and Picard used it to redraw France. [Rømer](../65-romer-io-speed-of-light/), timing those same eclipses, found they ran late when Earth was far from Jupiter — the first good evidence that light has a speed. Io only failed at sea, because you cannot hold a telescope steady on a rolling deck. The clock had to come aboard. That clock was Harrison's H4.

## Every Town Its Own Noon, Until the Railroads

If every meridian has its own noon, then every town did too. For most of history that was fine. A railroad timetable is not.

Britain got there first, but with one time for the whole country rather than zones. The Great Western Railway adopted London time in 1840, and by 1847 most British railways ran on "railway time," which was Greenwich time. By 1855 most public clocks in Britain matched it. It did not become law until 1880.

The first true national standard time was New Zealand's. In 1868 the colony set one time, 11½ hours ahead of Greenwich.

In the United States the railroads ran on something like 50 to 100 different local times. Charles F. Dowd (1825–1904), who ran a girls' school, Temple Grove Seminary in Saratoga Springs, New York, was not a scientist or a railroad man. Starting around 1870 he proposed four national time zones and lobbied railroad officials for years. His first plan was measured from Washington's meridian, not Greenwich; he switched to Greenwich later in the 1870s. The railroads borrowed the idea, but the plan that was adopted is remembered as William F. Allen's. Dowd was killed by a train at a crossing in Saratoga Springs in 1904, a train running on the time zones he had helped create.

On 18 November 1883, the "Day of Two Noons," the railroads switched the country to four zones on their own authority, each centered on a meridian 15° from Greenwich, so each clock is a whole number of hours off Greenwich time:

- **Eastern:** 75° W, 5 hours behind Greenwich, a line that runs near Philadelphia
- **Central:** 90° W, 6 hours behind, close to Memphis and New Orleans
- **Mountain:** 105° W, 7 hours behind, close to Denver
- **Pacific:** 120° W, 8 hours behind, close to Fresno

Look back at the noon strip: Memphis, Denver, and Fresno sit almost on those center lines. In theory each zone runs 7.5° on either side of its meridian. In practice the lines were bent onto state and county borders for convenience, which is why they wiggle. A few places in the world, such as India, Newfoundland, and parts of Australia, even use half-hour offsets.

People fought it. Some cities refused "railroad time" for years. Preachers called it tampering with God's time. Detroit went back and forth on its clocks into the 1900s. It was not federal law until the Standard Time Act of 1918.

If every town rang noon when the Sun was highest, what happens to a train timetable that crosses three of those noons in an afternoon?

## Before Greenwich, Many Zeros

Before 1884 there was no single zero of longitude. Each country measured from its own capital or observatory, so the same city had different longitudes depending on whose map you used.

- **Greenwich**, already used on most sea charts: about 72 percent of the world's shipping tonnage relied on British charts.
- **Paris**, from the Paris Observatory, about 2°20′ east of Greenwich. On French maps, Memphis was about 92.4° W.
- **Washington**, from the U.S. Naval Observatory, about 77°03′ W of Greenwich. On many American maps, **Denver was about 28° west of Washington**.
- Others included Cádiz and Madrid, Pulkovo for Russia, Ferro in the Canary Islands for older European maps, Rome, Lisbon, and Philadelphia.

The U.S. railroads, in 1883, already described their meridians in Greenwich terms. That is part of why Greenwich won the vote at the [International Meridian Conference](../106-the-1884-meridian-conference/) in Washington in 1884. France abstained and kept Paris time until 1911. (A fuller account of why Greenwich, and not the City of London, is on [Why Greenwich, Not London](../111-why-greenwich-not-london/).)

The railroads adopted Greenwich-based zones a year before the world voted. How do you think that weighed on the delegates in Washington?

## Fleming's World of 24 Zones, Adopted Slowly

Dowd was solving America's railroad problem. The global case was made by Sandford Fleming, a Canadian railway engineer, from 1879: 24 zones for the whole world, tied to a single prime meridian.

The 1884 conference adopted Greenwich as the prime meridian and agreed on a universal day counted from Greenwich. It did **not** adopt Fleming's 24 time zones. Countries were left to set their own local time.

The world joined one country at a time, over about 50 years. Japan joined in 1888, much of Europe in the 1890s, France in 1911, and the Netherlands in 1940. Some places still do not follow the scheme neatly: India is 5½ hours ahead of Greenwich, Nepal 5¾, and all of China runs on one time zone even though the country spans about five. Trains, telegraphs, and radio did more of the convincing than any one speech did.

China keeps one clock for a country that spans about five hour-zones. In the far west, what does "noon" on the clock have to do with the Sun?

## Key facts

- Earth turns 360° in 24 hours: 1 hour = 15°, 4 minutes = 1°, 1 minute = 15′, 4 seconds = 1′, 1 second = 15″.
- At the equator, 1′ of longitude is about 1 nautical mile; at Greenwich's latitude that distance is about 62 percent as long.
- H4's 5-second error is 75″, or 1.25′, of longitude.
- Solar noon walks west from New York (74.01° W) to Philadelphia in about 5 minutes, Pittsburgh in 24, Nashville in 51, Memphis in 64, Denver in about 2 hours, and Los Angeles or Fresno in about 3 hours.
- At New York's latitude, noon moves about 13 miles a minute, or about 790 mph.
- When it is noon in New York, it is about 1 p.m. in Barbados, 2 p.m. in Rio, 3 p.m. in the Azores, 5 p.m. in London, 6 p.m. in Rome and Berlin, and 7 p.m. in Istanbul.

- Charles F. Dowd proposed U.S. time zones around 1870, first from Washington's meridian, later from Greenwich. William F. Allen's plan was adopted.
- On 18 November 1883, the "Day of Two Noons," U.S. railroads created four zones centered on 75°, 90°, 105°, and 120° W. Federal law followed in 1918.
- Before 1884, maps used many zeros; on many U.S. maps Denver was about 28° west of Washington. Greenwich charts already served about 72 percent of shipping tonnage.
