## The poem
> Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
> Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
> Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
> And summer's lease hath all too short a date;
> Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines,
> And often is his gold complexion dimm'd;
> And every fair from fair sometime declines,
> By chance or nature's changing course untrimm'd;
> But thy eternal summer shall not fade,
> Nor lose possession of that fair thou ow'st;
> Nor shall Death brag thou wander'st in his shade,
> When in eternal lines to time thou grow'st:
> So long as men can breathe or eyes can see,
> So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.

## The shape
A Shakespearean sonnet is 14 lines: three groups of four (quatrains) and a rhyming couplet. The first eight lines show how summer fails: too windy, too short, too hot, too cloudy, and every beautiful thing fades. Line 9 turns with "**But**": your summer won't fade.

The real **turn** comes in the final couplet. Why won't the beloved fade? Because of "**this**," **the poem itself**. As long as people read it, it keeps the beloved alive. The poem claims to beat Time, and four centuries later it is still being read, so it was right. (Compare [Ozymandias](../36-ozymandias/), where only words survive too.)

## Summer's lease: a legal metaphor
"**And summer's lease hath all too short a date**" is property law.
- Summer is a **tenant** renting the year.
- **Date** means the **end date of the lease**, when it expires.
- **Time** is the **landlord**, who always takes the property back.

"**Nor lose possession of that fair thou ow'st**": *ow'st* means **ownest**, "the beauty you own." "Possession" is a legal term too. The poem gives the beloved an **eternal lease**: possession that Time can't end.

## Shakespeare the lawyer?
Legal language runs all through the sonnets. **Sonnet 87** ("Farewell! thou art too dear for my possessing") is built on contracts: "The **charter** of thy worth gives thee releasing; / My **bonds** in thee are all determinate." Because of this, some scholars have argued Shakespeare worked as a lawyer's clerk in his "lost years" before London. There's no record to prove it; legal talk was common among Elizabethans, who sued each other constantly.

## Key facts
- Sonnet 18's final couplet turns on "this," the poem itself, which gives the beloved life
- "Summer's lease hath all too short a date": summer the tenant, date the lease's end, Time the landlord
- "Thou ow'st" means "you own"
- Sonnet 87 uses charter and bonds; the lawyer theory is unproven
