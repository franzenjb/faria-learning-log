## 1. Not one star: a multiple system

To the naked eye Polaris is a single point of light. It is not.

**α Ursae Minoris** (Alpha Ursae Minoris, abbreviated **α UMi**) means "the brightest star of Ursa Minor," the Little Bear / Little Dipper. Greek letters were assigned by **Johann Bayer** in 1603: α = brightest, β = next, and so on. "Ursae Minoris" is the Latin genitive, "of the Lesser Bear."

What you see as Polaris is really a **triple system** (two more distant stars once thought to belong have been shown **not** to be bound to it):

| Component | What it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Polaris Aa** | Yellow **supergiant**, spectral type **F7Ib** | The star you see. Mass about **5.13 ± 0.28** solar masses (Evans et al. 2024, CHARA + Hubble + Gaia); radius about **46** solar radii; thousands of times the Sun's luminosity. |
| **Polaris Ab** | Close dwarf, roughly **F6V** | Orbits Aa in about **29.4 years**. Angular separation only ~0.17″; at the Gaia distance the closest approach is about **6 AU**. Hubble first imaged it in **January 2006**. A's glare had hidden it for centuries. |
| **Polaris B** | Main-sequence dwarf, spectral type **F3V** | Much farther out, about **2,400 AU** from A. **William Herschel** discovered it in **August 1779** with a reflecting telescope he built himself. Visible in a modest amateur telescope. |

**Spectral classes F7 and F3.** The letters O B A F G K M run from hottest to coolest. **F** stars are yellow-white, a little hotter than our G-type Sun. The number (0–9) is a finer grade: F3 is hotter than F7. The suffix **Ib** means a luminous supergiant; **V** means a normal main-sequence ("dwarf") star like the Sun.

**Polaris C and D** were once listed as distant companions. Modern proper-motion and distance work shows they are **not physically associated** with the Polaris system: background (or foreground) stars that happen to lie near it on the sky.

## 2. Why it stands still

Earth spins on an axis. Extend that axis into the sky and you hit the **north celestial pole**. Polaris sits almost on that point, so as Earth turns, every northern star wheels around Polaris while Polaris itself barely moves.

**How close?** In 2018 it was about **0.66°** (about **40 arcminutes**) from the true pole: roughly **1.4 times the width of the full Moon**. It therefore traces a tiny daily circle about **1.3°** across. Navigators have always corrected for that.

**Finding true north with the Big Dipper.** The two stars at the "bowl" end of the Big Dipper, **Dubhe** and **Merak** (the "pointers"), point toward Polaris. Because Polaris itself is a little off the pole, its azimuth is true north only twice each sidereal day; the rest of the time you correct east or west. A classic rule of thumb treats the leading edge of the Dipper like a **clock face** and reads the correction from Dubhe–Merak. Nathaniel **Bowditch's *American Practical Navigator*** (first edition **1802**) lists Polaris among the navigational stars and has been teaching American sailors this geometry for more than two centuries — full story in [Bowditch](/topics/49-bowditch/); why the daily correction exists in [The sidereal day](/topics/50-sidereal-day/).

**Altitude = latitude.** The height of Polaris above your northern horizon **is** your latitude (to within that ~0.7° offset, which tables correct). At the North Pole it is overhead; at the equator it sits on the horizon; in Florida (~28°N) it stands about 28° up. That is the GIS / navigation fact already on [Looking Up](/topics/45-looking-up-polaris-planets-ptolemy-copernicus/) and the reason latitude was easy for sailors while longitude was hard (see [Galileo](/topics/46-galileo/)).

## 3. It will not always be the pole star: precession

Earth's axis does not point forever at the same spot. It **wobbles**, like a slowing top, tracing a circle on the sky once every roughly **26,000 years**. This is the **precession of the equinoxes**. The north celestial pole therefore drifts among the stars, and different stars take turns as "the" North Star.

![Precession circle of pole stars](../../img/Polaris_Precession_Circle_Pole_Stars_Tufte.png?v=1)
*The north celestial pole traces a ~26,000-year circle among the stars. Diagram drawn for this site.*

Key stops on that circle:

| When | Pole star (or none) |
|---|---|
| ~**2750 BC** | **Thuban** (α Draconis), in Draco; near the pole when the pyramids were rising |
| Classical antiquity | Closer to **Kochab** (β UMi) than to Polaris, though still ~10° from either |
| ~**320 BC** | The Greek navigator **Pytheas** described the pole as **devoid of stars** |
| 5th century AD | **Stobaeus** called Polaris ἀεί φανής (*aei phanēs*), **"always visible"** |
| ~**2100 AD** | Polaris's closest approach (~0.45° / 27′) |
| ~**4000 AD** | **Gamma Cephei** (Errai) |
| ~**10,000 AD** | Near **Deneb** |
| ~**14,500 AD** | Near **Vega** |
| +26,000 years | Back to Polaris |

Shakespeare's Caesar boasts of being "as constant as the northern star" (*Julius Caesar*, ~1599). In Caesar's own century there was **no** bright star sitting on the pole; Polaris was still several degrees off. The line is poetry, not astronomy.

## 4. Cepheids: a pulsing yardstick

**Pronounce it SEF-ee-id.**

A **Cepheid** is a star that **pulses**: it swells and shrinks on a steady beat, brightening and dimming like a slow heartbeat. The class is named for **Delta Cephei**, the prototype studied in the late 18th and 19th centuries.

**Henrietta Swan Leavitt** (1868–1921), working as a "computer" at Harvard College Observatory (full story: [The Harvard Computers](/topics/51-harvard-computers/)), measured variable stars on photographic plates of the Magellanic Clouds. In **1912** she published the result that made cosmology possible: a Cepheid's **period tells you its true (intrinsic) brightness**. The slower the pulse, the more luminous the star. Compare that true brightness with how bright the star *looks*, and you know how far away it is. Cepheids became **standard candles**.

**Edwin Hubble** used Leavitt's period–luminosity law on Cepheids in the Andromeda Nebula (the 1920s) to show that Andromeda is a separate galaxy, outside the Milky Way, and that the universe is expanding.

![Henrietta Swan Leavitt](../../img/Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt.jpg?v=1)
*Henrietta Swan Leavitt (1868–1921). Public domain, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt.jpg).*

**Polaris is the nearest classical Cepheid.** Its period is about **3.97 days** (roughly four days). Two odd facts make astronomers stare:

- **The amplitude is shrinking (and misbehaving).** Before 1963 the brightness swing was over 0.1 magnitude and slowly decreasing; after **1966** it dropped rapidly below 0.05 magnitude, then wandered. Reports since then suggest the amplitude may be growing again, a reversal not seen in other Cepheids.
- **The period has been lengthening** by around **4.5 seconds per year** for much of the modern record (with a pause around 1963–65). Recent work (Torres 2023) finds the period change has **reversed** and been decreasing since roughly 2010.
- **It may be brightening over centuries.** Research reported in *Science* (2004) suggests Polaris is about **2.5 times brighter** now than when **Ptolemy** recorded it ~1,900 years ago (third magnitude then, second now). Astronomer **Edward Guinan**: if real, "these changes are **100 times larger** than [those] predicted by current theories of stellar evolution."

Polaris is overluminous compared with the best Cepheid evolution models, and its age is hard to reconcile with its companions. It remains a puzzle even while it is a yardstick.

## 5. How far away? Parallax, and why it matters

**Parallax, the finger-blink analogy.** Hold a finger at arm's length and blink each eye in turn: the finger jumps against the background. The closer the finger, the bigger the jump. Astronomers do the same with Earth's orbit. Look at a star in January, then again in July (Earth has moved ~2 AU), and measure how far the star shifts against distant background stars. That angle is the **stellar parallax**. One **parsec** is the distance at which the parallax is one arcsecond; a light-year is about 0.3066 parsecs.

**The fight over Polaris's distance:**

| Method | Distance | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Hipparcos** satellite (observations 1989–93; revised reduction 2007) | about **432** light-years (133 pc) | Direct trigonometric parallax of the primary |
| **Turner et al.** (spectral / photometric estimates, 2004–2013) | about **323** light-years (≈99 pc) | "Short" distance from spectroscopy and main-sequence fitting of B |
| **Gaia** (DR3, via companion B) | about **446.5** light-years (136.9 pc) | Current best geometric estimate for the system, assuming A and B share a distance |

**Why binaries spoil the measurement.** Polaris Ab tugs Aa around a 29-year orbit. That orbital wobble mixes with the tiny parallax wobble and with the Cepheid pulsation itself. Hipparcos had to disentangle all three. Close companions and variability are exactly what make bright, famous stars *harder* to measure, not easier.

**Why it matters: the cosmic distance ladder.** Cepheids are the rung that reaches from our neighborhood out to nearby galaxies. Every rung above them (Type Ia supernovae, Hubble's constant, the age and expansion rate of the universe) rests on how well we know Cepheid luminosities. Polaris is the **nearest** classical Cepheid, so an error in its distance calibrates an error into everything farther out. That is why grown astronomers argue in public about 323 vs 434 vs 446 light-years. Polaris is a prized check because it is the nearest classical Cepheid. It is a difficult one (a companion, a small amplitude), so the modern zero point uses many Galactic Cepheids, not this star alone. How the rungs under that yardstick were built, from Kepler's relative orbits through the Venus transits and stellar parallax to the calibration Leavitt's slope still needed, is [From a Venus transit to the Cepheids](/topics/52-venus-transit-to-cepheid-distances/).

## 6. Names: a word-lover's cabinet

Jeff loves words. Polaris has collected them for millennia.

- **Cynosure.** From Greek **κυνόσουρα** (*kynosoura*), **"dog's tail."** The Little Bear was once pictured as a dog; Polaris is the tip of its tail. Because everyone stared at it, *cynosure* came to mean **"the center of attention"** ("she was the cynosure of all eyes"). Pronounce **SY-nuh-shoor** (or SY-nuh-syoor).
- **Lodestar.** Middle English: the **guiding star**. Related to *lode* (a way or course) and to **lodestone**, the magnetic rock that also points the way.
- **Spenser's "steadfast star."** In *The Faerie Queene* I.ii.1: "By this the northern wagoner had set / His sevenfold team behind the **steadfast star**." The "northern wagoner" is the Big Dipper; the steadfast star is Polaris.
- **Stella polaris / Polaris.** Neo-Latin **"polar star,"** in print by the Alfonsine Tables of **1492**; Gemma Frisius used *stella polaris* in 1547. The IAU made **Polaris** the official proper name of α UMi Aa in 2016.
- **Stella Maris**, **"star of the sea,"** a medieval Marian title (Bartholomaeus Anglicus, c. 1270s) transferred to the pole star; the hymn ***Ave Maris Stella*** ("Hail, Star of the Sea") carries the same image.
- **Sanskrit Dhruva (Dhruva tara).** The word ध्रुव means "fixed, constant, immovable." In the Puranas the devotee Dhruva is set in the sky as the pole star.
- **Arabic.** Pre-Islamic / medieval astronomy called it ***al-Jady*** ("the kid," a young goat). Star-name dictionaries also record ***al-quṭb al-shamālī*** (**"the northern axle / pole"**) and ***mismār*** (**"needle"**): the pin on which the sky seems to turn.
- **Old English.** *Scip-steorra*, **"ship-star."** And in the **Old English Rune Poem**, the T-rune **Tir**:

> **Tir biþ tacna sum, healdeð trywa wel**
> **wiþ æþelingas; a biþ on færylde**
> **ofer nihta genipu, næfre swiceþ.**

> "Tir is a certain sign (a guiding star); well does it keep faith with princes; it is ever on its course over the mists of night and never fails."

(Dickins and others read this as a circumpolar / guiding star; the original rune-name was likely the god **Tiw**, with *tir* "glory" a later or alternate reading.)

## 7. Shakespeare, Sonnet 116: the centerpiece

![Title page of Shakespeare's Sonnets, 1609](../../img/Shakespeare_Sonnets_1609_Title_Page.jpg?v=1)
*Title page of the 1609 Quarto of Shake-speares Sonnets, published by Thomas Thorpe. Public domain, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sonnets1609titlepage.jpg).*

### The full sonnet (public domain; 1609 Quarto, modernized spelling)

> Let me not to the marriage of true minds
> Admit impediments. Love is not love
> Which alters when it alteration finds,
> Or bends with the remover to remove:
> O, no! it is an ever-fixed mark
> That looks on tempests and is never shaken;
> It is the star to every wandering bark,
> Whose worth's unknown, although his height be taken.
> Love's not Time's fool, though rosy lips and cheeks
> Within his bending sickle's compass come;
> Love alters not with his brief hours and weeks,
> But bears it out even to the edge of doom.
> If this be error and upon me proved,
> I never writ, nor no man ever loved.

### Line-by-line close reading

**"Let me not to the marriage of true minds / Admit impediments."** Echoes the Book of Common Prayer marriage service ("if either of you know any impediment..."). Shakespeare refuses to allow any obstacle into a union of *minds*, not just of bodies or estates.

**"Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds, / Or bends with the remover to remove."** If it changes when circumstances change, or yields when someone tries to take it away, it was never love. The stacked *alter / alteration* and *remover / remove* are deliberate: love that mirrors change is only change wearing love's name.

**"O, no! it is an ever-fixed mark / That looks on tempests and is never shaken."** A **sea-mark**: a beacon, landmark, or seamark fixed on shore (or a shoal) by which sailors steer. Tempests howl; the mark does not move. "Ever-fixed" is the same steadfastness Spenser gave the pole star.

**"It is the star to every wandering bark, / Whose worth's unknown, although his height be taken."** This is the GIS line.

- **Bark** = a **ship** (poetic for any sailing vessel; from Old French *barque*). A "wandering bark" is a ship off on the open sea.
- **"The star"** = the pole star, Polaris: the one star every northern sailor steered by.
- **"His height be taken"** = a sailor **measuring the star's altitude** above the horizon with an **astrolabe**, **quadrant**, or **cross-staff** (later a sextant). That altitude **is the ship's latitude**. This is exactly the rule on your GIS Month talk and on [Looking Up](/topics/45-looking-up-polaris-planets-ptolemy-copernicus/): **altitude of Polaris = latitude**.
- **"Whose worth's unknown"** = even though you can *measure* the star (take its height), you cannot know its **true nature or value**. In 1609 nobody knew Polaris was a triple Cepheid 446 light-years away; Shakespeare means something deeper: the guide can be used without being understood. Love, likewise, can be relied on without being fathomed.

So the couplet says: love is to the lover what Polaris is to the sailor. You can take its height (measure it, depend on it for your position) and still not know its worth.

**"Love's not Time's fool, though rosy lips and cheeks / Within his bending sickle's compass come."** Time is a reaper with a curved sickle; beauty falls inside its sweep. Love is not Time's toy or victim.

**"Love alters not with his brief hours and weeks, / But bears it out even to the edge of doom."** *Doom* = Judgment Day. Love lasts to the end of the world.

**"If this be error and upon me proved, / I never writ, nor no man ever loved."** The poet stakes his entire life's work and the reality of human love on the claim. If he is wrong, no poem was ever written and no one ever loved: a deliberate impossibility, sealing the argument.

## 8. Coordinates: latitude and longitude for the sky

Polaris (J2000):

- **Declination:** **+89° 15′ 50.8″**
- **Right ascension:** **02h 31m 49.09s**

**Declination** is **latitude on the sky**: 0° at the celestial equator, +90° at the north celestial pole. Polaris at +89° 16′ is almost at the top of the sky's globe.

**Right ascension (RA)** is **longitude on the sky**, but measured in **hours, minutes, and seconds** (0h to 24h), not degrees. Why hours? Because the sky turns **360° in 24 hours = 15° per hour**, the same arithmetic as terrestrial longitude (see [Galileo](/topics/46-galileo/)). RA 2h 31m is about 37.8° east of the vernal equinox, the sky's Greenwich.

Because Polaris sits so near the pole, its right ascension changes rapidly as precession moves the pole: from about 2.5h in AD 2000 toward 6h by AD 2100.

## Key facts
- Polaris = α Ursae Minoris, a triple: Aa (F7Ib Cepheid ~5.13 M☉), Ab (close F6V, ~29.4 y orbit), B (F3V at ~2400 AU, found by Herschel 1779).
- Altitude of Polaris ≈ latitude; ~0.66° from the true pole (about 1.4 Moon-widths).
- Precession ~26,000 y: Thuban ~2750 BC; Pytheas (c. 320 BC) saw a pole with no star; Polaris closest ~2100; Vega ~14,500.
- Cepheid (SEF-ee-id): pulsating star; Leavitt 1912 period–luminosity law; Polaris period ~3.97 d.
- Distance debated: Hipparcos ~432 ly; Turner ~323 ly; Gaia (via B) ~446.5 ly; calibrates the cosmic distance ladder.
- Cynosure = Greek "dog's tail" → "center of attention"; lodestar; Stella Maris; Dhruva; OE Tir "never fails."
- Sonnet 116: love = "the star to every wandering bark, / Whose worth's unknown, although his height be taken" — bark = ship; height taken = measuring altitude for latitude.
- Sky coordinates: dec +89° 15′ 51″, RA 02h 31m 49s (RA in hours because 15° = 1 h).

## Answers
1. **Straight overhead** (at the zenith). That is latitude **90°N**.
2. That a Cepheid's **pulse period reveals its true brightness**. Measure period → know luminosity → compare with apparent brightness → get distance. Hubble found Cepheids in Andromeda and showed it lies far outside the Milky Way.
3. **Bark** = ship. **Taking the height** = measuring the star's altitude above the horizon (with astrolabe, quadrant, or cross-staff) to find the ship's **latitude**.
4. Because of **precession**: Earth's axis will then point near **Vega**, not Polaris.
5. Polaris is the **nearest classical Cepheid**. Cepheids calibrate distances to galaxies; those calibrate supernovae and Hubble's constant. An error in Polaris's distance feeds every rung above it on the cosmic distance ladder.
