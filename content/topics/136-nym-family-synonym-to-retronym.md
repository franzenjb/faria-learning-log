Once you hear *-nym* as "name," this family is easier than it looks. Each word is a kind of name, with a different front half. The sound-and-spelling set (*homonym*, *heteronym*, and the rest) is on [its own page](../135-homonym-homophone-homograph-heteronym/). This page is the other *-nym* words.

This is one of four lessons under [Confusing Word Terms](../133-confusing-word-terms/).

## Synonym

**A synonym is a word with the same, or nearly the same, meaning as another.**

Greek *syn* means "together," plus *onyma*: a together-name. *Big* and *large*. *Begin* and *start*. *Purchase* and *buy*. They are close, almost never identical. Register, shade, and habit pull them apart. You can *start* a car more naturally than you can *begin* one.

Mark Twain put the gap in one line, in a letter printed in 1890 in *The Art of Authorship*: "The difference between the almost right word and the right word is really a large matter — 'tis the difference between the lightning-bug and the lightning." Near-synonyms are the almost right word.

Say it: SIN-uh-nim. Memory hook: *syn*, together.

## Antonym

**An antonym is a word with the opposite meaning.**

Greek *anti* means "against": an against-name. *Hot* and *cold*. *In* and *out*. *Win* and *lose*. Some pairs are graded (*hot* / *cold*, with *warm* in between). Some are switches, with no middle (*dead* / *alive*). The English word *antonym* is a nineteenth-century coinage, built to sit beside the much older *synonym*.

Say it: AN-tuh-nim. Memory hook: *anti*, against.

## Contronym, or auto-antonym

**A contronym is a word that is its own opposite.**

It is also called an auto-antonym, and sometimes a Janus word, after the Roman god with two faces. *Cleave* can mean to split apart or to cling. *Sanction* can mean to permit or to punish. *Oversight* can mean careful watch, or a failure to notice. *Dust* can mean to remove dust or to sprinkle it on. *Fast* can mean firmly fixed, or moving quickly. *Let*, in older English, could mean to allow or to hinder; "without let or hindrance" still keeps the second sense.

Jack Herring coined *contronym* in 1962, in Merriam-Webster's magazine *Word Study*, on the model of *synonym* and *antonym*. *Auto-antonym* makes the same point from Greek *auto*, "self": a self-against-name.

Say it: KON-truh-nim, or auto-AN-tuh-nim.

## Eponym

**An eponym is a name taken from a person, or the person a thing is named after.**

Greek *epi* means "upon": a name put upon someone, or taken from someone. Both uses are current. Some people call the Earl of Sandwich the eponym of the sandwich; some call the sandwich an eponym. The root will bear either load.

The sandwich is named for John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich (1718–1792). The gambling-table story is traditional, and not firmly documented. The name itself is not in doubt. *Boycott* comes from Charles Boycott, a land agent in County Mayo, Ireland, shunned in 1880 during the Land League campaign. *Algorithm* comes, through medieval Latin *algorismus*, from the name of the ninth-century Persian mathematician Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi. *Silhouette* comes from Étienne de Silhouette, a French finance minister.

On this site, the literary hook is close to home. Alexandre Dumas drew the teacher in *The Count of Monte Cristo* from a real man, the Goan priest José Custódio de Faria. The character carries the man's name. That is an eponym at work. The lesson on [the real Abbé Faria](../44-the-real-abbe-faria/) is the place to go further, and [Monte Cristo and Napoleon](../14-monte-cristo-and-napoleon/) sits beside it.

Say it: EP-uh-nim. Memory hook: *epi*, upon, plus *onyma*.

## Toponym

**A toponym is a place-name, or a common word taken from a place.**

Greek *topos* means "place." *Paris*, *the Nile*, and *Cape Horn* are toponyms in the first sense. In the second sense, the word has left home and become a thing. *Denim* is from French *serge de Nîmes*, the twill of Nîmes. *Dollar* comes from the German *Joachimsthaler*, a sixteenth-century coin minted in Joachimsthal, now Jáchymov, in the Czech Republic; *thaler* became *daler*, then *dollar*. *Spa* is from Spa, the town in Belgium. *Champagne* is from the French region. *Marathon* is the Greek place; the modern race, first run at the 1896 Athens Olympics, is a new event inspired by the old story of a messenger's run.

Say it: TOP-uh-nim. Memory hook: *topos*, place.

## Demonym

**A demonym is the word for the people of a place.**

Greek *dēmos* means "people." A *Parisian* is from Paris, a *Floridian* from Florida, a *Liverpudlian* from Liverpool, an *Oxonian* from Oxford, a *Cantabrigian* from Cambridge. The endings vary, and they have to be learned. *The French*, *the Dutch*, and *New Zealanders* are demonyms too.

The older learned word is *gentilic*, from Latin *gentilis*. *Demonym* itself is recent. It appears in George H. Scheetz's *Names' Names* (1988). Paul Dickson later popularized it, and credited Scheetz.

Say it: DEM-uh-nim. Memory hook: *dēmos*, people.

## Retronym

**A retronym is a new name for the old thing, coined after a new kind appears and the bare word is no longer enough.**

*Guitar* was enough, until the electric guitar arrived. Then the old instrument needed a name of its own: *acoustic guitar*. *Clock* was enough, until digital clocks arrived, and we began to say *analog clock*. *Phone* was enough, until the mobile phone, and then we needed *landline*. *Camera* later needed *film camera*. What was once simply the Great War became *World War I* after there was a second.

Frank Mankiewicz coined *retronym* in 1980, from Latin *retro*, "backwards," plus *-onym*. William Safire introduced it in print in his "On Language" column in *The New York Times Magazine* on 27 July 1980, and credited Mankiewicz.

Say it: RET-ruh-nim. Memory hook: *retro*, back, a name sent backward to the old thing.

Wordplay and slips — portmanteau, malapropism, eggcorn, and the rest — are the [last of the four groups](../137-wordplay-and-slips/).

## Key facts

- *Syn* together, *anti* against, *auto* self, *epi* upon, *topos* place, *dēmos* people, *retro* backwards: each plus *onyma*, "name."
- Synonyms are nearly the same, not identical. Twain's lightning-bug is the almost right word.
- A contronym, or auto-antonym, is its own opposite: *cleave*, *sanction*, *oversight*. Jack Herring coined *contronym* in 1962.
- An eponym comes from a person (*sandwich*, *boycott*, *algorithm*). Dumas's Abbé Faria takes his name from a real priest.
- A toponym is a place-name, or a word taken from a place (*denim*, *dollar*, *spa*). A demonym names the people (*Parisian*, *Floridian*). *Demonym* appears in Scheetz, 1988.
- A retronym is a new name for the old thing (*acoustic guitar*, *landline*, *World War I*). Coined by Frank Mankiewicz in 1980.
