People call almost any string of capital letters an acronym. That is the loose habit. The useful split is smaller, and it starts one step earlier: not every shortening is made of initials at all.

This is one of four lessons under [Confusing Word Terms](../133-confusing-word-terms/).

## Abbreviation

**An abbreviation is any shortened form of a word or phrase.**

The root is Latin *abbreviare*, "to shorten," from *brevis*, "short," the same family as *brief*. *Dr.*, *Mr.*, *lb.*, *etc.*, *vs.*, and *St.* are abbreviations. So are *Dr.* for *Drive* on a street sign and *lb.* for *libra*, the Latin pound. Some are spoken as the full word ("doctor"); some as letters.

Say it: uh-BREE-vee-AY-shun.

## Contraction

**A contraction is a shortening that drops letters from the middle, usually marked by an apostrophe.**

*Don't*, *I'll*, *we've*, *o'clock* (*of the clock*), and *it's* are contractions. The apostrophe stands in for what was left out. *Can't* comes from *cannot*; *won't* is the odd one, from an older *woll not*, not from *will* in its modern form.

A contraction is a kind of abbreviation, but not every abbreviation is a contraction. *Dr.* does not put an apostrophe in the hole where *octo* used to be. *Intl.* is just cut short.

Say it: kun-TRAK-shun.

## Acronym

**An acronym, in the strict sense, is a shortening made from initial letters (or initial bits) and pronounced as a word.**

*NASA*, *NATO*, *radar* (radio detection and ranging), *scuba* (self-contained underwater breathing apparatus), and *laser* (light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation) are acronyms. You say them as words, not as a string of letters.

The word itself is a twentieth-century coinage from Greek *akron*, "tip" or "end," plus *onyma*, "name": a name made from the tips of other words. German had *Akronym* by 1921. English *acronym* is first found in 1940, in a translation of a Lion Feuchtwanger novel, where it is used for a string of initials. The narrower "pronounced as a word" sense is already in use by 1943. People made words from initials long before they had this name for them.

Some acronyms settle into ordinary nouns and lose their capitals: *radar*, *scuba*, *laser*. Many people no longer feel them as shortenings at all.

Say it: AK-ruh-nim. Memory hook: *akron*, the tip, plus *onyma*, the name.

:::callout
*Acronym* is also used loosely for any string of initials, including the ones you spell out. Dictionaries record that broader sense. Style books often prefer the narrow one. On this site, *acronym* means the kind you say as a word, and *initialism* means the kind you spell.
:::

## Initialism

**An initialism is a shortening made from initial letters and pronounced letter by letter.**

*FBI*, *BBC*, *IBM*, *HTML*, and *USA* are initialisms. You say "F-B-I," not "fibby." *Initialism* is recorded in this modern sense in 1957, from *initial* plus *-ism*. An older use, in 1868, meant a writer's initials on a publication.

The line can blur. *JPEG* is often "J-peg," one letter and then a word. *CD-ROM* mixes both habits. That is fine. The terms are tools, not a court.

Say it: ih-NISH-uh-liz-um.

## Backronym

**A backronym is a phrase invented to fit letters that already exist, or a name built so that the initials spell a chosen word.**

The word is a late-twentieth-century blend of *back* and *acronym*. Two honest kinds are worth keeping apart.

One is a slogan designed from the start so the initials make a word. The USA PATRIOT Act of 2001 was written to spell *USA PATRIOT*: "Uniting and Strengthening America by Providing Appropriate Tools Required to Intercept and Obstruct Terrorism." That is a deliberate backronym.

The other is a later expansion fitted to a name that was already there. The AMBER Alert is named for Amber Hagerman, a nine-year-old abducted in Arlington, Texas, in 1996. The letters were later read as "America's Missing: Broadcast Emergency Response." The girl's name came first. The phrase was written to fit it.

A false etymology is a third, worse kind: a story told as if it were the origin, when it is not.

## Myths, not origins

Acronyms in the modern sense are almost unknown before the twentieth century, and the word *acronym* itself is from the 1940s. Any claim that a much older word "stands for" a phrase in English is almost certainly a myth.

- **Posh** does not stand for "Port Out, Starboard Home." The story says P&O tickets to India were stamped *POSH* so wealthy passengers could have a shaded cabin, port side out and starboard side home. No such ticket has been found, and the company's records do not support the tale. The story is first recorded in the 1930s, after *posh* was already in use. The true origin is uncertain. It may come from older slang for a dandy, and before that from slang for money, perhaps from Romani *posh*, "half."
- **SOS** does not stand for "Save Our Ship" or "Save Our Souls." The International Radiotelegraphic Convention of 1906 adopted a Morse distress signal of three dots, three dashes, and three dots because it is easy to send and hard to miss. The letters *SOS* are just a way to read that pattern. They were not chosen as an abbreviation of an English phrase.
- **Golf** does not stand for "Gentlemen Only, Ladies Forbidden." *Golf* is a Scots word, recorded in the fifteenth century, related to Dutch *kolf*, "club." It is hundreds of years older than English acronyms.
- Two more that go the same way: **TIP** is not "To Insure Promptness," and **NEWS** is not "North East West South." *Tip* in the gratuity sense is older slang. *News* is simply the plural of *new*.

*Adidas* is sometimes said to mean "All Day I Dream About Sports." That is a later joke. The name is from the founder, Adi Dassler.

A memorable expansion can be fun. It is not the same thing as a history of the word. For the rest of this set, start at the [hub](../133-confusing-word-terms/), or go on to [sound and spelling](../135-homonym-homophone-homograph-heteronym/).

## Key facts

- An abbreviation is any shortened form. A contraction drops letters in the middle, usually with an apostrophe.
- In the strict sense, an acronym is pronounced as a word (*NASA*, *radar*); an initialism is read letter by letter (*FBI*, *HTML*).
- *Acronym* is from Greek *akron*, "tip," plus *onyma*, "name." The English word is first found in 1940; German *Akronym* is earlier (1921).
- A backronym is a phrase invented to fit existing letters, or a slogan built so the initials spell a word. The USA PATRIOT Act is a deliberate example. The AMBER Alert's expansion was fitted to a girl's name.
- *Posh*, *SOS*, and *golf* are not acronyms. Those letter-stories are myths.
