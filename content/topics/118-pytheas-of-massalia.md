**Pytheas** (*PITH-ee-us*) of **Massalia** (*ma-SAL-ee-uh*), today's Marseille, sailed north about **325 BC**. The date is the usual round one; some scholars prefer a few years later. On the [Where Am I?](/claude1/v3/?talk=30) talk he appears as a marker at Land's End. His book, *On the Ocean*, is lost. We know him from later writers, some of whom called him a liar. The voyage, and the one measurement we can still check, have worn rather well.

## A Greek City, and the Tin Trade

Massalia was a Greek colony, founded about **600 BC** by sailors from **Phocaea** (*fo-SEE-uh*) in Asia Minor, on the coast of what is now Turkey. Bronze needs tin as well as copper. Cornwall was a rich source, and it could be reached by sea. Tin also came from **Iberia** and, overland, from **Central Europe**. A commonly given motive for Massalia's interest in the Atlantic is a Carthaginian hold on the Strait of Gibraltar. That is a fair suspicion, not a documented order to Pytheas. Whether he himself slipped through the Strait or traveled overland through Gaul is debated.

## On the Ocean, Lost

*On the Ocean* survives only as fragments, quoted or paraphrased by later writers: [Strabo](../104-hipparchus-ptolemy-and-the-grid/), Pliny, Diodorus Siculus (often drawing on Timaeus), and Polybius. Strabo called Pytheas a liar, or at any rate a man not to be believed about the far north. Polybius scorned the idea that one man had walked the whole of Britain. Later geography, and the archaeology of the tin trade, have largely vindicated the core of the voyage. The book itself we still do not have.

## The Gnomon at Massalia

Before or after the voyage, Pytheas measured a noon shadow at Massalia on the summer solstice. The ratio he reported is precise and odd enough to be real: a gnomon of **120** to a shadow of **41 4/5**. That is a zenith distance of about **19.2°**, so the Sun stood about **70.8°** above the horizon. Add the Sun's solstice declination, about **23.7°** in his century, and the latitude comes out about **43°**. Marseille is about **43.3° N**. He reported a ratio, not a figure in degrees. Two centuries later [Hipparchus](../104-hipparchus-ptolemy-and-the-grid/) used the same ratio and compared it with a measurement at Byzantium.

Latitude from the Sun needs the noon altitude and the Sun's **declination** that day. You do not need a north star. [Polaris](../48-polaris/) is simpler, because its height is nearly your latitude and you can skip the declination table. In Pytheas's day, though, precession had not yet delivered a pole star; see below.

One claim that is **not attested** is that Pytheas built a table of noon Sun heights by latitude from his own yearly observations. What is attested is this Massalia gnomon ratio, and his reports of day lengths and of the Sun in the far north, which Hipparchus and Eratosthenes later used.

![Pytheas's solstice gnomon at Massalia: a ratio of 120 to 41 4/5, implying a latitude of about 43°. Marseille is 43.3° N.](../../img/Pytheas_Massalia_Gnomon_Ratio_Tufte.svg)
*The ratio is his. The arithmetic, and the 43.3°, are ours.*

## The Voyage

From the Channel he reached **Belerion**, Land's End in Cornwall, the country of the tin. Diodorus, probably following Timaeus and behind him Pytheas, says the metal was worked into ingots and carried to an island called **Ictis**, which could be reached on foot at low tide. St Michael's Mount is the identification most often offered. Mount Batten, near Plymouth, and the Isle of Wight have their advocates too. The identification is debated.

He is said to have sailed around Britain and to have given its perimeter as more than **40,000 stadia**. Diodorus has **42,500**, as a triangle around the island; Pliny gives a similar circuit in Roman miles. The length of a stadion is itself uncertain, so a modern mile figure is only a rough check. Polybius doubted that one man had walked the whole island. What Pytheas actually measured, and how, we cannot recover from the fragments.

Six days' sail north of Britain, he said, lay **Thule** (*THOO-lee*), near a frozen sea, a place of midnight sun, or of nights so short they barely counted. Iceland and Norway are the two leading identifications. Both are argued; neither is settled. Beyond, or about, that sea he described a place where earth, water, and air were no longer separate, a **sea lung**, on which one could neither walk nor sail. Many modern readers take this as pancake ice, the slushy skin of a freezing sea. The reading is debated.

He seems also to have reached a coast of **amber**. The exact itinerary on the North Sea or Baltic side is, again, debated.

Two firsts, among the Greeks, sit on firmer ground. He described the **midnight sun** and the very long summer days of high latitude, and he related the length of the day to how far north you stand. And he linked the **tides** to the Moon, a connection the nearly tideless Mediterranean had not forced on his predecessors. How long the polar day and night really last, latitude by latitude, is on [From Unequal Hours to Equal Ones](../119-from-unequal-hours-to-equal-ones/).

## The Empty Pole

[Hipparchus](../104-hipparchus-ptolemy-and-the-grid/), in his commentary on Aratus, says Eudoxus was wrong to claim that a star sits on the pole. At the pole, Hipparchus writes, there is no star at all, but an empty space, with three stars close by, with which the pole makes a sort of square, "as Pytheas of Massalia also states." The usual modern guess for those three stars is Kochab in the Little Dipper, and Thuban and Kappa in Draco. The identification is not certain, and the square is not a tidy one.

The report itself is the point. Because of [precession](../100-celestial-navigation-and-earth-geometry/), [Polaris](../48-polaris/) was then many degrees from the true pole. Pytheas looked up and found a blank.

## A Round Earth, and What Eratosthenes Did With Pytheas

Later tradition often gives **Pythagoras**, in the 500s BC, the spherical Earth, on aesthetic or mathematical grounds: the sphere was the perfect shape. That attribution is late, and it is debated. Theophrastus, a better and earlier witness, credited **Parmenides**. What Parmenides meant, a spherical universe or a spherical Earth, is itself not settled.

**Aristotle**, in *On the Heavens*, gave evidence. In a lunar eclipse the Earth's shadow on the Moon is always curved, so the Earth is a sphere. And the stars change as you travel north or south: some that are seen in Egypt are not seen farther north. The familiar argument that a ship disappears hull first, mast last, is commonly pinned on Aristotle. He did not make it. It appears later, in Pliny and in Ptolemy.

[Eratosthenes](../18-eratosthenes-measures-the-earth/), later head of the Library of Alexandria, knew Pytheas's work and used it. Strabo, who did not approve, still reports Eratosthenes measuring the breadth of the inhabited world as far as the parallel of Thule, "which Pytheas says is six days north of Britain." The same Eratosthenes, about 240 BC, compared a well at **Syene** (Aswan) that had no noon shadow on the solstice with a **7.2°** shadow at Alexandria, 1/50 of a circle, and turned a distance of about 5,000 stadia into a circumference of 250,000, later 252,000, stadia. He also drew the first map of the inhabited world known to have been organized on a grid of parallels and meridians. The map is lost. We meet it through Strabo.

## From the Noon Sun to Latitude

The Sun does not stay on the equator. Over the year its declination swings about **23.44°** north and the same amount south. That swing is the sky's expression of Earth's axial tilt, and it is why we have seasons.

To turn a noon Sun into a latitude, in the northern hemisphere when the Sun is south of the zenith:

**latitude = 90° − noon altitude + declination**

At the equinox, declination is zero, so latitude is just 90° minus the noon altitude. At the summer solstice you add the tilt; at the winter solstice you subtract it. The gnomon at Massalia is that formula, with the solstice declination put in. A north star is simpler, when you have one, because you can skip the table. How a sailor later took the height of the Sun or of Polaris is on [the sextant and latitude at sea](../87-sextant-and-latitude-at-sea/).

![Latitude equals 90° minus the noon altitude plus the Sun's declination, when the Sun is south of the zenith in the northern hemisphere.](../../img/Noon_Sun_Altitude_Declination_Latitude_Tufte.svg)
*The plus sign is for our usual case in the north, with the noon Sun in the south. Cross the equator, or catch the Sun on the other side of the zenith, and the signs need a second look.*

## The Moon's Two Months

The Moon has two months, and they are not the same length, for the same kind of reason a solar day is longer than a [sidereal day](../50-sidereal-day/). A trip around the sky against the stars, a **sidereal month**, takes **27.32 days**, so the Moon completes about **13.37** such orbits in a year. The cycle of the phases, a **synodic month** from new Moon to new Moon, takes **29.53 days**, so there are about **12.37** phase cycles in a year, not a neat 12. After the Moon has lined up with the same stars again, Earth has moved along its orbit, and the Moon still needs about **2.2 extra days** to catch the Sun and show the same phase.

Most years have twelve full Moons. A thirteenth comes around about every **2.7 years**. The interval is sometimes rounded to two and a half; the arithmetic, 1 divided by the leftover 0.37 of a cycle, is closer to 2.7.

That extra full Moon is the origin of the two "blue Moon" meanings. The older one, in the *Maine Farmers' Almanac*, is the **third of four** full Moons in a single season. The popular one, the **second full Moon in a calendar month**, comes from a 1946 *Sky & Telescope* article by James Hugh Pruett, who misread the Almanac. Both meanings are now in circulation. Neither makes the Moon look blue.

## Twelve Hours of Daylight

Greeks and Romans split daylight, sunrise to sunset, into twelve hours, and the night into twelve more. The hours stretched in summer and shrank in winter. At Rome a summer daylight hour was about 75 minutes and a winter one about 45; only at the equinox was an hour about 60 minutes. That older noon, when the Sun stood highest over your own town, is the starting point of [Before Time Zones: Every Town Its Own Noon](../117-before-time-zones-every-town-its-own-noon/).
