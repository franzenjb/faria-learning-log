Look closely at a lump of green bronze, about the size of a thick book, in a case in Athens. A four-spoked wheel is still there, with a ring of fine triangular teeth. Turn a handle on the box this came from, and the machine would have told you where the Sun and Moon would be, whether the Moon was full, and when an eclipse was due.

It is often called the world's first computer. That is a fair popular name for the most complex geared machine to survive from the ancient world. It is a mechanical analog calculator: bronze gears, turned by a handle, whose pointers keep time with the sky. Whether it was the first of its kind, we cannot say. Similar devices are described in writing from the same era. What we have here is the one that survived.

## A wreck, and a box of gears

In the spring of 1900, around Easter, sponge divers from the island of Symi found a wreck off Antikythera, in the channel between the Peloponnese and Crete. The Greek government organized a recovery in the winter of 1900–1901, with the divers and the Navy. Among the statues, glass, and coins were scraps of corroded bronze. At first no one quite saw what they were.

In May 1902, at the National Archaeological Museum in Athens, Spyridon Stais noticed gear teeth on two of those pieces. He had helped organize the excavation as minister of education. Many later accounts credit his cousin Valerios, the museum's director; the newspapers of the time name Spyridon. About 82 fragments survive, including about 30 gearwheels. Only about a third of the original machine is left. It is still in Athens.

The ship is usually dated to about 70–60 BC, mainly from a coin hoard: silver pieces issued at Pergamon and Ephesus, with issues running down to 67 BC, probably closed before about 60 BC. Some writers widen the window slightly, to about 80–50 BC. The mechanism itself may have been made some decades earlier, around the end of the second century BC.

## What the machine was for

The device sat in a wooden case, about the size of a large book. A handle on the side turned the large four-spoked wheel you can still see in Fragment A.

The back is the part we understand most securely. The upper spiral was a 19-year calendar — the Metonic cycle, in which 19 solar years hold 235 lunar months, so full moons fall on the same dates of the year again. Beside it, a small dial marked a four-year cycle of Greek games, the Olympics and the other Panhellenic festivals. The lower spiral predicted eclipses from the Saros cycle of 223 lunar months. Marks told the user when an eclipse of the Sun or the Moon was due. A still smaller dial tracked three of those cycles together, so the time of day of the eclipse would come back into line.

![A labeled diagram of the back face. The upper spiral is the 19-year Metonic calendar, with a small Games dial beside it. The lower spiral is the Saros eclipse dial, with a small Exeligmos dial beside it. Original diagram for this lesson, after the 2006 and 2008 reconstructions.](../../img/Antikythera_Mechanism_Back_Face_Diagram_Faria_2026-11-24.svg)

The front showed the sky: the Sun and Moon in the zodiac, and the Moon's phase. Inscriptions on the covers describe all five planets then known and say that they were shown on the front as a cosmos of little spheres. The gearing that would have driven those planets is mostly lost. A 2021 reconstruction by Tony Freeth and colleagues at University College London is a careful attempt to put that display back, not a photograph of a surviving face.

## Looking inside

In 2005 the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project brought a powerful X-ray scanner to the museum, along with a method of surface imaging that could tease letters out of corroded metal. The scans showed layers of tiny gears hidden inside the bronze. Freeth and colleagues reported the main results in *Nature* in November 2006. They roughly doubled the number of letters that could be read. Later work has brought the total of transcribed characters to about 3,000, out of perhaps 15,000 that were originally on the machine.

![An X-ray CT slice through a gear inside Fragment A. The triangular teeth and the square hole for the axle are clear, though the bronze itself is a corroded lump. From the 2005 scan data, as reprocessed by Pakzad and colleagues, *PLoS ONE*, 2018. CC BY 4.0.](../../img/Antikythera_Mechanism_Gear_b3_Xray_CT_Pakzad_2018.jpg)

The same scans revealed, still in the surviving gears, a pin-and-slot on slightly offset centers. The first gear turns steadily. A pin on its face rides in a slot on the second, which therefore speeds up and slows down once per turn. That uneven turn moves the Moon's pointer, matching the Moon's real, uneven pace — the motion Hipparchus had described in the second century BC. Unlike the planetary display, this part is not a reconstruction of missing pieces.

The 2006 and 2008 papers solved the back of the machine. The 2021 paper offered a compact model of the front: Earth and the Moon's phase at the center, then rings for Mercury, Venus, the Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, and the date. With so much missing, the authors do not claim a replica of the original. They argue that the surviving evidence leaves surprisingly few options that actually work.

Michael Wright, formerly of the Science Museum in London, had already built working metal models, including a planetary front, from the 1980s onward. They showed that a complete machine of this kind will actually run.

## Was it the first computer?

The popular name is useful, and a little too tidy. The machine does not store a program. It is a dedicated calculator in bronze. Turn the handle to a date, and the pointers show what the sky should be doing. In that sense it computes. It is also an analog machine: the gears themselves are the calculation.

Was it the first? We do not know. In the first century BC, Cicero described a sphere associated with Archimedes that showed the Sun, the Moon, and the five planets, and another, made by Posidonius of Rhodes, that produced the same motions as in the sky. Rhodes is sometimes suggested as a place where such devices were made. That is a hypothesis, not a proof. These fragments are not a claim of uniqueness. They are the one machine of this kind that we can still hold, scan, and, in replica, turn by hand.

**Question.** The back of the machine is read mainly from surviving gears and dials. The front planetary display is read mainly from inscriptions, plus a reconstruction of missing gearing. What should we treat as fact, and what as the best current model?

**Question.** If Cicero already knew of devices that showed the Sun, Moon, and planets, what exactly is special about the machine from Antikythera?

## The last word

Someone, more than two thousand years ago, found a way to put the sky into a box of gears and turn it with a handle. We are still finding out how much of the sky they managed to put in.
