Christiaan Huygens was a Dutch scientist who worked on light, Saturn's rings and the math of motion. In 1656 he made the most accurate clock the world had seen: the pendulum clock. He knew at once what that might mean for sailors. A clock that kept good time at sea would solve longitude. Huygens spent years trying. He did not succeed, and the reasons why are a lesson in physics.

## The Pendulum Clock, 1656

Before Huygens, the best clocks could drift by around a quarter of an hour a day. Galileo had noticed that a pendulum swings in nearly the same time whether the swing is big or small. Huygens turned that idea into a working clock. He made the first one in December 1656, in The Hague.

A clockmaker there, **Salomon Coster**, built the clocks. On 16 June 1657 the States General of the Dutch Republic granted a patent for the design. Huygens described it in a short book, *Horologium*, in 1658. The new clocks kept time to within about a minute a day or better. That was a huge jump, and pendulum clocks spread across Europe within a few years.

## Taking It to Sea

The Netherlands was a nation of ships, and longitude was worth a fortune. Huygens worked with **Alexander Bruce**, a Scottish nobleman and an early member of the Royal Society in London. Between about 1662 and 1665 they built pendulum clocks for ships. The clocks were hung so they could swing with the ship.

In 1663 two of these clocks went to London. An English naval officer, **Robert Holmes**, took them to sea, first toward Lisbon and then on a voyage to West Africa, which he made in 1663 and 1664.

## The 1664 Claims

Holmes came home early in 1665 with a striking story. On the way back from Africa, he said, his ship's masters thought by dead reckoning that they were about 100 leagues from the Cape Verde islands. The clocks said they were about 30 leagues away. Holmes trusted the clocks, held his course, and sighted the islands the next day.

The Royal Society printed an account in its brand-new journal, the *Philosophical Transactions*, in 1665. Huygens used the story in a Dutch booklet on how to find longitude with his clocks, and again in his great book *Horologium Oscillatorium* of 1673.

But there were doubts at the time, and there still are. Holmes had a reputation as a bold and difficult man. Some Royal Society fellows questioned whether his numbers could be trusted. Robert Hooke later said the excitement was not deserved. Modern historians generally think Holmes overstated how well the clocks did. One good landfall also proves little. It could have been luck.

## Why Pendulums Fail at Sea

A pendulum clock depends on two things staying steady: the swing and gravity. A ship upsets both.

- **The ship moves.** Rolling, pitching and sudden jolts push the pendulum off its rhythm. Hanging the clock in a frame helps a little. It cannot cancel every motion.
- **Temperature changes.** Heat makes the pendulum rod longer, and a longer pendulum swings more slowly. A ship sailing from the North Sea to the tropics meets large changes in heat.
- **Gravity changes with latitude.** In 1672 the French astronomer **Jean Richer** took a pendulum clock to Cayenne, near the equator. It lost about two and a half minutes a day compared with Paris. Gravity is a little weaker there, partly because Earth spins and bulges at the equator. A clock that loses minutes a day is useless for longitude. Four minutes of time is one degree, which is 60 nautical miles at the equator.

Newton listed these same troubles in 1714 when he told Parliament that a good sea watch "hath not yet been made." See [the Longitude Act of 1714](../103-the-longitude-act-of-1714/).

Huygens kept trying. The Dutch East India Company tested his improved clocks on a voyage to the Cape of Good Hope in 1686 and 1687. The results were mixed, and the method was not adopted.

## The Spring Balance

Huygens also took a different path. In January 1675 he worked out a way to control a watch with a **balance wheel and a spiral spring**, the hairspring. The spring makes the balance swing back and forth at a steady rate, like a pendulum that does not need gravity. Robert Hooke claimed he had the idea first. That argument has never been fully settled.

The spring balance made pocket watches far more accurate. But the early ones were still not steady enough for longitude. Their springs changed with heat, and the balance was easily upset.

## How This Leads to Harrison

Huygens showed what a sea clock needed: something that keeps a steady beat without relying on gravity or a calm deck. Half a century later, **John Harrison** took up the problem. His first sea clock, H1, finished in 1735, had no pendulum at all. It used two linked balances, joined so that the ship's motion acted on them in opposite ways and canceled out. His later watch, H4, used a fast-beating balance and a spring, and it corrected for temperature. In that sense it was the heir to Huygens's spring balance. For the rest of the story, see [Harrison and the marine chronometer](../84-harrison-and-the-marine-chronometer/) and [time to longitude](../70-time-to-longitude/).

## Key facts
- Huygens made the first pendulum clock in December 1656. Salomon Coster built them, and the Dutch patent is dated 16 June 1657.
- Huygens and Alexander Bruce built marine pendulum clocks between about 1662 and 1665.
- Robert Holmes took them to sea in 1663 and 1664 and claimed they found the Cape Verde islands better than dead reckoning. Historians think he overstated it.
- Pendulums fail at sea because of the ship's motion, temperature changes and gravity that varies with latitude.
- In 1672 Jean Richer's pendulum clock lost about two and a half minutes a day near the equator.
- Huygens devised the balance spring in 1675. Hooke disputed who was first.
- Harrison's H1 of 1735 used two linked balances, not a pendulum.
