Every date you write traces back to a practical decision made in Rome in the year we now call 525. A monk was not trying to invent a world calendar. He was trying to say, cleanly, which year an Easter table belonged to. The label he chose is still the one we use.

## A Monk and an Easter Table

Dionysius Exiguus was a learned monk from Scythia Minor, the Dobruja on the Black Sea. *Exiguus* is a humble byname, "the Little," not a family name. Around 525, working in Rome, he compiled a 95-year table of Easter dates, continuing Alexandrian tables attributed to Cyril of Alexandria. Those tables numbered the years from the emperor Diocletian, whose reign began in 284. Cyril's table ended in Diocletian's year 247, which is our 531. Dionysius began his own continuation in 532.

He would not keep honoring Diocletian, who had persecuted Christians. In the preface to the tables, addressed to Bishop Petronius, he wrote that he did not want "to bind to our cycles the memory of an impious man and persecutor, but chose rather to mark the years from the incarnation of our Lord Jesus Christ." (Translated for this lesson from Dionysius's Latin.) In a short computing rule in the same work, he showed that the present year, the consulship of Probus Junior, was year 525 from the Incarnation.

We do not have a clear record of how he arrived at that year one. He may have taken a Christian year-count already used in some chronicles and simply aligned it with the tables he was extending. Modern scholars, notably Alden Mosshammer, have argued that he did not independently calculate the birth of Jesus from the Gospels. What we know is the choice, and what followed from it.

## The Years That Don't Line Up

Matthew and Luke place Jesus's birth in the reign of Herod the Great. The Jewish historian Josephus says that shortly before Herod died, "that very night there was an eclipse of the moon," and that Passover followed (William Whiston's 1737 translation of the *Antiquities*, 17.6.4). Most historians identify that eclipse with the partial lunar eclipse of 13 March 4 BC. That is the usual date for Herod's death. A minority prefer a total eclipse in 1 BC, which would leave less of a gap, or none, between Herod and year one.

If the usual date is right, and if the Gospels are right that Jesus was born while Herod still lived, then Jesus was born no later than 4 BC, and perhaps a year or two earlier. Matthew mentions boys two years old and under, which is one reason some later writers placed the birth around 6 or 5 BC. On that reading, Dionysius's year one is about four to six years late. That is a careful conclusion from later scholarship, not a fact he could have looked up.

There is also no year zero. In this system, 1 BC is followed immediately by AD 1. Roman and medieval counters had no zero. Dionysius numbered years from the Incarnation forward. He did not count years before Christ at all.

## What Bede Knew, and What He Did Not

The person who turned a table-label into a way of writing history was Bede of Jarrow, in Northumbria, in the eighth century, not the ninth. He wrote *The Reckoning of Time* in 725 and the *Ecclesiastical History of the English People* in 731. He took Dionysius's year-count and used it to date a whole past.

![A page of Bede's Ecclesiastical History of the English People, the book that did most to spread Dionysius's year-count. British Library, Cotton MS Tiberius C II, folio 87v, late eighth century. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.](../../img/Bede_Ecclesiastical_History_Cotton_Tiberius_C_II_fol_87v_late_8th_century.jpg)

Bede also took a first step backward. In the summary at the end of the *History*, in A. M. Sellar's 1907 translation: "In the sixtieth year before the Incarnation of our Lord, Caius Julius Caesar, first of the Romans invaded Britain." The phrase is close to what we mean by BC, but it is not yet a complete backward calendar. Systematic "before Christ" numbering comes much later.

It is sometimes said that Bede already knew the date was wrong and kept the system because it was too useful to replace. The truth is close to that, but not quite. He compared Dionysius's numbers with other chronicles and with the Gospels, and he wrestled with the date of the Crucifixion, where older tradition and Dionysius's tables do not sit easily together. What the evidence does not show is that he identified the later, decisive argument: that Herod most likely died in 4 BC, so Jesus, if born in Herod's reign, was born before the year Dionysius called year one. That argument was worked out in the early 1600s. Bede kept and taught the count because it was a shared, workable way to date Easter and to write history.

## How the Number Spread, and Why It Stayed

English missionaries, and Alcuin of York at Charlemagne's court, carried Bede's learning to the Continent. In the Frankish kingdoms, Dionysian Easter tables largely replaced older tables in the mid-eighth century. Charlemagne's *Admonitio generalis* of 789 required that computus, the art of reckoning time and Easter, be taught in schools. Frankish annals began to hang each year's events on the year of the Incarnation.

Legal documents moved more slowly. Charlemagne's own royal diplomas still used years of his reign. AD dating becomes regular in Carolingian royal charters only in the 870s. Church books, private charters, and annals carried the system first. Law and contracts followed. By the later Middle Ages, in the Latin West, it was the ordinary way to date a document. The same long effort to keep a calendar in step with the sun is the story of [Was 1900 a Leap Year?](../110-leap-years-and-the-calendar/) and [From Unequal Hours to Equal Ones](../119-from-unequal-hours-to-equal-ones/).

In 1605 the Polish scholar Laurentius Suslyga argued that Jesus was born in 5 BC, and that at least four years must be added to the usual epoch. Johannes Kepler read him, and in 1606 and more fully in 1614 agreed that year one was too late. He identified Josephus's eclipse with the eclipse of 4 BC and placed the birth around 6 or 5 BC. His date for Herod's death became the standard view, and it remains the majority view.

By then the numbering was in church records, legal charters, contracts, and everyday habit. To move year one would have been to renumber every document for a thousand years. People kept the numbers and simply noted that the historical birth, if the usual reading of Herod is right, falls a few years "BC."

**Question.** Dionysius was labeling Easter tables, not inventing a world calendar. How did a column heading become the way the world counts years?

**Question.** If we now think year one is a few years late, why don't we just move it?

## The Last Word

He was trying to keep Easter on the right Sunday. The number he chose is still the one we write at the top of the year. Once a way of counting is woven into law, contracts, and habit, being a few years late is no longer a reason to start over.

## Key facts
- Dionysius Exiguus, a monk from Scythia Minor working in Rome around 525, compiled Easter tables and numbered the years from what he took to be Christ's incarnation, replacing the era of Diocletian.
- He may have inherited that year-count rather than calculating the birth from the Gospels. His tables begin in 532; he dated his own year as 525.
- Most historians date Herod the Great's death to 4 BC, from a lunar eclipse in Josephus followed by Passover. If Jesus was born in Herod's reign, Dionysius's year one is about four to six years late. A minority date Herod's death to 1 BC.
- There is no year zero: 1 BC is followed by AD 1. Dionysius did not count years before Christ. Bede, in the eighth century (725 and 731), popularized the forward count and took a first step toward counting backward.
- Bede compared reckonings and wrestled with the date of the Crucifixion, but he did not identify the Herod problem. Laurentius Suslyga (1605) and Johannes Kepler (1606, 1614) did.
- The count spread through Easter tables, Bede's *History*, Carolingian schools (the *Admonitio generalis* of 789), and annals, and only later into royal charters and contracts. By the time the error was pinned down, the numbering was too deeply embedded to move.
