This page was written for the learning log from the published sources named below. It is a researched account, not a transcript of a conversation.

Jean Picard was born at La Flèche on 21 July 1620 and died in Paris on 12 October 1682. He was a priest, and from 1666 a member of the Académie royale des sciences. The job on this page is one measurement: how long, on the ground, is one degree of a meridian.

A meridian is a north-south line on the Earth. Latitude is the angle, at the centre of the Earth, between the equator and the place where you stand. If you walk due north and the latitude grows by one degree, the distance you walked is one degree of that meridian. A toise is the old French fathom used to write those distances. Picard did not print metres. Where this page turns a toise into metres, it says so, and it uses 1.949 metres to the toise, a modern equivalent of the Paris toise.

## The baseline, then the triangles

You cannot tape the whole degree. You tape one short line, the baseline, and you find the rest by angles.

In a right triangle, the sine of an acute angle is the side opposite that angle, divided by the hypotenuse. The same sine belongs to that angle when the triangle is not a right triangle. In any triangle, a side divided by the sine of the opposite angle is the same number for all three sides. That is the law of sines. If you know one side and the angles, you can find the other sides.

The angles of a triangle add to 180 degrees. A degree of angle is split into 60 arcminutes, and an arcminute into 60 arcseconds. Those are angles. They are not the toises on the ground.

Here is a classroom triangle, invented so the rule can be checked. It is not a triangle from Picard's field book. Let the baseline be 10 kilometres. Let the angle at the left end be 40 degrees and the angle at the right end be 60 degrees. The angle at the far station is 180 minus 40 minus 60, which is 80 degrees. The side opposite the 40-degree angle is 10 times the sine of 40 degrees, divided by the sine of 80 degrees. The sine of 40 degrees is about 0.6428. The sine of 80 degrees is about 0.9848. So 10 times 0.6428, divided by 0.9848, is about 6.53 kilometres. Check the division on a scrap of paper. The lesson is the rule. The 6.53 is not a station Picard occupied.

Picard's own baseline was laid on the road from the mill at Villejuif to the pavilion at Juvisy. A nineteenth-century retelling puts that base at 5,663 toises. At 1.949 metres to the toise, 5,663 times 1.949 is about 11.0 kilometres. The Paris Observatory's account rounds the same base to about 11 kilometres. Thirteen triangles then carried the chain from the farm of Malvoisine, near La Ferté-Alais, to Sourdon, near Amiens. Picard names the pieces Malvoisine to Mareuil, Mareuil to Clermont, and Clermont to Sourdon.

## One degree, and the radius

Sourdon and Malvoisine do not by themselves hand you a round degree. Further triangles reach the parallel of Amiens. Picard then compared the ground distance between the parallel of Malvoisine and the parallel of Amiens with the difference of their latitudes. In *Mesure de la Terre* (1671) he states the degree as 57,060 toises, on the supposition that the Earth is a sphere.

Turn that into kilometres with the same conversion, and label the conversion. 57,060 times 2 is 114,120. 57,060 times 0.051 is 2,910. 114,120 minus 2,910 is 111,210. So 57,060 toises is 111,210 metres, to the nearest metre, or 111.210 kilometres. Three hundred sixty degrees, if every degree were that long, would be 360 times 111.210, which is 40,035.6 kilometres. That is the circumference, still under the sphere.

The radius is that circumference divided by two times π. π is the number of times the diameter fits around the circle, about 3.1416, so two times π is about 6.2832. 40,035.6 divided by 6.2832 is about 6,372 kilometres. A survey history states the same result as 3,269,300 toises, which is the same 6,372 kilometres under this conversion. The radius is a length from the centre of the Earth to the surface. It is not the 57,060, and it is not the baseline.

## The plate, and the map that is not the plate

The book is *Mesure de la Terre*, 1671. The sheet on this site is older than a finished atlas page and is not a later coastline. It is the preparatory drawing preserved at the Bibliothèque de l'Observatoire de Paris, D5/42 (14), [ark:/11287/2hkG3](https://bibnum.obspm.fr/ark:/11287/2hkG3). It depicts the 1669-1670 network: Malvoisine, Sourdon, and an Amiens inset. Orléans is not on it. The creator of the drawing is unknown. Archive credit: Bibliothèque de l'Observatoire de Paris. No commercial use.

The file, unchanged, is [Picard's triangulation, 1669-1670 (PDF)](/downloads/Picard_1669_triangulation_map.pdf).

A different sheet is the corrected map of France, presented in 1682 and published in 1693 by Philippe de La Hire after Picard's death in 1682. Picard and La Hire had taken longitudes of western ports from eclipses of Jupiter's moons, using tables prepared by Jean-Dominique Cassini, and compared them with the Paris meridian. That work moves the coast, including Brest, eastward of the older maps. It is not the triangle plate of 1671, and it is not a picture of the Malvoisine-Sourdon chain.

## Key facts
- Picard, 21 July 1620 to 12 October 1682, measured a meridian arc in 1669 and 1670 and published *Mesure de la Terre* in 1671.
- The chain runs Malvoisine, Mareuil, Clermont, Sourdon, with Amiens used for the latitude. Orléans is not on the 1671 plate.
- One degree of that meridian is 57,060 toises. At 1.949 metres to the toise, that is 111.210 kilometres, and the spherical radius is about 6,372 kilometres.
- The Observatoire plate D5/42 (14) is the 1669-1670 network. The 1693 coast map is a later publication by La Hire.
