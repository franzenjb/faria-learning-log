Our day has 24 equal hours of 60 minutes of 60 seconds. Every piece of that is borrowed. The twelves are Egyptian, the sixties are Babylonian, the equal hours are Greek astronomy made public by mechanical clocks, and the leap days are Roman and papal. This page follows them in order, then looks at two things the sky does that no calendar can flatten: Earth speeds up and slows down on its orbit, and near the poles the day itself comes apart.

## Egypt: Twelve Hours of Night, Then Twelve of Day

The oldest Egyptian hours are night hours. By about 2000 BC, star tables painted inside coffin lids list stars called **decans** (*DEK-ans*), Sirius-like stars that rose one after another. The tables have **12 rows**, so the night was split into twelve parts, each marked by a star. By the New Kingdom, from about 1500 BC, there were also **12 day hours**, measured with shadow clocks and sundials, and water clocks for the night.

Why twelve? Nobody knows for certain. One popular explanation is hand counting: four fingers have three segments each, **12** in all, and the thumb can tick them off. It is a neat idea and a real way of counting, but no Egyptian text says that is where the hours came from. Historians more often connect the twelve to the star tables themselves, which were built on 10-day periods of the civil calendar. Treat the finger story as a likely-sounding guess, not a documented fact.

These were not equal hours. Egyptian sundials and water clocks show day hours long in summer and short in winter, and night hours the reverse.

## Babylon: Counting in Sixties

Babylonian astronomers counted in base **60**. Sixty divides cleanly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30, so fractions come out even. They divided the whole day and night into **12 *bēru*** (*BAY-roo*), "double hours," and each *bēru* into **30 UŠ**. That makes 360 UŠ in a day, each the length of four of our minutes. The same sexagesimal habit divided the Sun's yearly path into 12 signs of 30 parts, **360 degrees**; that story is on [Before Time Zones](../117-before-time-zones-every-town-its-own-noon/).

Our minutes and seconds came a longer way round. Greek astronomers kept the Babylonian sixties for fractions. [Ptolemy](../104-hipparchus-ptolemy-and-the-grid/) split a degree, and an hour, into sixtieths and sixtieths of sixtieths. Medieval Latin called these the *partes minutae primae*, "first small parts," and *partes minutae secundae*, "second small parts." The first became the **minute**, the second the **second**. So the base is Babylonian, but the hour of 60 minutes of 60 seconds was assembled by later astronomers.

## Twelve Hours of Daylight, Whatever Its Length

Greeks and Romans used **seasonal hours**, also called temporal hours. The Roman *hora* was one twelfth of the daylight, sunrise to sunset, and the night had twelve more. At Rome, a little under 42° N, the summer daylight hour was about **75 minutes** and the winter one about **45**. Only at the equinoxes was an hour about 60 minutes. Noon, the end of the sixth hour, never moved.

*Geometric sunrise and sunset at 41.9° N with the Sun's tilt of 45 BC, as in Ideler's table. The sixth hour always ended at noon.*

Astronomers already used something better. [Hipparchus](../104-hipparchus-ptolemy-and-the-grid/), in the second century BC, worked with **24 equinoctial hours**, the equal hours of an equinox day, and Ptolemy's list of cities in hours east of Alexandria uses those even hours. But daily life stayed with sundials and seasonal hours for more than a thousand years.

## Equal Hours Arrive With the Clock

The mechanical clock changed that. A weight-driven clock ticks at the same rate in June and December, so its hours are equal. Mechanical clocks appear in Europe around 1300, and during the 1300s town clocks began to strike **equal hours**; a tower clock at the church of San Gottardo in Milan, working by the later 1330s and described by the chronicler Galvano Fiamma, struck all 24 hours, one stroke for the first, up to 24 for the last. Over the next centuries the **24 equal hours** of the astronomers became everyone's hours. The minute hand became common only after the [pendulum clock](../109-huygens-pendulum-clocks-at-sea/) of 1656 made clocks accurate enough to need one.

Equal hours are what made [longitude by clock](../84-harrison-and-the-marine-chronometer/) possible. One hour of difference is 15° only if every hour is the same length.

## The Calendar Problem: Moons, Days, and Years Do Not Divide

A year, from equinox to equinox, is about **365.2422 days**. A month of phases is **29.53 days**. Neither divides the other.

- **Twelve lunar months** make about **354.37 days**, about **11 days** short of the solar year. A purely lunar calendar slides through the seasons. The Islamic calendar is lunar on purpose, so Ramadan comes about 11 days earlier each year.
- Babylon kept its lunar months near the seasons by adding a **13th month** when needed, later on a fixed plan: 7 extra months in every 19 years.
- **Egypt** chose the Sun and ignored the Moon for civil purposes: **12 months of 30 days, plus 5 extra days**, the *epagomenal* (*ep-uh-GOM-en-al*) days, **365** in all. With no leap day, the civil year drifted about one day every four years against the seasons.
- **Julius Caesar** fixed Rome's calendar in **46 BC**, a year stretched to **445 days** to catch up. The Julian calendar, from **45 BC**, added a leap day every four years: an average of **365.25 days**. At first the priests inserted it every third year, and Augustus had to pause leap years to repair the count.
- 365.25 is about **11 minutes** too long. That is one day in about 128 years. By the 1500s the spring equinox had slid to about **11 March**. **Pope Gregory XIII** dropped **10 days** in **1582**: Thursday **4 October** was followed by Friday **15 October**. The new rule: a century year is a leap year only if it divides by **400**. That gives 97 leap days in 400 years and an average year of **365.2425 days**.
- **Britain and its colonies** waited until **1752**, by which time the gap was **11 days**: Wednesday **2 September** was followed by Thursday **14 September**.

The fuller leap-year story, including why 1900 was not a leap year, is on [Was 1900 a Leap Year?](../110-leap-years-and-the-calendar/).

## Kepler: Why the Seasons Are Not Equal

If Earth moved around the Sun at a steady speed, the four seasons would be equal. They are not, and [Kepler](../66-kepler-mars-triangulation-and-scale/) explained why. His second law, published in *Astronomia nova* in **1609**, says a line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out **equal areas in equal times**. Near the Sun the line is short, so the planet must move faster; far away, it moves slower.

Earth is closest to the Sun, at **perihelion**, in early January: **3 January** in 2026. It is farthest, at **aphelion**, in early July: **6 July** in 2026. The dates wander from year to year between about 2 and 5 January and 3 and 6 July. NASA gives Earth's speed as about **30.29 km/s** at its fastest and **29.29 km/s** at its slowest, a difference of about 3 percent.

So Earth hurries through the northern winter. Using the U.S. Naval Observatory's times, the northern winter of 2025–26 lasted **89.0 days**, and the summer of 2026 lasted **93.7 days**: winter is about **4.7 days** shorter. From the March equinox to the September equinox is about **186.4 days**; the other half of the year is about **178.8**. Northern winters are not cold because we are far from the Sun; we are closest in January. The seasons come from the tilt.

## The Poles: When the Day Comes Apart

At the equator, day and night are each about 12 hours all year. Move north and the summer days lengthen. At the **Arctic Circle**, about **66.56° N** (90° minus Earth's tilt of 23.44°), the Sun in principle stays up for 24 hours on the June solstice and stays down for 24 hours on the December solstice. North of it come the **midnight sun** and the **polar night**. [Pytheas](../118-pytheas-of-massalia/), about 325 BC, brought the Greeks the first reports of nights that barely came at all.

At the **North Pole** there is one sunrise a year, near the March equinox, and one sunset, near the September equinox. That is often called six months of day and six of night, but two things tip the balance toward daylight:

- **Refraction** lifts the Sun's image by about half a degree at the horizon, so you see the Sun while it is still geometrically below the horizon. It adds daylight, rather than taking it away.
- **Kepler** again: the northern summer half-year is about a week longer than the winter half.

Together, with standard refraction, the Sun's upper edge is above the horizon at the North Pole for about **190 days** in 2026, and below it for about **175**. At the South Pole the Kepler effect works against daylight, and the figure is about **183 days**. Refraction in polar air varies, so these are typical values, not exact ones. The same refraction carries the midnight sun a little south of the Arctic Circle.

## A Decimal Day, Briefly

The French Revolution wanted every measure in tens. The [metre](../83-the-metre-from-a-meridian/), defined in **1791** as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator, was measured by **Delambre** and **Méchain** along the meridian from Dunkirk to Barcelona. Time got the same treatment. A decree of **24 November 1793** divided the day, midnight to midnight, into **10 hours** of **100 minutes** of **100 seconds**. Noon was 5 o'clock. A decimal hour was 2 hours 24 minutes of ours, and a decimal second 0.864 of ours. It was required for public records only from **22 September 1794**, and on **7 April 1795** the same law that set out the metric system suspended it indefinitely. Some towns and records kept it for a few more years. The Republican calendar that went with it, 12 months of 30 days plus 5 or 6 extra days, was close to Egypt's, and it lasted until Napoleon ended it at the start of **1806**. The metre survived; the decimal hour did not.

## Ptolemy's Zero Is Not Quite at the Canaries

[Ptolemy](../104-hipparchus-ptolemy-and-the-grid/) counted longitude east from the **Fortunate Isles**, generally identified with the Canaries. In his catalogue (*Geography* 4.6) the six islands are not all on the zero line. In the usual text, several sit at **0°**, and **Canaria** itself is at **1° east**, so the zero meridian runs through the westernmost of his islands, a degree west of Canaria. The manuscripts differ by a degree for some islands. Ptolemy also put the islands at about 10½° to 16° N; the real Canaries are near 28° N.

Later Europe kept the idea of a zero at the western edge. In **1634**, at a meeting called under **Cardinal Richelieu**, **Louis XIII** ordered French maps to count from the island of **Ferro**, today's El Hierro, the westernmost of the Canaries. Its true position was hard to pin down, so French geographers simply defined the Ferro meridian as exactly **20° west of Paris**, a value standard by the mid-1700s. The real island lies about 20°23′ west of Paris. The Ferro meridian is therefore about **17°40′ west of Greenwich**. At the [1884 conference](../106-the-1884-meridian-conference/), a French delegate admitted it was really "the meridian of Paris disguised." On the [Where Am I?](/claude1/v3/?talk=30) talk, Ferro is one of the choices in the vote on where zero should be.
