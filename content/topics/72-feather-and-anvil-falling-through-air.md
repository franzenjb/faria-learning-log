Release a feather and an anvil from rest, in air, and follow the downward speed. At the first instant the two speeds rise together. The slope on the sheet is 9.81 metres per second squared for both. That is the familiar acceleration of a body near Earth's surface when the air has not yet begun to push back. The sheet states the consequence in one line: the anvil does not accelerate faster at release.

Acceleration is the change in velocity during a second. Velocity on this page is the downward speed, in metres per second. Time is seconds since the release. Both curves start at zero because both objects start at rest.

Air resistance is the push of the air against a body that is already moving. The push grows as the speed grows. Weight stays the weight. At the opening instant the speed is still zero, so the two objects share that first acceleration. Afterward the paths separate. The feather is light and spread out, and the air's push soon balances its weight. The anvil in this picture is heavy and compact, and it keeps gaining speed much longer.

Balance is the terminal speed. The air's push has matched the weight, so the acceleration is zero and the downward speed stops changing. The object is still moving downward at that speed. Terminal speed is the end of the speeding-up. It is the continued fall at a steady pace.

The sheet is an illustrative model. It is not a record of a measured feather or a measured anvil. The chosen limit for the feather is 2 metres per second. The chosen limit for the anvil is 60 metres per second. A real object takes its limit from its mass, its shape, and the air it falls through. The two numbers were picked so both curves fit on one graph.

The formula printed on the sheet is v(t) = V × tanh(9.81 t / V). Here v is the downward velocity, t is the time in seconds, and V is the chosen terminal speed. tanh is the hyperbolic tangent. At the instant of release, t = 0, and the formula gives v = 0. The opening slope is 9.81 metres per second squared for either choice of V. That is why the curves leave the origin together. As time goes on, v approaches V and then stays there. This is the formula for a drag force that grows with the square of the speed.

The small panel enlarges the first half-second, so the shared slope can be read before the feather's curve bends over. The main panel runs to 25 seconds. By then the feather has long been at its chosen 2 metres per second, still falling, and the anvil is still climbing toward its chosen 60.

The sheet assumes enough distance to fall. Impact with the ground is not part of the model.
