To find longitude at sea you need to know the time at home while you are far away. Harrison's answer was to carry home time in a box, a [marine chronometer](../84-harrison-and-the-marine-chronometer/). The other answer was to read it off the sky. The Moon moves steadily against the stars, like the hand of a clock across a dial. Measure where it is, look up when it would be there as seen from Greenwich, and you have Greenwich time. That idea took about 250 years to become practical.

## The Moon as a Clock Hand

The Moon goes around the sky once a month. Against the background stars it moves about 13° a day, roughly half a degree an hour. Half a degree is about the Moon's own width. So every hour, the Moon slides about one Moon-width along its path.

The navigator measures the angle between the Moon and a bright star, or the Sun. That angle is the **lunar distance**. If a table says the distance will be, for example, 40° 10' at 3 a.m. Greenwich time, and you measure exactly that, then it is 3 a.m. at Greenwich. Compare with your own local time, from the Sun or a star, and the difference gives longitude, 15° for every hour.

The catch is precision. Because the Moon moves only about 30 arcminutes an hour, an error of 1 arcminute in the measured distance is an error of about 2 minutes of time. Two minutes of time is half a degree of longitude, about 30 nautical miles at the equator. Every small error in the instrument or the tables is multiplied.

## Werner's Idea, 1514

Johannes Werner was a priest and mathematician in Nuremberg. In 1514 he published a new Latin translation of the first book of Ptolemy's *Geography*, with his own commentary. In it he described finding longitude by measuring the Moon's distance from a star with a cross-staff and comparing it with predicted positions. Peter Apian took the idea up in 1524, and the name "lunar distance method" grew from that work. The *Geography* itself is on [Hipparchus, Ptolemy and the Grid](../104-hipparchus-ptolemy-and-the-grid/).

The idea was right. It could not be used. Two things were missing.

- **Good tables.** The Moon's motion is tangled. The Sun and Earth both pull on it, and its speed and path wobble. No one in 1514 could predict its position to a few arcminutes, and no one could for another two centuries.
- **A good instrument.** A cross-staff held up on a rolling deck could not measure an angle to a minute of arc.

## Mayer's Tables and the Reflecting Octant

The instrument came first. In 1731 John Hadley in England, and Thomas Godfrey in Philadelphia independently, devised a reflecting quadrant, usually called the **octant**. Two mirrors bring one object onto the image of another, so the pair holds steady even when the ship moves. Later, larger versions became the **sextant**, which could measure the wider angles that lunar distances often need. How the sextant takes a height is on [The Sextant and Latitude at Sea](../87-sextant-and-latitude-at-sea/).

The tables came from Tobias Mayer, an astronomer at Göttingen. His lunar tables, published in the early 1750s and sent to Britain's Board of Longitude in 1755, were the first good enough to try at sea. Mayer died in 1762. The Board later paid his widow £3,000 for the tables, and paid Leonhard Euler £300 for theory that had helped.

## Maskelyne Tests It

Nevil Maskelyne sailed to St Helena in 1761 to watch the transit of Venus. Clouds spoiled the transit. The voyage still mattered, because he and Robert Waddington tried lunar distances with Mayer's tables on the way out and back. They worked.

In 1763 Maskelyne published *The British Mariner's Guide*, a handbook for the method that included Mayer's tables. But the arithmetic was brutal. By Maskelyne's own estimate, working one observation from Mayer's tables took about three to four hours.

His answer was to move the hard part ashore. In 1765 he became Astronomer Royal. That year the Board backed a yearly almanac, edited by him. The first **Nautical Almanac**, for the year **1767**, came out in late 1766. It listed the Moon's predicted distance from the Sun and selected stars, every three hours, in Greenwich time. The navigator still had to correct his measured distance for refraction and parallax, but most of the computation was done before the ship sailed.

## The Human Computers

The almanac's tables were worked out by hand by a small network of paid **computers**, people who computed. A **comparer** checked their work. Royal Museums Greenwich calls the work tedious and poorly paid. One of the few women named in the record is Mary Edwards, who became skilled enough to help train new computers. Maskelyne ran this network for over forty years, until his death in 1811.

## Clock Against Moon

Maskelyne and Harrison were rivals. In 1765 the Board recognized both Harrison's watch H4 and the lunar method. Maskelyne, as Astronomer Royal, then tested H4 at Greenwich and reported unfavorably, and Harrison accused him of bias. Historians still argue over how fair Maskelyne was.

In practice, both won. Chronometers were costly and scarce for decades, while a lunar needed only a sextant and an almanac. Navigators often used lunars to check their chronometers. The American navigator [Nathaniel Bowditch](../49-bowditch/) made lunars easier to work in his *New American Practical Navigator* of 1802, and lunars stayed in use well into the 1800s.

## Key facts
- The Moon moves about half a degree an hour against the stars, roughly its own width. Its distance from a star is a clock.
- A 1-arcminute error in a lunar distance means about 2 minutes of time, or half a degree of longitude.
- Johannes Werner of Nuremberg proposed the method in 1514, in his commentary on Ptolemy's Geography.
- It waited for Hadley's reflecting octant, 1731, and Tobias Mayer's lunar tables, sent to London in 1755.
- Maskelyne tested lunars on his 1761 voyage to St Helena and published The British Mariner's Guide in 1763.
- The first Nautical Almanac, for 1767, gave predicted lunar distances every three hours, computed by hand by paid computers and checked by comparers.
- Working a lunar from Mayer's raw tables took about three to four hours. The almanac moved most of that work ashore.
- The Board of Longitude recognized both H4 and the lunar method in 1765. Ships used both, often together.
