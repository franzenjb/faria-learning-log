Jupiter's four big moons are a clock and a ruler. Seen from opposite sides of Earth's orbit, the ruler changes size, and that tells you something real about distances. It does not, by itself, tell you miles.

## 1. A clock and a ruler

Galileo found the moons in January 1610: Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto, outward from Jupiter.

**The clock.** Io goes around in about 42.5 hours. When it enters Jupiter's shadow, the sunlight it reflects is cut off, and that eclipse can be timed. Galileo's plan to use it as a longitude clock is [Galileo's moons](../64-galileo-jupiters-moons/).

**The ruler.** Callisto, the outer moon, stays about 1.88 million km from Jupiter. At its farthest swing to one side, its *greatest elongation*, the gap from Jupiter to Callisto is a stick of fixed length.

![Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto at their true distances from Jupiter; Io is the clock, Jupiter to Callisto is the ruler](../../img/Jupiter_Four_Moons_Clock_and_Ruler_To_Scale.svg)

## 2. Two sides of Earth's orbit

Jupiter is about 5.2 AU from the Sun. (1 AU, Earth's average distance from the Sun, is about 93 million miles.)

- **Near side (opposition):** Jupiter opposite the Sun in our night sky. Earth is about 5.2 − 1 = **4.2 AU** from Jupiter.
- **Far side (conjunction):** Jupiter beyond the Sun. Earth is about 5.2 + 1 = **6.2 AU** away.

The difference, about 2 AU, is the width of Earth's orbit.

![Plan view: Earth on a slight ellipse around an off-centre Sun, near side 4.2 AU and far side 6.2 AU from Jupiter, glare zone marked at the far side](../../img/Earth_Slight_Ellipse_Near_and_Far_from_Jupiter_Glare_Zone.svg)
*Drawn for this lesson. At the far side Jupiter is lost in the Sun's glare for some weeks, so real "far" views come from the morning or evening sky, a little short of 6.2 AU.*

## 3. A fixed stick looks smaller farther away

The angle you see is the true length divided by the distance. An object looks 1 arcminute (1′) wide when it is 1/3,438 of its distance, like a dime seen face-on at about 200 feet.

- **Near:** 4.2 AU ≈ 628 million km. There 1′ covers 628,000,000 ÷ 3,438 ≈ 183,000 km. Callisto's 1,880,000 km ÷ 183,000 ≈ **10′**.
- **Far:** 6.2 AU ≈ 928 million km. There 1′ covers about 270,000 km. 1,880,000 ÷ 270,000 ≈ **7′**.

![Callisto about 10 arcminutes from Jupiter when Earth is near, about 7 when Earth is far](../../img/Callisto_Elongation_About_10_vs_7_Arcminutes_Near_and_Far.svg)
*Same stick, two distances, one angular scale. Rounded, idealized numbers.*

## 4. Ratios, not miles

The two angles give a ratio: 10.3′ ÷ 7.0′ ≈ 1.48, so the far distance is about 1.48 times the near one. Far minus near is about 2 AU. Together, near ≈ 2 ÷ 0.48 ≈ 4.2 AU, which fits Kepler's scale. That is a teaching check, not a historical method.

Every answer comes out in AU. Even the 1.88 million km is known today only because we know the AU. To get miles you need one length measured on the ground. That came from other work: Cassini and Richer's Mars parallax of 1672, then the [transits of Venus in 1761 and 1769](../55-venus-transits-1761-1769/). Today 1 AU = 149,597,871 km.

## 5. Rømer timed the same stretch

In 1676 Rømer argued that Io's eclipses run late when Earth is farther from Jupiter and early when nearer, because light needs time to cross the extra distance. Same geometry, measured in time instead of angle. The full story is the [Rømer lesson](../65-romer-io-speed-of-light/).

![Light crossing the 2 AU width of Earth's orbit: modern 16 minutes 40 seconds, traditional about 22 minutes](../../img/Romer_Same_Stretch_Light_Time_16_40_vs_22_Minutes.svg)
*Modern: 299,195,742 km ÷ 299,792.458 km/s ≈ 998 s ≈ 16 min 40 s. The traditional figure is about 22 minutes.*

Rømer did not publish a speed. Huygens, in 1690, turned an orbital scale into a speed near 230,000 km/s. Once the AU is known in miles, the minutes become a speed; with a known speed, the delay reads as a distance.

**Not two magic endpoints.** The glare hides Jupiter near conjunction, so the "far" data fall short of 2 AU. Jupiter moves about 30° a year, Earth's orbit is a slight ellipse, and Io is not a perfect clock. The argument is a stack of dates. The 4.2 AU, 6.2 AU, 10′, and 7′ here are rounded teaching numbers.

## Key facts
- Io is the clock (about 42.5 hours). Jupiter to Callisto, about 1.88 million km, is the ruler.
- Earth is about 4.2 AU from Jupiter on the near side and about 6.2 AU on the far side, a change of about 2 AU.
- Callisto's greatest elongation is about 10′ near and about 7′ far. Angle ≈ length ÷ distance.
- Angles give ratios in AU, not miles. Miles came from Mars in 1672 and the Venus transits of 1761 and 1769.
- Rømer (1676) timed the same stretch: traditional 22 minutes, modern 16 minutes 40 seconds. Huygens, not Rømer, gave a speed.
