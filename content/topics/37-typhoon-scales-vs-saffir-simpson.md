## Correction, 8 November 2026
Three sentences on this page, as it stood from 2 November, were wrong. They are withdrawn. The original day of the lesson stays 2 November. This note is the correction.

First, the page said a 1-minute wind reads about 12 percent higher than the 10-minute average, and it also said to multiply the 10-minute wind by 1.14. Those two instructions disagree. 1.14 is 14 percent higher, not 12 percent. Neither number is kept as a rule.

Second, the page said a longer average always gives a lower number, because it dilutes gusts with lulls. That is not true of every minute you might pick. A quiet minute can sit below the 10-minute mean. What does follow from the arithmetic of one trace: if you split ten minutes into ten non-overlapping one-minute averages, the largest of those ten is at least as large as their ten-minute mean, and some of the ten can be smaller. That inequality is about those ten pieces. It is not a law that every one-minute sample is higher, and it is not how the National Hurricane Center assigns a storm's intensity.

Third, the page turned a reporting difference into extra physical force: about 12 percent more speed, therefore about 25 percent more force, for the same storm. If only the averaging convention changed, the wind did not get stronger. The square law compares two real speeds. It does not square a change of label. The [8 November lesson](/topics/71-hurricane-wind-force-square-not-exponential/) is where the square belongs, and only when density, area, and shape are the things held still.

## The question
Looking at Wikipedia's RSMC Tokyo typhoon scale, Jeff asked: does Saffir–Simpson not apply to typhoons? The answer: it does, but not officially.

## RSMC Tokyo: the official scale
The **Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA)**, acting as **RSMC Tokyo**, is the World Meteorological Organization's official warning center for the western North Pacific. Its scale uses **10-minute sustained winds**:

- **Tropical Depression**: ≤33 kt (≤61 km/h)
- **Tropical Storm**: 34–47 kt (62–88 km/h)
- **Severe Tropical Storm**: 48–63 kt (89–117 km/h)
- **Typhoon**: 64–84 kt (118–156 km/h)
- **Very Strong Typhoon**: 85–104 kt (157–193 km/h)
- **Violent Typhoon**: ≥105 kt (≥194 km/h)

## The American view: JTWC
The US **Joint Typhoon Warning Center (JTWC)** reports **1-minute sustained winds**. JTWC unofficially applies the **Saffir–Simpson** categories to typhoons, and calls any storm of **≥130 kt** (1-minute) a **super typhoon**, roughly a strong Category 4 to a Category 5. The IBTrACS technical notes record that the U.S. agencies, NOAA and JTWC, report a 1-minute averaging time for the sustained wind. That sentence names the averaging window. It is not a factor for turning Tokyo's number into JTWC's number.

## One storm, two labels
**Typhoon Haiyan** (called **Yolanda** in the Philippines), 2013, was a **Violent Typhoon** to JMA and a **Category 5 super typhoon** to JTWC. Same storm. The two offices printed different class names. This page does not convert one printed wind into the other.

## Every basin has its own scale
- **India (IMD)** ranks North Indian Ocean storms in steps up to **super cyclonic storm**.
- **Australia (Bureau of Meteorology)** has its own **Category 1–5** scale based on **wind gusts**, not sustained winds, so an Australian Category 3 is not the same as a US Category 3.

## The class boundaries, kept apart
The old table on this page multiplied each Tokyo bound by 1.14 and set the product beside a Saffir–Simpson category. That column is withdrawn. It was written as if it were a converter. It is not used here.

The US **super typhoon** threshold (≥130 kt 1-minute) falls inside Tokyo's **Violent Typhoon** class.

## What the two averaging windows are, and what they are not
JMA's scale, above, uses a **10-minute** sustained wind. The National Hurricane Center's description of Saffir–Simpson uses a **peak 1-minute** wind at 10 meters over unobstructed exposure. JTWC reports a **1-minute** averaging time. Those are the windows the agencies name. The World Meteorological Organization's standard for estimating the mean wind, in the 2010 guidelines cited below, is the **10-minute** average.

A gust is not that mean. A 3-second gust is a short average of its own. The maximum 3-second gust during a 10-minute period is the largest of those short averages inside the period. It is not the 10-minute average. The Florida design speed on the [8 November lesson](/topics/71-hurricane-wind-force-square-not-exponential/) is one such 3-second gust, used for a building. JMA also publishes a maximum gust separately from its 10-minute sustained wind. None of those gusts is the sustained wind that names the class.

The National Hurricane Center's 2013 aircraft-data briefing says intensity is not the highest 1-minute wind that exists in the circulation, and not the highest 1-minute wind occurring over an interval of time. The advisory intensity is the expected value of the maximum sustained surface wind at advisory time. A best-track intensity is that analyzed value. It is not the recipe "take the strongest minute on one anemometer trace."

Bruce Harper's guidelines, prepared for WMO and dated 27 August 2010, recommend an **at-sea** conversion of **0.93** between the 1-minute sustained estimate of peak storm intensity and the 10-minute average, rather than a traditional **0.88**, which that review associates more with an off-land exposure. The recommendation depends on exposure. It is an estimate relating two mean winds. The same paper separates that job from a gust factor, which is a theoretical step from a mean wind to a gust, and which scatters in real storms. This page does not adopt 0.93, or 0.88, as a number to multiply in operations.

![A drawn ten-minute trace. The mean is 87 knots. One strong minute is 99 knots. A quiet minute sits below the mean. 99 divided by 87 is about 1.14 in this drawing only.](../../img/Wind_1_Minute_and_10_Minute_Illustration_Not_a_Factor.svg)
*87 kt is the mean of these ten minutes. 99 kt is one strong minute inside them. 99 ÷ 87 is about 1.14, which is 14 percent higher than 87, in this drawing only. A quiet minute is drawn below 87. The drawing is not an NHC intensity and not a factor for converting agency reports.*

On one trace, ten non-overlapping one-minute averages lie both above and below their ten-minute mean. The largest of them is at least the mean. A minute you select because it is quiet can be lower. The old picture on this page annotated 87 kt and 99 kt as about 13 percent more speed and about 28 percent more force. That annotation is withdrawn. The 87 and the 99 can stay as a picture of one strong minute beside a ten-minute mean. Squaring 1.14 would compare two different true speeds. It does not describe two names for one storm.

Before comparing records, name the agency and the averaging period. The class names above are the agencies' own bins. They are not a conversion table.

## Connection
This picks up the Nov 1 lesson on the words themselves: **hurricane** from the Taíno god **Hurakán**, and **typhoon** from the Chinese **tai fung**, "great wind." The words differ by ocean, and so do the rulers used to measure the storms.

## Key facts
- Corrected 8 November 2026. The 12 percent claim, the factor 1.14, the sentence that a longer average is always lower, and the step from that reporting gap to 25 percent more force are withdrawn.
- JMA uses 10-minute sustained winds. Saffir–Simpson and JTWC use 1-minute sustained winds. Those names are not a multiplier.
- JMA scale: TD ≤33 kt, TS 34–47, STS 48–63, Typhoon 64–84, Very Strong 85–104, Violent ≥105 kt.
- JTWC "super typhoon" = ≥130 kt 1-minute winds, roughly strong Cat 4 to Cat 5.
- Haiyan (Yolanda) 2013: Violent Typhoon to JMA, Category 5 super typhoon to JTWC.
- India's IMD tops out at "super cyclonic storm."
- Australia's BoM Categories 1–5 are based on gusts and don't match US categories.
- Saffir–Simpson (1-min): Cat 1 64–82 kt, Cat 2 83–95, Cat 3 96–112, Cat 4 113–136, Cat 5 ≥137. These bins are not converted from Tokyo's bins.
- The US super typhoon threshold (≥130 kt 1-min) is JTWC's own threshold. It is not a translation of Tokyo's Violent Typhoon bound.
- WMO's 2010 guidelines recommend 0.93 at sea, not 0.88, and say the choice depends on exposure. That recommendation is not a gust-factor conversion and not an operational multiplier on this page.
- On one trace, the largest of ten non-overlapping one-minute averages is at least their ten-minute mean. A quiet minute can be lower. NHC intensity is an analyzed expected value, not that maximum minute.
