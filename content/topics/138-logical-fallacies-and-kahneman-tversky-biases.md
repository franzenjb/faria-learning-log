A fallacy is a move in an argument. A bias is a tilt in the mind. They often travel together, and they are not the same thing. This section is a refresher: four short pages, one chart, and a PDF you can print.

It belongs with the Kahneman and Tversky thread that began on the evening of 8 November 2026, in [the note of one evening](../85-kahneman-tversky-the-first-evening/), and continued on 20 November with [the peak-end rule](../120-peak-end-rule/).

## The Difference, in One Minute

A **fallacy** is a defect in an argument. Someone may offer a straw man, a false dilemma, or a hasty generalization. You can point at the move and say, "That does not follow."

A **bias**, in the sense Kahneman and Tversky studied, is a systematic tilt in judgment. The mind uses shortcuts that are usually cheap and often useful, and that also lead to regular errors. You can feel the tilt in yourself, even when no one is arguing with you.

The tightest overlap is the **conjunction fallacy**. It has a formal name in logic: a combination of two facts cannot be more likely than one of those facts alone. It also has a famous experiment, the Linda problem of 1983, which is on the [probability page](../141-probability-traps-linda-base-rates-and-regression/). If a story fits "feminist bank teller" better than "bank teller," representativeness makes the longer description feel more likely. That is a bias doing the work of a fallacy.

## The Papers, in the Order of the Work

In 1974, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman published "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases" in *Science*. The shortcuts, they wrote, are highly economical and usually effective, and they also lead to systematic errors. Anchoring, availability, and representativeness are in that paper. So are the law of small numbers, the gambler's fallacy, and base rates, treated under representativeness. A related base-rate paper, "On the Psychology of Prediction," had appeared in 1973.

In 1979 they published "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk" in *Econometrica*. Gains, losses, and the different ways people take risks are in that one paper. The same year, in "Intuitive Prediction: Biases and Corrective Procedures," they described the planning fallacy.

In 1981, in *Science* again, they showed that the wording of a choice can reverse it while the facts stay the same. That is the Asian-disease framing problem.

In 1983, in *Psychological Review*, they gave the conjunction fallacy its name and its most famous story: Linda.

In 2011 Kahneman gathered the program, including later ideas such as System 1 and System 2, WYSIATI, and two selves, in *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. The labels "System 1" and "System 2" were proposed by Keith Stanovich and Richard West in 2000; Kahneman adopted them. WYSIATI — "what you see is all there is" — is his phrase from that book.

This section does not invent quotations. When a number is given, it comes from one of those papers, or from a later study that is named on the page.

## Key facts
- A fallacy is a move in an argument. A bias is a tilt in the mind. They often travel together and are not the same thing.
- The tightest overlap is the conjunction fallacy: "feminist bank teller" cannot be more likely than "bank teller," yet it can feel more likely because it better matches the story of Linda (Tversky and Kahneman, 1983).
- The 1974 *Science* paper is the source for the three heuristics. The 1979 *Econometrica* paper is prospect theory. The 1981 *Science* paper is framing. The 1983 *Psychological Review* paper is Linda. *Thinking, Fast and Slow* appeared in 2011.
- System 1 and System 2 were named by Stanovich and West (2000). Kahneman adopted the labels. WYSIATI is his phrase.
