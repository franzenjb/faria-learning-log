On a dark, stormy night in October 1707, a British fleet coming home from the Mediterranean ran onto the rocks of the Isles of Scilly, off the tip of Cornwall. Four warships were lost. So was the admiral, Sir Cloudesley Shovell. It was one of the worst disasters in the history of the Royal Navy. It is also the story most often told as the reason Britain offered a prize for longitude. That link is weaker than the legend says.

## The Fleet Coming Home

Shovell was one of the best-known admirals of his day. In the summer of 1707 he had supported the allied attack on the French naval base at Toulon, in the War of the Spanish Succession. The attack failed to take the city, though the French sank many of their own warships in the harbor to keep them safe.

With the campaign over, Shovell headed home. Twenty-one warships were at Gibraltar on 29 September 1707, getting ready for the voyage north. Shovell flew his flag in the *Association*, a big three-decker. The weather was bad for much of the trip, and the sky was often too cloudy for a good sight of the Sun.

## The Night of 22 October

Britain still used the old Julian calendar in 1707. The wreck happened on the night of 22 October, Old Style. On the modern calendar that is 2 November 1707.

The fleet thought it was safely west of the Channel approaches. It was not. At about 8 p.m. the *Association* struck the Outer Gilstone rock in the Western Rocks of Scilly and sank within minutes. Every man aboard was lost, about 800 of them, Shovell among them.

Three more ships went down that night:

- **Eagle**, a ship of the line, lost with all hands.
- **Romney**, a smaller warship. One man, quartermaster George Lawrence, is said to have survived.
- **Firebrand**, a fireship. A couple of dozen of her men got ashore.

Other ships came close to the rocks and escaped.

## How Many Died

No one knows the exact number. Estimates usually run from about **1,400 to 2,000** men. Royal Museums Greenwich has given "over 1,300." Historic England has given "over 2,000." The muster books were not complete, and the ships were not all at full strength. The honest answer is a range.

Shovell's body came ashore at Porthellick Cove on St. Mary's. It was later taken to London and buried in Westminster Abbey.

## What Went Wrong

The fleet did not know where it was. Navigators of 1707 had two tools for that. They could measure **latitude** from the height of the Sun at noon. They worked out everything else by **dead reckoning**: speed, heading and time, added up hour by hour. Every small error in that sum grew over a long voyage. Clouds had kept them from good latitude sights for days.

In 1960 W. E. May of the National Maritime Museum studied the surviving ships' logbooks, more than 40 of them. His work, and later research, points to several causes at once:

- **Latitude error.** Most of the fleet's own estimates put them north of their true position. A ship that thinks it is farther north than it is can sail straight into Scilly while believing it is passing safely clear.
- **Poor charts.** Scilly itself was not well placed on the charts of the time, in latitude or in longitude.
- **Unknown currents.** Currents at the mouth of the Channel could set a ship off course without anyone noticing.
- **Compasses and tables.** Compass errors and errors in printed navigation tables added to the mess.
- **No way to find longitude.** Nobody at sea could measure longitude directly. This mattered, but May's study did not find it to be the main error.

So the short version, "they did not know their longitude," is not wrong. It is just incomplete. Many historians now think latitude and bad charts did more damage than longitude did.

## Myth and Evidence

Two famous stories grew up around the wreck.

**The hanged sailor.** A common seaman is said to have warned Shovell that the fleet was off course, and to have been hanged for it. There is no record of this from 1707. The story seems to appear first on Scilly around 1780, and it grew in the telling. Naval historians reject it. Dava Sobel's popular book *Longitude* repeated it as fact.

**The murdered admiral.** Another legend says Shovell reached shore alive and was killed by a local woman for his emerald ring, and that she confessed on her deathbed decades later. There is no good evidence for this either. What seems true is that his body was found and stripped, and the ring was missing.

## Did It Cause the Longitude Act?

Many books say the 1707 wreck caused the [Longitude Act of 1714](../103-the-longitude-act-of-1714/). Historians now debate how direct that link was. Researchers at Royal Museums Greenwich found little sign of public talk linking the wreck to longitude in the seven years after it. The one clear mention came in a 1714 flyer by William Whiston and Humphry Ditton, written while the bill was already moving. Their longer book on the subject did not mention the wreck at all.

The fairest summary: the disaster was a famous example of what bad navigation could cost. It may have helped the argument. There is no strong evidence that it drove the Act on its own. For how a clock later solved longitude, see [Harrison and the marine chronometer](../84-harrison-and-the-marine-chronometer/) and [time to longitude](../70-time-to-longitude/).

## Key facts
- The wreck was on the night of 22 October 1707, Old Style, which is 2 November 1707 on the modern calendar.
- Four ships were lost: Association (the flagship), Eagle, Romney and Firebrand. Admiral Sir Cloudesley Shovell died.
- The fleet was coming home after the failed allied attack on Toulon, by way of Gibraltar.
- Deaths are usually given as about 1,400 to 2,000. Sources disagree.
- W. E. May's 1960 logbook study found latitude error, poor charts, currents and compass errors mattered more than longitude alone.
- The hanged-sailor and murdered-admiral stories have no good evidence behind them.
- Historians debate whether the wreck directly caused the 1714 Longitude Act. The evidence for a direct link is thin.
