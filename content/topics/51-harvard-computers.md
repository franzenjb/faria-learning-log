## 1. The word: *computer* meant a person

**Computer** is **compute** + **-er**: a person who computes.

- Earliest recorded English use: **Richard Brathwaite** (also spelled Braithwaite), *The Yong Mans Gleanings* (London, **1613**):¹

> "I haue read the truest **computer** of Times, and the best Arithmetician that euer breathed, and he reduceth thy dayes into a short number."

- The *Oxford English Dictionary* sense that matters here (chiefly historical): *"A person who makes calculations or computations… specifically a person employed to make calculations in an observatory, in surveying, etc."*
- Machine sense arrives much later: calculating machines mid-19th century; programmable electronic computers mid-20th century (OED's early electronic citations cluster around the **1940s**). Until then, *computer* almost always meant a human being.

**Gloss.** In Brathwaite the "computer" is **God**, the reckoner of our days ("The daies of Man are threescore and ten"); the word then moved from a reckoner of time, to human calculators like the Harvard women, to machines.

**Human computers before Harvard.** The British *Nautical Almanac* (founded **1767**, tables from **1767** onward under Nevil Maskelyne) hired dozens of human computers to produce the ephemerides sailors needed for lunars and for finding longitude. From **1765 to 1811** about **35** computers worked on it; one of them, **Mary Edwards**, was a woman who computed for a living after her husband's death. That world is the direct cousin of [Nathaniel Bowditch](/topics/49-bowditch/)'s world: tables of the Moon, the Sun, and the stars, checked and rechecked by hand, then bound into the book a navigator trusted with a ship.

So when Jeff hit the word *computer* on a Polaris page and felt something was off, his ear was right. The word had not yet been stolen by the machine.

## 2. Edward Pickering and the factory observatory

**Edward Charles Pickering** (1846–1919) became director of the **Harvard College Observatory** in **1877**. Photography and spectroscopy were turning astronomy into a flood of data. Glass photographic plates could freeze the whole sky; objective-prism spectra could record thousands of stellar fingerprints on a single exposure. Someone had to *measure* them.

Pickering specialized the labor. Night observers (mostly men) took the plates; daytime staff measured positions, magnitudes, and spectra. Starting in the early **1880s** (the program is often dated from **1881**, when Williamina Fleming moved from the household staff into the computing room; by **1883** half a dozen women were already at work, and by the late 1880s about fourteen), he hired **women** for that daytime analysis. Over the decades the group grew to roughly **80** women in total. They were called **computers**. The observing itself stayed mostly closed to them.

**Why women?** Skilled, careful, and — crucially for the budget — cheap. Typical pay was about **25 cents an hour** (Sobel and AIP sources; some cite 25–30¢). Men doing similar work earned substantially more (AIP: nearly five times as much in one comparison; other accounts say about 40% more). Fleming, once she became curator of astronomical photographs (**1899**), earned about **50 cents an hour**. The mocking contemporary nickname for the group was **"Pickering's Harem."** Modern writers drop the sneer and say **the Harvard Computers**.

**The glass.** Over decades the Observatory (Cambridge, Massachusetts, plus a southern station at **Arequipa, Peru**) amassed on the order of **500,000** glass photographic plates — still one of the great sky archives on Earth. The plates were funded in large part by **Anna Palmer Draper**, widow of the pioneer astrophotographer **Henry Draper**. She underwrote the **Henry Draper Memorial** that paid for the spectral survey and the catalogues that still carry his name: the **Henry Draper Catalogue** (HD).

## 3. Williamina Fleming: from housemaid to curator

**Williamina Paton Stevens Fleming** (1857–1911) was a Scottish schoolteacher who emigrated to Boston, was abandoned by her husband while pregnant, and took work as a **maid / housekeeper** in Pickering's household around **1879**. In **1881** he moved her onto the Observatory payroll as a computer. The story that he declared his housemaid could do better than his (male) assistants is the popular version of a real promotion.

What she did:

- Helped invent and run the early **Harvard** spectral classification (letters for types of spectra).
- Discovered the **Horsehead Nebula** on a plate taken **1888** February 6 by William Henry Pickering (Edward's brother); she noted the dark nebula on **1888** May 19 while examining the plate. (Credit for the *discovery* is hers; the plate was his.)
- Found hundreds of variable stars, dozens of novae, and the first recognized **white dwarfs** (spectral oddities that later got that name).
- In **1899** became Harvard's first **Curator of Astronomical Photographs** — the first woman given a Corporation title at Harvard.

## 4. Antonia Maury: a finer classification

**Antonia Maury** (1866–1952), a niece of Henry Draper, built a more refined spectral scheme that distinguished stars of the same letter by the *width* and appearance of their lines — in modern terms, separating **giants** from **dwarfs**. Pickering preferred Fleming's simpler letter system for the big catalogue; Maury's subtler scheme was vindicated later when Hertzsprung and Russell made luminosity classes central to stellar evolution. She left and returned more than once; the tension between speed and depth is part of the story of the plates.

## Oh Be A Fine Girl, Kiss Me

*How Annie Jump Cannon sorted the stars.*

**Annie Jump Cannon** (1863–1941) studied at Wellesley, lost much of her hearing after scarlet fever, and joined the Observatory in **1896**. She took Fleming's letters and **reordered** them into the temperature sequence still used worldwide:

> **O – B – A – F – G – K – M**

That mnemonic — the one Cannon's students and every later textbook used — is the heading of this section. It looks alphabetical. It is not. It is a **temperature** sequence: **O** hottest (blue, ≥30,000 K), **M** coolest (red, a few thousand K). Our Sun is a **G2** star. Polaris Aa, on the [Polaris](/topics/48-polaris/) page, is **F7Ib** — an F-type yellow-white *supergiant*. That "F7" is Cannon's language.

![Cannon's spectral classes O through M](../../img/Cannon_Spectral_Classes_OBAFGKM_Tufte.png?v=1)
*The Harvard spectral sequence, with rough temperatures and familiar examples. Diagram drawn for this site. (Temperatures are approximate modern ranges; the sequence itself is Cannon's.)*

**How many stars?** For the nine volumes of the **Henry Draper Catalogue** (1918–1924) she classified about **225,300** spectra. Across her lifetime, counting the *Henry Draper Extension* and later lists, the usual figure is about **350,000** (some archival tallies push toward **~400,000**). She could classify several stars a minute by eye. The International Astronomical Union adopted her system in **1922**. She became Curator of Astronomical Photographs after Fleming, received the **Henry Draper Medal** (1931) — the first woman so honored by the National Academy of Sciences — and an honorary doctorate from Oxford, but was not made a Harvard professor until **1938**, three years before her death.

## 6. Henrietta Swan Leavitt: the period–luminosity law

**Henrietta Swan Leavitt** (4 July **1868**, Lancaster, Massachusetts – 12 December **1921**, Cambridge) graduated from what became **Radcliffe College**, volunteered at the Observatory in **1895**, and took a permanent staff post in the early **1900s** (often dated **1902**). She was nearly **deaf**. Her assignment was **variable stars** — stars that brighten and fade — measured by comparing glass plates of the same field taken on different nights.

**1908.** In *Annals of Harvard College Observatory* 60: *"1777 Variables in the Magellanic Clouds,"* she listed periods for a handful of variables in the Small Magellanic Cloud and wrote the sentence that starts everything:

> **"It is worthy of notice that the brighter variables have the longer periods."**

She cautioned that the sample was still small.

**1912.** Harvard College Observatory **Circular 173** (3 March 1912), *"Periods of 25 Variable Stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud,"* opens:

> "The following statement regarding the periods of 25 variable stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud has been prepared by **Miss Leavitt**."

The circular carries **Pickering's** signature as director — the institutional form of the day — but the first sentence names her. With **25** Cepheids, all at roughly the same distance in the Small Magellanic Cloud, she plotted magnitude against the **logarithm of the period** and wrote:

> "A straight line can readily be drawn among each of the two series of points corresponding to maxima and minima, thus showing that there is a simple relation between the brightness of the variables and their periods. **The logarithm of the period increases by about 0.48 for each increase of one magnitude in brightness.**"

![Leavitt 1912 period-luminosity data](../../img/Leavitt_1912_Period_Luminosity_Relation_Tufte.png?v=1)
*All 25 stars from Table I of HCO Circular 173 (1912), replotted for this site from her published periods and provisional photographic magnitudes. Brighter is upward. The lines are least-squares fits to her table — the same relation she described as "a straight line."*

**Why this changes the universe.** Apparent brightness alone cannot tell distance (a faint star may be dim, or far, or both). But if period tells you **true** brightness, then comparing true with apparent brightness yields distance. Cepheids become **standard candles**. **Ejnar Hertzsprung** (1913) and later **Harlow Shapley** calibrated the zero point; **Edwin Hubble**, using Cepheids in the Andromeda "nebula" in **1923–1924** (announced **1924–25**), showed Andromeda lies far outside the Milky Way. The universe got vastly larger overnight. That ladder still starts with Leavitt's relation — now often called **Leavitt's Law**. Circular 173 fixed the *slope*, because the Cloud stars share a distance. It did not fix the zero point in watts or in parsecs; she said the parallaxes still had to be measured. The rungs under and above that slope, and the difference between a solar parallax and a stellar one, are [From a Venus transit to the Cepheids](/topics/52-venus-transit-to-cepheid-distances/).

**Deafness, pay, death.** Leavitt's hearing had been damaged by illness; she worked through it. She was paid a little more than the base computer rate (one institutional account says **30¢/hour**). She died of cancer in **1921**, age 53, before cosmology finished catching up to her.

**The Nobel letter that came too late.** On **23 February 1925**, the Swedish mathematician **Gösta Mittag-Leffler** wrote to her at Harvard, unaware she had been dead for three years:

> "Honoured Miss Leavitt, What my friend and colleague Professor von Zeipel of Uppsala has told me about your admirable discovery of the empirical law touching the connection between magnitude and period-length for the S Cepheid-variables of the Little Magellan's cloud, has impressed me so deeply that I feel seriously inclined to nominate you to the Nobel prize in physics for 1926…"

Shapley, by then director, replied that she was dead. Nobel Prizes are not awarded posthumously. She never received the nomination.

## 7. Cecilia Payne: stars are mostly hydrogen

**Cecilia Payne** (later **Payne-Gaposchkin**, 1900–1979) came from Cambridge, England, to Harvard on a fellowship in **1923**, under Shapley. Her **1925** Ph.D. thesis — often called the most brilliant in astronomy's history — used Cannon's spectral classes and the new quantum theory of ionization (Saha) to show that stars are composed overwhelmingly of **hydrogen** and **helium**, not of terrestrial rock-forming elements. A senior reviewer (Henry Norris Russell) urged her to soft-pedal the conclusion; she did in the printed thesis, then lived to see it become orthodoxy. In **1956** she became Harvard's first woman professor of astronomy. She is the bridge from the Computers' plates to modern astrophysics.

## 8. Later recognition

- **Dava Sobel**, *The Glass Universe* (2016): the narrative history of the plates and the women who read them.
- **DASCH** (Digital Access to a Sky Century at Harvard) and **Project PHaEDRA**: ongoing efforts to digitize the ~500,000 plates and the computers' notebooks, so a century of sky is searchable again.
- A crater on the Moon is named **Leavitt**; others honor Cannon and Fleming.
- The period–luminosity relation is increasingly called **Leavitt's Law** (a 2008 AAS resolution urged the name).

The unpaid and underpaid work that looked like "just computing" was, in Jeff's own phrase about not being left hanging, *the* entry into modern cosmology. Without Fleming's plates, Cannon's letters, and Leavitt's straight line, there is no Cepheid rung — and the [Polaris](/topics/48-polaris/) page's yardstick has nothing to stand on.

## Key facts
- **Computer** = person who computes (Brathwaite 1613; OED historical sense); first cite is God as "computer of Times"; then human calculators; machine sense mid-20th century.
- Human computers also made the British *Nautical Almanac* (Mary Edwards among them) — same world as [Bowditch](/topics/49-bowditch/).
- Edward C. Pickering, Harvard College Observatory director from 1877; women computers from early 1880s; ~80 women over the decades; ~**500,000** glass plates; Henry Draper Memorial funded by **Anna Palmer Draper**.
- Pay ~**25¢/hour** (Fleming as curator ~50¢; Leavitt reportedly ~30¢); nickname **"Pickering's Harem."**
- **Williamina Fleming**: housemaid → computer (1881) → Curator (1899); Horsehead Nebula **1888**; variables, novae, white dwarfs.
- **Antonia Maury**: finer spectral scheme distinguishing giants/dwarfs.
- **Annie Jump Cannon**: **O B A F G K M** (temperature); mnemonic *Oh Be A Fine Girl, Kiss Me*; ~**225,300** stars in the Henry Draper Catalogue, ~**350,000** in her lifetime; IAU 1922.
- **Henrietta Swan Leavitt** (1868–1921): nearly deaf; 1908 "brighter variables have the longer periods"; 1912 Circular 173, 25 SMC Cepheids, log P vs magnitude; foundation for Hubble's 1924–25 Andromeda distances; Mittag-Leffler Nobel letter **1925**, too late.
- **Cecilia Payne(-Gaposchkin)**, 1925 thesis: stars mostly **hydrogen** (and helium).
- Later: Sobel's *The Glass Universe*; DASCH / PHaEDRA plate digitization; Leavitt lunar crater; "Leavitt's Law."

## Answers
1. A **person who calculates**; **Richard Brathwaite**, *Yong Mans Gleanings* (1613), "the truest computer of Times."
2. About **25 cents an hour**; **"Pickering's Harem."**
3. **Williamina Fleming**, on a plate taken by **W. H. Pickering**, **1888**.
4. **O B A F G K M** — "Oh Be A Fine Girl, Kiss Me." Sun = **G** (G2). Polaris Aa = **F** (F7Ib).
5. 1908: brighter Magellanic variables have longer periods. 1912: a **straight-line** relation between log(period) and brightness for 25 Cepheids — period tells intrinsic luminosity.
6. If the stars are at nearly the same distance, differences in *apparent* brightness are differences in *true* brightness; period can then be matched to luminosity without knowing the absolute distance.
7. He wrote that he felt "seriously inclined" to nominate her for the **1926 Nobel Prize in Physics**; she had died in **1921**, and Nobels are not awarded posthumously.
8. That stars are composed overwhelmingly of **hydrogen** and **helium**, not Earth-like heavy elements.
