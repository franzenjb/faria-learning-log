## Milton the man
In 1660 the English monarchy came back, and Charles II hunted down the people who had defended executing his father, Charles I. John Milton had written that defense. He hid, was arrested and was released, probably thanks to friends like the poet **Andrew Marvell**. By then he was completely blind, poor and on the losing side of a revolution.

Then he wrote the greatest epic in English. He composed verses in his head at night and dictated them each morning to helpers, saying he wanted to be "milked." The poem was published in **1667 in ten books**, and in **twelve from 1674**: almost **10,600 lines of blank verse** (unrhymed iambic pentameter, Shakespeare's da-DUM beat). Milton defended dropping rhyme as freedom from

> "the troublesome and modern bondage of rhyming."

His stated aim was to

> "justify the ways of God to men."

Yet the poem opens in Hell, and its most magnetic character is Satan, the rebel who lost a war against a king.

## Satan in Book I
The rebel angels fall for nine days through Chaos onto a burning lake. Satan rises and speaks:

> "What though the field be lost? / All is not lost; the unconquerable will, / And study of revenge, immortal hate, / And courage never to submit or yield."

> **"The mind is its own place, and in itself / Can make a Heav'n of Hell, a Hell of Heav'n."** (I.254–55, Jeff's favorite)

> "Better to reign in Hell, than serve in Heav'n." (I.263)

William **Blake** said Milton was "of the Devil's party without knowing it." Percy **Shelley** thought Satan morally finer than the God he fights.

Milton's trick: the grandeur is undercut from the start. Satan's face is "intrenched" with scars, and he is "Vaunting aloud, but racked with deep despair." He shrinks across the poem: from archangel, to a cormorant perched on the Tree of Life, to a toad squatting at Eve's ear, to a serpent on his belly.

## Why twelve books, why so long
**Virgil's Aeneid has twelve books.** The 1667 edition had ten, perhaps to stand apart from Virgil's imperial poem; in 1674 Milton split two books, placing himself in Virgil's line. **Homer's** epics have 24 books each, so twelve is half a Homer.

Epic was the highest genre, and Milton took the biggest subject there is: the war in Heaven, the Creation, the Fall, and history down to the Flood and beyond. He promised

> "Things unattempted yet in prose or rhyme."

At about 10,600 lines it is still shorter than the *Iliad* (about 15,700).

## How big is it today; the audiobook
- The poem alone runs about **300–350 paperback pages**; annotated editions (Penguin, Norton Critical) run about **450–650 pages**.
- Read aloud it takes about **11–12 hours**. For scale, *The Count of Monte Cristo* is over 1,200 pages.
- **Why listen:** Milton was blind and composed by ear, so the sound *is* the design. His long sentences roll over the line endings.
- **Best approach:** listen while following the printed text.
- **Recordings:** Anton Lesser (Naxos, unabridged, about 12 hours). On Audible, Jeff chose **Simon Vance**.

## Did Milton know his success?
He sold it in 1667 for **£5 down**, with £5 more after the first edition of 1,300 copies sold, which took about two years. His widow later settled the rights for **£8**. He lived to see the 1674 twelve-book edition and died in November 1674, aged 65.

John **Dryden** reportedly said:

> "This man cuts us all out, and the ancients too."

Dryden asked to turn it into a rhymed opera; Milton reportedly replied, "Ay, you may tag my verses." Within a generation the poem was treated as England's national epic.

## Milton's cosmos
**Heaven** (the Empyrean) is at the top. Our starry **World** hangs from it by a **golden chain** inside **Chaos**, "the vast immeasurable abyss," with **Hell** far below, its gates kept by **Sin and Death**. After the Fall, Sin and Death build a **bridge across Chaos** from Hell to our world.

## What the churches say
- **No pope has ever ruled on it**, and no church has officially endorsed or banned it.
- Milton was fiercely **anti-Catholic**: in Book III's "Paradise of Fools," monks, relics and papal bulls are blown about by the wind.
- His **prose works** went onto the Vatican's Index of Forbidden Books. *Paradise Lost* itself is often reported as listed, but that is **uncertain**.
- Catholic critics today admire the art and reject the theology.
- For 200 years Protestant England treated it almost as a second Bible. Much of the popular picture of Satan's rebellion and the war in Heaven comes **from Milton, not Scripture**.
- **Heresies:** *On Christian Doctrine* (found in 1823) denies the Trinity (the Son is lesser than the Father), argues for divorce, and rejects creation from nothing.
- C. S. **Lewis**, *A Preface to Paradise Lost* (1942), argued Satan is meant to be absurd, and that readers cheering him are fooled as Eve was.

## "God the Father turns a School-Divine"
The line is Alexander **Pope's**, from *Imitations of Horace* (1737). A *school divine* is a scholastic theologian who argues by dry logic. When God speaks in Book III, explaining free will and why the Fall isn't his fault, he sounds like a professor defending a thesis:

> "I made him just and right, / Sufficient to have stood, though free to fall."

Satan gets the thrilling speeches; God gets lecture notes. Milton set out to justify God's ways to men, and in Pope's view he made God sound like a man justifying himself.

## How it ends
It ends quietly, not with thunder. In Books XI and XII the archangel Michael shows Adam the future: Cain killing Abel, the Flood, and finally the coming of Christ. Adam is consoled that good will come out of evil. Then Michael leads Adam and Eve out of Eden by the hand, while behind them the flaming sword of the cherubim guards the gate.

These are the last lines, among the most beautiful in English:

> **Some natural tears they dropped, but wiped them soon;**
> **The world was all before them, where to choose**
> **Their place of rest, and Providence their guide:**
> **They hand in hand with wandering steps and slow,**
> **Through Eden took their solitary way.**

Notice the mix. They are exiled and alone, "solitary," yet they walk "hand in hand." It's grief, but also freedom. "The world was all before them" sounds almost like a new beginning. The epic that opened in Hell closes with two people walking into our world, which is where every one of us begins.
