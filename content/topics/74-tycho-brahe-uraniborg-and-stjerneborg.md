This page was written for the learning log from the published sources named below. It is a researched account, not a transcript of a conversation. It does not put words in Tycho's mouth.

Tyge Brahe, who signed the Latin form Tycho, was born on 14 December 1546 at Knutstorp in Scania. Scania was then Danish. It is now in Sweden. He died in Prague. The Danish biographical lexicon prints the death as 14/24 October 1601, the older calendar and the newer one side by side.

His father was Otte Brahe. His mother was Beate Bille. The uncle who raised him was Jørgen Brahe of Tosterup, Otte's brother, so a paternal uncle, and Jørgen's wife Inger Oxe. The lexicon says the child was taken at about one year old and brought up as Jørgen's own, Jørgen's marriage having no children. That is one boy moved to one uncle. It is not a claim that the rest of the brothers and sisters left home, and it is not a claim that Otte and Jørgen were the same man.

Jørgen died in Copenhagen on 21 June 1565. The lexicon's account is an accident in the harbour. Frederick II had been out with some noblemen to inspect the fleet that had come home. On the way back, the king, Jørgen, and others were thrown from the ship. They were pulled out. Jørgen did not survive the consequences. A popular story instead has the king fall into a canal after drinking, and Jørgen jump in and take a fever. That is a different telling. This page follows the lexicon.

## The duel

On 29 December 1566, in Rostock, Tycho fought Manderup Parsberg with swords. The lexicon calls Parsberg his countryman. Tycho lost part of his nose and afterward wore a prosthesis. This page does not describe the metal. Later laboratory reports exist, and they are not a sentence from 1566.

The quarrel did not make him an astronomer by itself. He had already been watching the sky, including the great conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn in 1563, and finding the printed tables off by days. After Jørgen's death he could study openly what a noble household had treated as an odd hobby.

## The island

In 1576 Frederick II granted him the island of Hven in the Sound, for life, with money to build. The foundation stone of Uraniborg was laid on 8 August 1576 by Charles Dançay, the French envoy. Tycho says the house was begun that year. The lexicon says the symmetrical building, with a laboratory cellar, rooms, a library, and observing rooms, stood finished in 1580. Hven was Danish. The island is now Ven, in Sweden, in Landskrona municipality. The Danish name and the Swedish name are the same place in two centuries.

About 1584, on a hill outside the castle, he built Stjerneborg, a subterranean observatory of several cellars, so that large instruments could stand fixed and out of the wind, and so that two teams could observe without leaning over each other's shoulders. He left the island in April 1597, after the new reign cut his income and the complaints had piled up. He died in Prague, as the date above says.

The [Galileo moons lesson](/topics/64-galileo-jupiters-moons/) names his world system in one sentence: the planets go around the Sun, and the Sun goes around the Earth. It fitted the moons of Jupiter without requiring the Earth to move. The new star of 1572, and the comet of 1577 moving among the planets rather than under the Moon, were his blows against the hard celestial spheres. Picard, a century later, went to the island to tie Tycho's place to the Paris observatory. That is the [meridian lesson](/topics/73-picard-triangulation-1669/).

## The radius, and the two seventies

A quadrant is a quarter of a circle, 90 degrees. The centre is the corner where the two straight edges meet. The radius is the distance from that centre out to the divided arc. Altitude is the angle of a star above the horizon. The zenith is straight up. Zenith distance is 90 degrees minus the altitude. If a quadrant reads 37 degrees of altitude, the zenith distance is 53 degrees. That subtraction is the definition. It is not a measurement Tycho logged on a particular night.

The large steel quadrant at Stjerneborg, the one set inside a steel square, measures nearly five cubits from the centre to the circumference. The English translation of *Astronomiae instauratae mechanica* converts that to 194 centimetres, about 1.94 metres. That length is the radius. It is not the height of the instrument, and it is not the depth of the cellar it stood in. Each degree on the arc is divided into minutes, and each minute into six parts, so the instrument is meant to show 10 arcseconds. An arcminute is a sixtieth of a degree. An arcsecond is a sixtieth of an arcminute.

![A quarter circle with the radius drawn from the centre to the arc](../../img/Tycho_Steel_Quadrant_Radius_Teaching_Sketch.svg)
*The marked line is the radius, about 1.94 metres. The arc is 90 degrees. Nothing in the sketch is a pit.*

How long is that arc? A full circle is two times π times the radius. π is about 3.1416, so two times π is about 6.2832, and a quarter of the circle is a quarter of that, about 1.5708 times the radius. Times 1.94 metres, the quarter arc is about 3.05 metres. One degree along it is 3.05 divided by 90, about 34 millimetres. One arcminute is a sixtieth of that, about 0.56 millimetres. A sixth of a minute, the 10-arcsecond mark, is about 0.09 millimetres along the brass. Those are lengths on the scale. They are not a height.

Two different seventies sit near each other in the same book, and they are not depths.

The outer premises of Stjerneborg, gallery included, measure 70 feet on each side of the square that faces the four points of the sky. That is a horizontal side of a ground plan. The semicircles in the middle of those sides have a diameter of 24 feet, also a plan measure. The translator takes Tycho's foot at about 25.9 centimetres, so 70 feet is about 18 metres. Seventy times 0.259 is 18.1. The 18 metres is that conversion. It is not a depth Tycho printed, and it is not the radius of the quadrant.

Separately, he places the hill of Stjerneborg about seventy small passus south of the castle wall. A passus is a pace, and in his usage a geometric pace is several feet. That seventy is a distance across the ground from the wall to the hill. It is not the 70-foot side, and it is not how deep the cellars were dug.

This page does not give a ten-foot square. The description checked here does not use that figure for these parts. Inventing one would only add a third length to mix up.

Tycho prints the polar altitude at the castle, which is the latitude, as 55 degrees 54 and a half arcminutes. That is 55 plus 54.5 divided by 60, or 55.908 degrees. A modern map puts Ven near 55.9 degrees north. His figure is his own measurement. It is not a satellite coordinate.

## The museum now

The Tycho Brahe Museum stands on Ven, at Landsvägen 182. For 2026 the municipal page of Landskrona gives the regular hours as 1 May through 31 August, 09:30 to 16:00, and weekends in September, 09:30 to 14:00. That same page states one exception inside the summer run: closed on Midsummer Eve. This lesson does not add any other closure. A different tourism page lists October weekends as well. The two public calendars do not match, so the hours above are the municipal page, not a blend. Neither page states when the museum opens in 2027. That date is not known here.

## Key facts
- Born 14 December 1546 at Knutstorp in Scania, then Denmark. Died in Prague; the Danish lexicon prints 14/24 October 1601.
- Raised by his paternal uncle Jørgen Brahe. Jørgen died 21 June 1565 after the harbour accident recorded in the lexicon.
- 29 December 1566, in Rostock, a sword fight with Manderup Parsberg cost him part of his nose.
- Uraniborg's foundation stone was laid 8 August 1576. Stjerneborg is the cellar observatory of about 1584, on Hven, now Ven in Sweden.
- The steel quadrant's nearly five cubits, about 1.94 metres, is the radius. The 70 feet is the side of the outer square, about 18 metres by the translator's foot, not a pit depth.
