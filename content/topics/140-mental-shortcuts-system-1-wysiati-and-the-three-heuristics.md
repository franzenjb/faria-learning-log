The mind does not work through every problem from scratch. It uses shortcuts. In 1974, Tversky and Kahneman called those shortcuts heuristics, and showed that they are usually efficient and also lead to systematic errors. A fast answer is not a wrong answer by default. That is the same caution already on [the note of one evening](../85-kahneman-tversky-the-first-evening/), with the bat-and-ball check.

This page is the map of the shortcuts. The next page, [probability traps](../141-probability-traps-linda-base-rates-and-regression/), is where those shortcuts meet chances and counts.

## System 1 and System 2

In *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011), Kahneman describes two ways of thinking. **System 1** is fast, automatic, and often effortless. It offers a first answer. **System 2** is slower and more deliberate. It can check that answer, or fail to.

Kahneman did not invent the labels. Keith Stanovich and Richard West proposed "System 1" and "System 2" in 2000. Kahneman adopted them because they were already in use and easy to keep straight. Nothing in this account says that a person has two brains. It says that a first answer can arrive on its own, and that a slower pass can test it.

The bat-and-ball puzzle is a small picture of the two. A bat and a ball cost $1.10 together. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. Ten cents arrives quickly. It does not survive a check. Five cents does.

Spot it: Notice the first answer, then ask whether you have tested it.

Self-test: Before you lock in a figure, can you say what would make that figure fail?

## WYSIATI

WYSIATI is Kahneman's phrase from the 2011 book: "what you see is all there is." The mind builds a coherent story from the information in view, and it is not naturally designed to ask what is missing. A clear story feels true, even when the file is thin.

Example: You hear one vivid anecdote about a school, a town, or a colleague, and the story feels complete. You may not notice that you have no base rate, no comparison, and no account of the cases you did not hear.

Spot it: Ask, "What would I need to see that I have not seen?"

Self-test: Name one fact that, if it came to light, would change the story. If you cannot, you may be treating what is in view as if it were the whole.

False dilemma and straw man live here too: only the options, or the substitute, are in view. See the [fallacies page](../139-fourteen-classic-fallacies/).

## Anchoring

You start from a number that is already on the table, and you do not move far enough away from it, even when the number is random.

In the 1974 *Science* paper, people spun a wheel of fortune that was rigged to stop at 10 or at 65, and were then asked for the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. Those who saw 10 gave a median estimate of 25. Those who saw 65 gave a median estimate of 45. The wheel could not have been information. It still pulled the answers.

Example: A first offer in a negotiation, a first budget number on a slide, or a first guess typed into a form can pull the final figure toward itself.

Spot it: Ask, "Where did that starting number come from, and how far did I actually move?"

Self-test: Hide the first number and estimate again. If your new figure is very different, the first number was doing work it did not earn.

## Availability

You judge how common or how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind.

Tversky and Kahneman developed this idea in a 1973 paper in *Cognitive Psychology*, and restated it in 1974. Dramatic, recent, or heavily reported events come to mind easily. Quiet, regular events do not. Ease of recall is then mistaken for frequency.

Example: After a plane crash is in the news, flying can feel more dangerous that week, even if the year's risk has not changed. A flood of news is not the same thing as a flood of cases.

Spot it: Ask, "Is this easy to recall because it is common, or because it is vivid?"

Self-test: Try to name three dull counterexamples. If they are hard to think of, availability may be running the estimate.

Bandwagon and red herring borrow this tilt: what is popular, or what is vivid, crowds out the quieter evidence. See the [fallacies page](../139-fourteen-classic-fallacies/).

## Representativeness

You judge the chance of a category, or of a cause, by how much the case resembles a stereotype of that category.

In the 1974 paper, this is the master shortcut behind several of the probability traps on the [next page](../141-probability-traps-linda-base-rates-and-regression/): Linda, base-rate neglect, the law of small numbers, and the gambler's fallacy. A description that *looks like* a feminist activist makes "feminist bank teller" feel more likely than "bank teller," even though every feminist bank teller is already a bank teller.

Example: A quiet, tidy person is judged more likely to be a librarian than a salesperson, even in a world with many more salespeople than librarians. The resemblance is real. The base rate still matters.

Spot it: Ask, "Does this match a picture in my head, and have I also asked how common that picture really is?"

Self-test: Before you name the category, ask how many people are in it. If you never asked, representativeness was probably doing the work alone.

Appeal to nature belongs here as well: what seems typical or "pure" feels right.

## Two Questions

1. After a dramatic news story, do you find yourself revising a risk — flying, a storm, a market — without any new count of how often it actually happens?
2. Think of the last number you accepted in a plan or a negotiation. What was the first number on the table, and how far did you move?

## Key facts
- System 1 is fast and automatic. System 2 is slower and can check a first answer. The labels come from Stanovich and West (2000). Kahneman adopted them in 2011. A person does not have two brains.
- WYSIATI, "what you see is all there is," is Kahneman's phrase in *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. A coherent story can feel true even when the file is thin.
- In the 1974 wheel-of-fortune study, a rigged 10 pulled the median estimate of African countries in the U.N. to 25; a rigged 65 pulled it to 45.
- Availability judges likelihood by ease of recall. Representativeness judges likelihood by resemblance to a stereotype. Both are usually useful, and both skip information that is not in the picture.
- A fast answer is not a wrong answer by default. Ten cents in the bat-and-ball puzzle is a fast answer that fails a check.
