## Star sailor
**Astronaut** is Greek *astron* (star) + *nautēs* (sailor). So an astronaut is a "star sailor." The Soviet word **cosmonaut** is *kosmos* (the ordered universe) + *nautēs*, a "universe sailor."

## The sailor family
Greek *naus* means ship, and *nautēs* is the person who sails it. Latin borrowed the same idea as *navis* (ship). Relatives:

- **Nautical**: to do with sailors and ships
- **Nautical mile**: the sailor's mile, one minute of latitude (about 1.15 ordinary miles)
- **Navy**, **naval**, **navigate**: from Latin *navis*
- **Nausea**: literally seasickness, from *naus*
- **Argonauts**: the sailors of the ship *Argo* in the Jason myth
- **Nautilus**: see below

## Naut- yes, but what is -tilus?
*Nautilus* is the Greek *nautilos*, "sailor," built on the same *naut-* root. The *-ilos* ending just makes it a noun, so there is no separate "tilus" word. The Greeks gave the name to the sea creature with the coiled shell (the chambered nautilus, and the paper nautilus), because they imagined it sailing on the surface.

Robert Fulton borrowed the name for his 1800 submarine, and Jules Verne borrowed it again for Captain Nemo's submarine. See [Fulton's Nautilus](../13-fulton-nautilus-1800/).

## Key facts
- Astronaut: Greek astron (star) + nautēs (sailor) = star sailor
- Nausea literally means seasickness (Greek naus, ship)
- Nautilus: Greek nautilos, sailor; no separate "tilus" root
