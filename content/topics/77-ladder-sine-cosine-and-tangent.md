This page follows the six-page ladder lesson. The numbers are a paper example. They are not instructions for setting a real ladder.

Picture a ladder against a straight wall on level ground. The ladder, the wall, and the ground make a triangle. The same triangle is used throughout.

Trigonometry connects the angles of a triangle with the lengths of its sides. This page does not need calculus.

![A right triangle: 20-foot ladder, 10-foot height, base about 17.32 feet, 30 degrees at the ground](../../img/Ladder_Sine_Cosine_Tangent_Teaching_Sketch.svg)
*Paper triangle. The square corner is where the wall meets the ground.*

A right triangle has one square corner, 90 degrees. Here that corner is at the foot of the wall. The hypotenuse, pronounced hy-POT-uh-noos, is the side across from the square corner. It is the longest side. Here it is the ladder, 20 feet. An angle measures how wide two lines open. The ground angle, from the ground up to the ladder, is 30 degrees. The sign ° means degrees.

Relative to that ground angle, the height is the opposite side: it sits across from the angle. The base is the adjacent side: it touches the angle and is not the hypotenuse.

## Sine: how high

Sine, pronounced like "sign," is the height divided by the ladder length. A ratio is one length compared with another by division. It is not itself a height.

For 30 degrees, the height is half the ladder. A calculator writes sin(30°) = 0.5. The short name sin means sine. So the height is 20 feet times 0.5, which is 10 feet. The sine is the ratio 0.5. Multiplying by the ladder turns the ratio into a length.

That half is not a mysterious calculator fact. An equilateral triangle has three equal sides and three 60-degree angles. Split one with a 20-foot side down the middle and you get two right triangles. The 60-degree top angle becomes two 30-degree angles, and the base becomes two 10-foot pieces. The side opposite 30 degrees is 10 feet. The hypotenuse is still 20. Ten divided by 20 is one half. Turn that half-triangle over and it is the ladder.

## Cosine: how far out

Cosine, pronounced "co-sign," is the base divided by the ladder. For 30 degrees a calculator gives about 0.866. The short name is cos. The base is 20 times 0.866, about 17.32 feet. The sign ≈ means approximately equal. The 0.866 is rounded.

## Tangent: height from the base

Tangent, shortened to tan, is the height divided by the base. If you know the ground angle and the distance from the wall, and you do not know the ladder, tangent still reaches the height. For 30 degrees, tan is about 0.577. Every foot along the ground goes with about 0.577 feet of rise. So 17.32 times 0.577 is about 10 feet again.

The ratios stay put when the triangle grows and the angle does not. A 40-foot ladder at 30 degrees reaches 20 feet. Double the size, keep the shape. 10 divided by 20 equals 20 divided by 40, and both equal 0.5. Change the angle and the ratios change.

On a calculator, choose degrees, marked DEG, not radians. A radian is another unit for the same opening. sin, cos, and tan then take 30 as 30 degrees.

## A shadow, and what Galileo did instead

Replace the wall with a mountain and the base with its shadow on level ground. A ray of sunlight takes the place of the ladder. The Sun's elevation is its angle above the local horizon, the level direction at that place. Keep 30 degrees as the example.

Height equals shadow length times the tangent of the Sun's angle. A shadow of 1,000 feet and a 30-degree Sun give about 1,000 times 0.577, which is about 577 feet. That example is invented. It is not Galileo's data. The same idea can be tried on the Moon if you have the true ground length of the shadow, not an uncorrected length in a photograph, and the Sun's angle at that place. Level ground and parallel rays are assumptions. Slopes, the Moon's curve, and the viewing angle ask for corrections. A popular account of measuring craters states those limits.

In 1610 Galileo described bright mountaintops shining inside the Moon's dark region, the way a peak on Earth can catch sunrise while the valley is still dark. He measured how far those bright peaks lay beyond the terminator, the boundary between lunar day and night, and compared that distance with the Moon's diameter, the full width through the center. His published calculation used the Moon's curved shape and a right triangle, and Pythagoras's rule for the sides, to find how high a peak had to rise to meet the sunlight. That is not the shadow-times-tangent sum on this page. Both turn light and a triangle into a height. They are different methods.

He reported a height of more than 4 Italian miles. Those are historical Italian miles. They are not automatically four English miles. The figure is his estimate, not a modern survey. The English translation of *Sidereus Nuncius* by E. S. Carlos (1880), pages 28 to 30, is the place to read the argument.

## Key facts
- Sine is height divided by the ladder. At 30 degrees that ratio is 1/2, so a 20-foot ladder reaches 10 feet.
- Cosine is base divided by the ladder. At 30 degrees the base is about 17.32 feet.
- Tangent is height divided by the base. At 30 degrees it is about 0.577. It is not the tangent line of a graph.
- Galileo's peak height used the Moon's curve and Pythagoras. It is not the shadow-times-tangent example, and "more than 4 Italian miles" is not four English miles.
