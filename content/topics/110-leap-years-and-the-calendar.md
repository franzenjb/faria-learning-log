1900 was not a leap year. 2000 was. 2100 will not be. The reason is a rule most people never learn, and behind it is a 2,000-year struggle to make a calendar of whole days fit a year that is not a whole number of days. The same struggle may also be why a circle has 360 degrees.

## The Rule

In our calendar, the Gregorian calendar:

- A year divisible by **4** is a leap year: 2024, 2028.
- **Except** a year divisible by **100** is not: 1700, 1800, 1900.
- **Except** a year divisible by **400** is a leap year after all: 1600, 2000.

So in every 400 years there are 97 leap days, not 100. That makes the average year 365.2425 days long. The year of the seasons, the tropical year, is about 365.2422 days. The two differ by about one day in roughly 3,000 years.

## A Year of 360 Days, and a Circle of 360 Degrees

Early calendars often used a tidy, schematic year of twelve 30-day months, 360 days. Babylonian astronomical texts used such an ideal year for reckoning. If the Sun takes about 360 days to go once around the sky, it moves about one step a day. One step a day, 360 steps to a circle.

That is the most common proposed origin of 360 degrees. It is a likely story, not a proven one. Base 60 also matters. The Babylonians counted in sixties, and 360 is six times 60. A circle also splits neatly into six equal triangles, each with a 60° corner. No surviving text says which reason came first, and historians generally say the true origin is unknown. Base 60 is also why a degree has 60 minutes, as on [Degrees, Arcminutes and Arcseconds](../95-degrees-arcminutes-arcseconds/).

## Egypt: 365 Days, Exactly

A 360-day year falls behind the seasons by five days every year. The Egyptian civil calendar fixed most of that. It had twelve months of 30 days and five extra days at the end, 365 days in all, every year.

But the true year is about a quarter day longer. With no leap day, the Egyptian calendar slipped one day every four years. Over about 1,460 years, its New Year wandered all the way around the seasons.

## Julius Caesar, 46 BC

Rome's calendar was a mess. Its officials added extra months by judgment, and sometimes by politics. By Caesar's time it was months out of step with the seasons.

Julius Caesar, advised by the astronomer Sosigenes of Alexandria, fixed it. To catch up, 46 BC was stretched to **445 days**, later called the year of confusion. From 45 BC the new **Julian calendar** gave a 365-day year with one leap day every four years. Average: 365.25 days.

That is about 11 minutes too long each year. It sounds small. But it adds up to about one day every 128 years.

## Pope Gregory, 1582

By the 1500s the Julian calendar had drifted about ten days. The spring equinox, which sets the date of Easter, was falling around 11 March instead of near 21 March, where it had been in AD 325.

In 1582 Pope Gregory XIII issued a decree, *Inter gravissimas*. It did two things.

- It dropped ten days. In 1582, **Thursday 4 October** was followed by **Friday 15 October**. The days of the week did not skip. Only the numbers did.
- It added the 100 and 400 rule, so the drift would not return.

Catholic countries changed within a few years. Protestant and Orthodox countries were slower.

## Britain and Its Colonies, 1752

Britain, and its American colonies, waited 170 years. By then the gap was **eleven** days, because 1700 had been a leap year in the Julian calendar but not in the Gregorian. Under the Calendar (New Style) Act of 1750, **Wednesday 2 September 1752** was followed by **Thursday 14 September 1752**. The same law moved the start of the legal year from 25 March to 1 January.

That is why George Washington, born 11 February 1731 in the old style, celebrated his birthday on 22 February 1732 in the new.

## "Give Us Our Eleven Days"

A famous story says crowds rioted, shouting "Give us our eleven days!", believing their lives had been shortened. The historian Robert Poole traced the story back. He found very thin evidence. The slogan appears on a banner in William Hogarth's painting *An Election Entertainment* of 1755, a satire of an Oxfordshire election, and the calendar was an election issue there. Later writers took the picture as proof of riots. Real grumbling and political jokes there were. Mass calendar riots, historians now say, are largely a myth.

## So, 1900

1900 is divisible by 4 and by 100, but not by 400. In the Gregorian calendar it was not a leap year. There was no 29 February 1900. In countries still on the Julian calendar, such as Russia until 1918, there was one, which widened their gap to 13 days.

One modern echo: Microsoft Excel treats 1900 as a leap year, a choice made long ago to match the older Lotus 1-2-3 spreadsheet. The spreadsheet is wrong. The calendar is not.

## Key facts
- Leap rule: divisible by 4, except by 100, unless by 400. 1900 was not a leap year; 2000 was; 2100 will not be.
- Gregorian average year: 365.2425 days, against a tropical year of about 365.2422.
- 360 degrees likely comes from a schematic 360-day year, with Babylonian base 60 also a factor. The true origin is not known.
- The Egyptian civil year was 12 months of 30 days plus 5 extra days: 365, with no leap day.
- Caesar's reform: 46 BC ran 445 days; the Julian calendar, from 45 BC, added a leap day every 4 years.
- 1582: Thursday 4 October was followed by Friday 15 October.
- Britain and its colonies, 1752: Wednesday 2 September was followed by Thursday 14 September.
- The "give us our eleven days" riots are largely a myth, traced mostly to a Hogarth painting of 1755.
