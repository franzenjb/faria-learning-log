## The rule

Daniel Kahneman found that we don't remember an experience as the average of every moment. We judge it mostly by two moments: the most intense one (the peak) and how it ended (the end). How long it lasted barely matters. That second fact is called duration neglect.

In 1993, Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber, and Redelmeier asked people to hold a hand in painfully cold water, at 14 °C, for 60 seconds. In a second trial they did the same 60 seconds, then 30 more seconds while the water was gradually warmed to 15 °C, still painful but less so. The longer trial meant more total pain, yet 22 of 32 people (69%) chose to repeat it, because it ended less badly. In a replication without moment-by-moment ratings, 24 of 37 made the same choice.

In 1996, Redelmeier and Kahneman recorded pain during colonoscopies (154 patients) and, in the same paper, lithotripsies (133). Patients later rated the procedure by the worst moment and by the pain of the last three minutes, not by how long it lasted.

A 2003 randomized trial by Redelmeier, Katz, and Kahneman tested a gentler finish. Of 682 colonoscopy patients, half had a short interval added at the end, with the instrument left still for up to three minutes. Those patients rated the final moments as less painful (1.7 versus 2.5 on a ten-point scale) and the whole experience as less unpleasant (4.4 versus 4.9). Over a median 5.3 years of follow-up they were somewhat more likely to return for another colonoscopy (odds ratio 1.41).

In *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011), Kahneman distinguishes the experiencing self, which answers "Does it hurt now?", from the remembering self, which answers "How was it, on the whole?" The remembering self keeps score and makes future choices.

## The speech: Dorie Clark's last word technique

The idea came up after an [Instagram reel by Dorie Clark](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DfLFl_SKghz/). In the reel she says she has given more than 500 keynotes over the past decade for companies such as Google, FedEx, and Sony.

Never end a talk with "So, any questions?" You risk closing on a random or inappropriate question, or on silence.

Because of the peak-end rule, the close is disproportionately what people remember.

What to do instead: near the end, say "We have time for a few questions and then I'll wrap up." Take the questions. Then finish with a close you wrote ahead of time, either one last story or a line like "If you remember one thing from today, make it this: …"

You decide how the talk ends. Even if questions wander, you bring everyone back to your main point.

Jeff's standing rule is that every talk he gives ends with a pre-written last word, never with Q&A.

## The shelter: applying peak-end to Red Cross shelters

This section is Jeff's own application of Kahneman's research, not a published study of shelters.

Residents will remember a stay by its worst moment and by how they left, not by the average day.

Soften the peak:

- The first hour: people arrive wet, scared, and lost. Give them a warm, fast welcome with a name, a cot, a phone charger, and a hot drink.
- The worst night: noise, cold, a fight, or no news. Set quiet hours, keep a staff member visible overnight, and post a news update at the same time every day.
- Losing control: offer small choices, such as which cot, which meal, or what color blanket.

Own the end:

- Don't let people just drift out when the shelter closes. Give each household a planned send-off: a caseworker check-in, next steps written down, a recovery or resource packet, and a goodbye by name.
- End on something hopeful, like "Here's who will call you Tuesday."

The takeaway: in how families remember the Red Cross, one bad night or a cold, chaotic closing can outweigh a week of good care.

## The last moment

A speaker and a shelter manager have the same job at the end: design the last moment instead of letting it happen.
