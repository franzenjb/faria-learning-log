These are moves in an argument. Some have close cousins among the [Kahneman and Tversky biases](../138-logical-fallacies-and-kahneman-tversky-biases/). Others are mainly debate tricks. Naming a fallacy helps when it reveals the move, not when it ends the talk. Each entry has a definition, an example, a way to spot it, and a self-test.

## Straw Man

You knock down a weaker copy of the other person's view, not the view itself.

Example: "We should tighten the requirements for this license." Reply: "So you want to ban ordinary people from doing their jobs."

Spot it: Ask, "Did they actually claim that?"

Self-test: Restate their view in a form they would accept. If you cannot, you may be holding a straw man.

A cousin of WYSIATI: a substitute is easier to hold than the real claim. See [mental shortcuts](../140-mental-shortcuts-system-1-wysiati-and-the-three-heuristics/).

## Ad Hominem

You attack the person instead of the claim.

Example: "Don't listen to her budget numbers. She just wants the promotion." The numbers may still be right, or wrong, on their own.

Spot it: Ask, "If someone I liked said the same thing, would I still dismiss it?"

Self-test: Set the speaker aside and score only the evidence.

The cousin is the [halo effect](../142-risk-frames-and-later-judgment/): a feeling about the person spills onto the claim.

## Appeal to Authority

You treat a name, a title, or a famous institution as if it settled the question.

Example: "A well-known consultant says this plan will work, so it will." The consultant may be expert in the wrong field, or simply wrong this time.

Spot it: Ask, "Are they expert on this exact question, and what is the evidence besides the name?"

Self-test: Remove the name. Does the argument still stand?

A title can throw a halo over a weak claim. Expertise is real; a name is not a proof.

## False Dilemma

You offer two options as if they were the only ones.

Example: "Either we cut the program entirely, or we leave it untouched." A smaller change, a delay, or a test may also be possible.

Spot it: Ask, "What is a third way?"

Self-test: Write two more options, even if you would not choose them.

The cousin is WYSIATI: only the options in view feel real.

## Slippery Slope

You say that one step will, by an unbroken chain, lead to a dire end, without showing why the chain has to hold.

Example: "If we allow this exception, soon there will be no rule left at all." Maybe. Or maybe this is a narrow exception with a clear limit.

Spot it: Ask, "What would have to happen at each step, and how likely is each step?"

Self-test: Break the chain into three links. If any link is only a fear, the slope is not yet an argument.

This is mainly a debate trick. The 1974 paper does not treat it as a named bias.

## Begging the Question

You assume, in your reason, the very thing you are trying to prove. The phrase does not mean "this raises a question."

Example: "This policy is fair because it treats people fairly." The conclusion is already sitting in the premise.

Spot it: Ask, "Did the reason sneak in the conclusion under other words?"

Self-test: Cross out any wording that already means "therefore I am right." If nothing is left, the question was begged.

This is a logic error, not a Kahneman and Tversky finding.

## Post Hoc

Full name: *post hoc, ergo propter hoc* — "after this, therefore because of this." You treat what came first as the cause of what came next.

Example: "We launched the new slogan on Monday, and sales rose on Tuesday, so the slogan worked." Tuesday may have been a gift, not a proof.

Spot it: Ask, "What else changed, and what would we have seen without this?"

Self-test: Name two other causes that could have produced the same result.

The cousin is seeing a cause after the fact: a tidy story feels like an explanation.

## Hasty Generalization

You draw a wide conclusion from a small, or badly chosen, handful of cases.

Example: "I met two rude clerks in that office, so the whole office is rude." Two people are not the office.

Spot it: Ask, "How many cases, and were they picked fairly?"

Self-test: If the sample were three times as large, and mixed, would you still say the same thing?

The close cousin is the [law of small numbers](../141-probability-traps-linda-base-rates-and-regression/).

## Red Herring

You drag the talk onto a side issue that is easier, or hotter, than the question at hand.

Example: "We can discuss the delay later. Have you seen how hard the team has been working?" Effort may be real, and it does not answer whether the work is late.

Spot it: Ask, "What was the original question, and are we still on it?"

Self-test: Write the original question in one sentence. If the last remark does not touch it, it is a herring.

Attention follows what is vivid: availability, at work in a conversation.

## Tu Quoque

"You too." You answer a charge by pointing at the other person's similar fault, as if that settled the charge.

Example: "You say I missed the deadline, but you missed one last spring." Both can be true. The second does not erase the first.

Spot it: Ask, "Even if they did it too, is my part still true?"

Self-test: Grant their fault for a moment. Does yours remain?

A debate trick, a cousin of ad hominem, not a named finding in the 1974 paper.

## No True Scotsman

You protect a sweeping claim by shrinking the group after a counterexample appears.

Example: "No engineer would miss that check." "Dana is an engineer, and Dana missed it." "Well, no *true* engineer would." The claim is saved by moving the fence.

Spot it: Ask, "Did the definition just change to rescue the claim?"

Self-test: State the rule so a real counterexample could still count. If it cannot, the Scotsman is at work.

A definition move, not a Kahneman and Tversky bias.

## Bandwagon

You treat popularity as proof.

Example: "Everyone is switching to this tool, so it must be the right one." A crowd can be early, late, or simply wrong.

Spot it: Ask, "If almost no one had adopted it yet, would the evidence look any different?"

Self-test: List the reasons that do not mention other people. If the list is empty, you are on a bandwagon.

What is popular is easy to recall. Availability can make a crowd feel like evidence.

## Appeal to Nature

You treat "natural" as if it meant "good" or "safe," and "unnatural" as if it meant "bad."

Example: "This remedy is natural, so it is safer than the medicine." Poison ivy is natural. Many useful medicines are made in a lab.

Spot it: Ask, "What is the evidence of benefit or harm, aside from the word *natural*?"

Self-test: Replace "natural" with "typical of this category." If the argument collapses, the word was doing the work.

The cousin is representativeness: what seems typical or pure feels right.

## The Texas Sharpshooter

You fire first, then draw the bull's-eye around the tightest cluster of holes, and call yourself a sharpshooter.

Example: A report looks at twenty possible patterns, finds two that look striking, and writes the story as if those two had been the question all along.

Spot it: Ask, "Was this pattern predicted before the data were seen, or found afterward?"

Self-test: If you had to name the target before looking, would you have named this one?

The cousin is seeing patterns in randomness, close to the law of small numbers. A cluster can be luck with a circle drawn around it.

## Two Questions

1. Which of these do you hear most often — in a meeting, or in your own head?
2. Take the last disagreement you remember. Was it a straw man, a false dilemma, a red herring, or something else?

## Key facts
- A fallacy is a move in an argument. Naming it is useful when it reveals the move, not when it ends the talk.
- "Begging the question" means assuming what you set out to prove. It does not mean "this raises a question."
- Hasty generalization is the close cousin of the law of small numbers. The Texas sharpshooter is the close cousin of seeing patterns in randomness.
- Tu quoque, no true Scotsman, begging the question, and slippery slope are mainly debate tricks, with no close named cousin in the 1974 heuristics paper.
