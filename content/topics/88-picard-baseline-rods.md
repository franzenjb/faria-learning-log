[Picard](../73-picard-triangulation-1669/) chained triangles from Malvoisine to Sourdon in 1669 and 1670 to measure a degree of the meridian. Every later side in that network depends on one first length, the baseline.

He chose a straight paved road south of Paris, from the middle of the Villejuif windmill to the nearest corner of the Juvisy pavilion. He measured it with wooden rods.

The rods were four straight wooden pike shafts, each 2 toises long, about 12.8 feet. He screwed them together in pairs at the thick end, making two measuring bars, each 4 toises, about 25.6 feet. Copper plates on the ends let one bar butt cleanly against the next.

A long cord was pulled tight along the road. Each time the cord was moved, it was aimed again at both ends. One bar always stayed on the ground, so the line never lost its place.

The length came out 5,663 toises, about 6.9 miles, or 11.04 kilometres. That single trusted distance is what later let star angles become miles. The same idea, a measured ground length tied to a measured angle in the sky, is the subject of [Geodetic networks](../82-geodetic-networks/).

The rods, the road, and the counting method are in [How Jean Picard measured a straight line 6.9 miles long (PDF)](/downloads/Picard_Baseline_Rod_Method_Faria_2026-11-09.pdf).

## Key facts
- Picard's 1669–70 network begins with one measured baseline, not with the triangles alone.
- He measured the Villejuif-to-Juvisy road with wooden rods: two bars, each 4 toises, about 25.6 feet, with copper ends.
- One bar stayed on the ground so the line never lost its place.
- The baseline was 5,663 toises, about 6.9 miles.
