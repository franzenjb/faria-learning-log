## Birth in Goa
**José Custódio de Faria** was born on **31 May 1756** (a few sources say 30 May) in **Candolim, Goa**, then **Portuguese India**. He came from a **Goan Catholic family of Brahmin descent**: his father's line went back to **Anantha Shenoy**, a Goud Saraswat Brahmin village clerk who converted in 1583. His father was **Caetano Vitorino de Faria**; his mother **Rosa Maria de Sousa**. The marriage was unhappy; the couple separated with the Church's permission, his **father became a priest** and his **mother a nun** at the **Convent of St. Monica** in Old Goa.

## Rome and the "vegetables"
Father and son reached **Lisbon in December 1771**, when José was 15, and King Joseph I sent them to **Rome**. José trained for the priesthood and earned a **doctorate in theology**, dedicating his thesis to **Queen Maria I of Portugal**. He was reportedly invited to preach in the **Sistine Chapel** before the Pope.

Back in Lisbon he was invited to preach before **Queen Maria I**. The famous **anecdote** (from family tradition, not a documented record): climbing the pulpit, he froze at the sight of the court, until his father whispered in **Konkani**:

> *"Hi sogli baji; kator re baji."* "They're all vegetables; cut the vegetables!"

Jolted, he lost his fear and preached fluently. The story goes that he spent the rest of his life wondering how a single phrase could change a mind's state so completely: the seed of his work on suggestion.

## The Pinto Conspiracy and flight to France
In **1787** Goan priests and others plotted the **Conspiracy of the Pintos**, the first organized attempt to overthrow Portuguese rule in Goa, named for the Pinto family of Candolim in whose house it was planned. It was betrayed and crushed. Faria was **implicated**, though how deeply is unclear, and he left for **France in 1788**.

## Revolution and prison
In Paris he is reported to have **led one of the sectional battalions in the uprising of 13 Vendémiaire (5 October 1795)**, the royalist-leaning revolt *against* the National Convention, which the young **Napoleon** broke with his "whiff of grapeshot." (Wikipedia's English article says "10 Vendémiaire" and has him helping cause the Convention's fall; the uprising was in fact on 13 Vendémiaire, and it failed.) He knew **Chateaubriand** and was a friend of the **Marquis de Puységur**, Mesmer's leading disciple.

He was **imprisoned at some point**, but the details are thin. **Whether he was ever held in the Château d'If**, as in Dumas's novel, is **doubtful and probably legendary**; no reliable record puts him there.

In **1811** he became **professor of philosophy at Nîmes** and a member of the **Société Médicale de Marseille**.

## Mesmer and animal magnetism
**Franz Anton Mesmer** (1734–1815) claimed that an invisible **magnetic fluid** filled the universe and living bodies, and that a "magnetizer" could channel it to cure illness. A royal commission including **Benjamin Franklin** and **Lavoisier** investigated in 1784 and concluded the effects came from imagination. His name gave us **"mesmerize."**

## Faria's idea: it's in the subject's mind
From his first public sessions in **1814** in Paris, Faria broke with the fluid theory:

- **Nothing comes from the magnetizer; everything comes from the subject**, from imagination and expectation working inside the subject's own mind. This is what we now call **suggestion**, and he also pointed to **autosuggestion**.
- He called the trance **"sommeil lucide"** (**lucid sleep**), the subject a **"concentrated"** person (*concentré*), and himself the **"concentrator"** (*concentrateur*). The state, he said, is part of the **natural order**, a form of sleep.
- His method was **command, following expectancy**: he seated the subject, asked them to close their eyes and focus on sleep, then ordered in a firm voice:

> **"Dormez!"** ("Sleep!")

Fixed gaze and mental fatigue helped, but the cause was moral, inside the person.

**His book.** In **1819** he published ***De la cause du sommeil lucide, ou Étude de la nature de l'homme*** (*On the Cause of Lucid Sleep, or Study of the Nature of Man*). It was planned in several volumes; **only volume 1 appeared**. Laurent Carrer published the first English translation in 2004.

## Mockery and an obscure death
Paris turned on him. He was accused of being a **charlatan**, and he was mocked on stage, including in the comedy ***La Magnétismomanie*** (1816), after a public demonstration reportedly went wrong when an actor pretended to be entranced. He retired as **chaplain to an obscure religious house** and **died of a stroke in Paris on 20 September 1819** (one passage in the English Wikipedia says 30 September; most sources give the 20th). He left no address, and his **grave is unmarked**, somewhere in **Montmartre**.

## Pioneer of hypnosis
Later historians restored him as **a pioneer of the scientific study of hypnosis**, the first to put **suggestion** at its center. The theory is sometimes called **Fariism**. The line runs through **Ambroise-Auguste Liébeault** and **Hippolyte Bernheim** of the **Nancy school**, and **Émile Coué**'s autosuggestion. The Scottish surgeon **James Braid**, who coined "hypnotism" in 1841, reached similar conclusions; how directly Faria influenced him is debated. Faria is also seen as a **forerunner of the placebo idea**: belief and expectation themselves produce real effects.

## Dumas's Faria
**Alexandre Dumas** took the name for the imprisoned priest in ***The Count of Monte Cristo* (1844)**: the "mad" abbé in the Château d'If who tunnels into Edmond Dantès's cell, educates him in languages, science and history, and reveals the treasure of Monte Cristo. Dumas's Faria is a **fictional composite**. Apart from the name, the priesthood and a certain mental self-mastery, he bears little resemblance to the real man.

## The statue in Panaji
![The bronze statue of Abbé Faria hypnotizing a woman, Panaji, Goa](../../img/Abbe_Faria_Statue_Panaji_Goa.jpg?v=1)
*Statue of Abbé Faria, Panaji, Goa. Vn nilesh at English Wikipedia, CC BY-SA 3.0, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:AbaDeFaria.JPG).*

In central **Panaji (Panjim)**, next to the **Old Secretariat** (the Adil Shah or Idalcão Palace), stands a **bronze statue of Faria, arms outstretched over a woman falling into a trance**. It dates from **1945** and is usually credited to the Goan sculptor **Ramchandra Pandurang Kamat**; some sources credit **Constâncio Fernandes** instead. Portugal marked his 250th birthday in **2006** with a stamp showing the statue, and Lisbon and Margao have streets named for him.

(No freely licensed portrait of Faria turned up; the Bibliothèque nationale in Paris holds a contemporary caricature.)

## Closing reflection
Dumas's Faria **educated Dantès's mind in prison**. The real Faria showed that **the mind can put itself into a trance**: the hypnotist only gives the cue, and the subject does the work. That's a question for any tutor:

> **Does the tutor teach, or does the student learn?**

## Key facts
- José Custódio de Faria: born 31 May 1756, Candolim, Goa; died 20 September 1819, Paris.
- Doctorate in theology in Rome; preached before Queen Maria I ("cut the vegetables," an anecdote).
- Implicated in the Pinto Conspiracy (1787); to France 1788; reportedly in the 13 Vendémiaire uprising (1795).
- Château d'If imprisonment: doubtful, probably legend.
- Rejected Mesmer's fluid: the trance comes from the subject (suggestion); "sommeil lucide," "Dormez!"
- *De la cause du sommeil lucide* (1819), only volume 1 published.
- Forerunner of the Nancy school (Liébeault, Bernheim) and the placebo idea.
- Panaji statue, 1945, usually credited to Ramchandra Pandurang Kamat.
