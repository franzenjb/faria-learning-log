[Open The Interactive Moon Mountain](/galileo/)

Galileo saw bright mountaintops in the dark part of the Moon. The peaks caught sunrise while the ground below stayed dark. Their distance beyond the daylight edge told him how high they had to be.

Move the mountain, change its height, or change the Sun angle. You can use the sliders or drag the mountain's peak. The four-mile example starts with a sunlit peak above dark ground. Lower it to two miles and watch the peak go dark.

## Four Miles, In Familiar Terms
Four modern miles is 21,120 feet above the ground. In this simplified model, a mountain about 93 miles beyond the daylight edge needs roughly that height to reach the sunlight.

Galileo's historical claim was **more than four Italian miles**. The interactive uses modern miles and a rounded modern Moon size, so it teaches his reasoning rather than reproducing his exact numbers.

## Key facts
- High peaks can catch sunlight while lower ground is still dark.
- Farther beyond the daylight edge, a peak must be taller to reach the Sun.
- Four modern miles is 21,120 feet; Galileo used historical Italian miles.
- The interactive is a side view with exaggerated height, not a telescope photograph.
