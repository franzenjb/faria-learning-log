## A life between giants
**Galileo Galilei, 1564–1642.** He was born in Pisa on **15 February 1564**, the year **Shakespeare** was born and three days before **Michelangelo died**. He died at Arcetri on **8 January 1642**; **Isaac Newton** was born on Christmas Day that year (by the English calendar then in use).

## The telescope, 1609
In 1608 Dutch spectacle-makers produced a **spyglass** magnifying about 3 times. Hearing of it in **1609**, Galileo worked out the optics and ground his own lenses, reaching about **8×, then 20×, and later about 30×**. He first called it a ***perspicillum***. The name ***telescope*** (Greek *tele*, "far," + *skopein*, "to look") was proposed by **Giovanni Demisiani** at a **banquet in Rome in April 1611** held by the Accademia dei Lincei in Galileo's honor.

## The five discoveries
**1. Mountains on the Moon (late 1609).** Near the **terminator**, the line between lunar day and night, he saw **points of light in the dark part**: mountaintops catching sunrise before the plains below. From how far beyond the terminator they lay, he calculated heights of about **four miles**. He recorded what he saw in **ink-wash drawings**. The Moon was not a perfect crystal sphere; it was a world like Earth.

*Galileo's ink-wash drawings of the Moon, 1609. Galileo Galilei, Public domain, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Galileo%27s_sketches_of_the_moon.png).*

**2. Jupiter's moons (7 January 1610).** He saw **three, then four, "stars"** strung in a line beside Jupiter, changing position night to night: moons circling Jupiter. He named them the **"Medicean Stars"** for **Cosimo II de' Medici**, Grand Duke of Tuscany, and his brothers, and won the post of **court mathematician and philosopher** in Florence. They answered a key objection to Copernicus, that **a moving Earth would leave the Moon behind**: Jupiter moves and keeps four.

**3. The phases of Venus (late 1610).** In Ptolemy's arrangement Venus always lies between Earth and the Sun, so it could only ever show **crescents**. Galileo saw a **full cycle of phases**, crescent to gibbous to nearly full, and the planet's **size changing**: big as a crescent, small when nearly full. Only a **Venus orbiting the Sun** does that. It killed Ptolemy's arrangement, though **Tycho Brahe's** hybrid system (planets circle the Sun, the Sun circles Earth) still fit. To claim priority before he was sure, he sent a scrambled Latin anagram, later revealed as:

> ***"Cynthiae figuras aemulatur mater amorum."*** "The mother of loves [Venus] imitates the shapes of Cynthia [the Moon]."

**4. Sunspots (1611–12).** By **projecting** the Sun's image onto paper, he tracked dark spots crossing the disc and showed they lay **on the Sun's surface**, which therefore **rotates, about once a month** (about 27 days as seen from Earth). The Jesuit **Christoph Scheiner** argued they were small bodies orbiting the Sun; the priority dispute made a dangerous enemy. Galileo went **blind in 1638**, but likely from **cataracts and glaucoma**, not, as legend says, from staring at the Sun.

**5. The Milky Way and countless stars.** The Milky Way resolved into **innumerable stars**. Around **Orion's belt and sword**, where the eye sees about **nine** stars, he plotted **over 80** more; in the **Pleiades**, where the eye sees **six or seven**, he drew **36** (he wrote "more than forty"). And **stars stayed points** in the telescope while **planets showed disks**: the stars were vastly farther away.

## The Starry Messenger and the trial
- ***Sidereus Nuncius*** (*The Starry Messenger*), **March 1610**, announced the Moon, the stars and Jupiter's moons in 24 pages.
- **1616:** the Church's Congregation of the Index **suspended Copernicus's book** pending correction and condemned heliocentrism as contrary to Scripture; **Cardinal Bellarmine** formally warned Galileo **not to hold or defend** it (the exact terms of the injunction were later disputed).
- **1632:** ***Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems*** staged a debate in which the Ptolemaic side is voiced by **Simplicio**, and Simplicio is given **Pope Urban VIII's own favorite argument** (that God could produce any effect in ways we can't know). The Pope, once his friend, took it as mockery.
- **1633:** tried by the Inquisition, Galileo knelt and swore that he would **"abjure, curse and detest"** his errors. Sentenced to prison, commuted to **house arrest** at Arcetri for the rest of his life.
- ***"Eppur si muove"*** ("And yet it moves") is a **legend**: the first record is **Giuseppe Baretti's in 1757**, over a century later.

## Milton visits Galileo
In **1638**, the young **John Milton**, on his Italian tour, visited the old man. He wrote in ***Areopagitica*** (1644):

> **"There it was that I found and visited the famous Galileo, grown old, a prisoner to the Inquisition, for thinking in astronomy otherwise than the Franciscan and Dominican licensers thought."**

Galileo is the only contemporary named in ***Paradise Lost***, and he appears first in Book I as the "Tuscan artist":

> "the moon, whose orb / Through optic glass the Tuscan artist views / At evening from the top of Fesole, / Or in Valdarno, to descry new lands, / Rivers or mountains in her spotty globe."

**Milton was not blind then.** He was **29**. His sight failed in the **mid-1640s**, he was **totally blind by about 1652**, and he **dictated *Paradise Lost* in the 1660s**. His sonnet on his blindness:

> **"When I consider how my light is spent"** ... ending, **"They also serve who only stand and wait."**

Two great minds met in 1638: one going blind, the other who would.

## Afterward
- **1758:** the general ban on books teaching heliocentrism was dropped from the Index (Galileo's *Dialogue* itself stayed listed until **1835**).
- **1992:** **Pope John Paul II** accepted a commission's finding that the Church's judges had erred.

## Memorize the four moons: I Eat Green Carrots
> ## **I** **E**at **G**reen **C**arrots
> **Io · Europa · Ganymede · Callisto**, innermost to outermost

Each is a lover of **Zeus (Jupiter)**, so the names come with stories:
- **Io**: a priestess Zeus turned into a **heifer** to hide her from Hera.
- **Europa**: carried off by Zeus disguised as a **white bull**; she gave her name to **Europe**.
- **Ganymede**: the beautiful Trojan youth carried to Olympus to be the gods' **cupbearer**. Ganymede is the **largest moon in the solar system, larger than the planet Mercury** (in diameter).
- **Callisto**: turned into a **bear** and set in the sky as the **Great Bear**, Ursa Major.

(The names were proposed by Simon Marius in 1614; Galileo called them simply I to IV.)

## Latitude is easy, longitude needs a clock
**Latitude** comes straight from the sky: the height of Polaris (or the noon Sun) gives it (see [Looking Up](/topics/45-looking-up-polaris-planets-ptolemy-copernicus/)).

**Longitude is a matter of time.** Earth turns **360° in 24 hours = 15° per hour**. If you know the time **here** and the time at a **reference place** at the same instant, the difference is your longitude.

**Galileo's idea: Jupiter's moons as a clock in the sky.** **Io** disappears into Jupiter's shadow **about every 42.5 hours**, and that eclipse is **seen at the same instant everywhere on Earth**. Make tables of eclipse times at a reference city, then observe one wherever you are.

**Worked example.** The tables say Io vanishes at **midnight in Florence**. You see it vanish at **10:00 p.m. local time**. Difference **2 hours × 15° = 30° west of Florence.**

**At sea it failed:** you can't hold a long telescope steady on a pitching deck. **On land it worked:** **Giovanni Domenico Cassini's** Jupiter-moon tables (1668) let French astronomers **remap France in the 1670s–80s**. The coast came out well inland of where maps had it, and **Louis XIV reportedly** complained that his astronomers had cost him more territory than his enemies.

**Sea longitude** was solved by a clock instead: **John Harrison's** marine chronometer (H4, tested in the **1760s**).

**The GIS link:** every coordinate on every map started as an angle measured against the sky and a clock. GPS still works the same way, by timing: satellites carry atomic clocks.

## Question to think about
Was Galileo a **martyr to truth**, or **done in by his own arrogance**, mocking a pope who had been his friend?

## Key facts
- Galileo 1564–1642; telescope 1609; "telescope" named 1611.
- Five discoveries: Moon mountains; Jupiter's moons (7 Jan 1610); Venus's phases; sunspots; countless stars.
- *Sidereus Nuncius* 1610; warning 1616; *Dialogue* 1632; trial and abjuration 1633.
- "Eppur si muove": first recorded 1757.
- Milton visited 1638, aged 29, not yet blind; blind by ~1652.
- **I Eat Green Carrots**: Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto.
- 15° of longitude = 1 hour; Io eclipses every ~42.5 h.
