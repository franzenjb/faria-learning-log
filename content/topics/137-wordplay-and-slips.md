Some of these are made on purpose. Some happen by accident. The accidental ones are easy to confuse, so this page keeps the four slips apart: the wrong word, the slip that still makes sense, the misheard line, and the swapped sounds.

This is one of four lessons under [Confusing Word Terms](../133-confusing-word-terms/).

## Portmanteau

**A portmanteau packs two words into one.**

*Smog* is *smoke* plus *fog*. *Brunch* is *breakfast* plus *lunch*. *Motel* is *motor* plus *hotel*. Lewis Carroll gave the method its name in *Through the Looking-Glass* (1871). Humpty Dumpty explains *slithy*, from the "Jabberwocky" poem: "Well, *slithy* means 'lithe and slimy.' You see it's like a portmanteau — there are two meanings packed up into one word." A portmanteau, in Victorian English, was a suitcase that opened into two compartments. The English word comes from French *porter*, "to carry," and *manteau*, "cloak."

Say it: port-MAN-toh.

## Onomatopoeia

**Onomatopoeia is a word made to imitate a sound.**

*Buzz*, *hiss*, *murmur*, *cuckoo*, *bang*, *splash*. Greek *onoma*, "name," plus *poiein*, "to make": the making of a name from a sound. In *The Tempest* (1.2), Ariel sings, "Hark, hark! / Bow-wow. / The watch-dogs bark!" In *Love's Labour's Lost* (5.2), the spring song has "The cuckoo then, on every tree, / Mocks married men; for thus sings he, / Cuckoo."

Say it: on-uh-mat-uh-PEE-uh. The stress lands on *PEE*.

## Palindrome

**A palindrome reads the same forwards and backwards.**

Greek *palin* means "again" or "back," and *dromos* means "running": a running back. Whole words include *kayak*, *radar*, *level*, and *civic*. Longer phrases ignore spaces and punctuation. "A man, a plan, a canal: Panama!" was published by Leigh Mercer in *Notes and Queries* on 13 November 1948. "Madam, I'm Adam" is an older traditional line. "Able was I ere I saw Elba" is a later English joke pinned on Napoleon, who did not say it.

Say it: PAL-in-drome.

## Anagram

**An anagram uses the same letters in a new order.**

Greek *ana* means "back" or "again," plus *gramma*, "letter." *Listen* and *silent*. *The Morse code* and *Here come dots*. *Eleven plus two* and *twelve plus one*. And a useful one: *William Shakespeare* rearranges to *I am a weakish speller*. The letters really do match.

Say it: AN-uh-gram.

## Malapropism

**A malapropism is the wrong word, close in sound to the right one, used in a way that is often comic and does not try to make hidden sense.**

The name comes from Mrs. Malaprop in Richard Brinsley Sheridan's play *The Rivals* (1775). French *mal à propos* means "inappropriate." She says a young woman is "as headstrong as an allegory on the banks of the Nile," when she means *alligator*, and she praises "the very pineapple of politeness" for *pinnacle*.

Shakespeare had already written the same kind of slip for Dogberry, the constable in *Much Ado About Nothing*. "Comparisons are odorous," he says (3.5), for *odious*. In 4.2 he threatens that a villain will be "condemned into everlasting redemption."

Say it: MAL-uh-prop-iz-um.

## Eggcorn

**An eggcorn is a slip that still makes a kind of sense.**

A woman wrote *egg corns* for *acorns*. An acorn is a seed, and the seed end looks a little like an egg, so the new form is not nonsense. That is the difference from a malapropism. *Tow the line* for *toe the line* (as if you were pulling a rope, not putting a toe to a mark). *For all intensive purposes* for *for all intents and purposes*. The reshaping is old; the name is new. In September 2003, Mark Liberman described the case on the blog Language Log, and Geoffrey K. Pullum suggested calling such slips *eggcorns*.

Say it: EGG-korn.

## Mondegreen

**A mondegreen is a misheard line, especially in a song or a poem, that makes a new scene.**

Sylvia Wright coined the word in "The Death of Lady Mondegreen," in *Harper's* in November 1954. As a child she heard a Scottish ballad as ending "They hae slain the Earl o' Moray, / And Lady Mondegreen." The real last line is "And laid him on the green." She preferred her version. "The point about what I shall hereafter call mondegreens," she wrote, "is that they are better than the original."

Later ears have done the same with songs. Jimi Hendrix sings "Excuse me while I kiss the sky," in "Purple Haze" (1967); many people hear "kiss this guy."

A mondegreen is mishearing. An eggcorn is a reshaping that still seems to mean something. A malapropism is a wrong word, usually spoken, that does not need to make that kind of sense.

Say it: MON-duh-green.

## Spoonerism

**A spoonerism swaps the first sounds of two words.**

The name comes from the Reverend William Archibald Spooner (1844–1930), of New College, Oxford. The *Oxford English Dictionary* has *spoonerism* from 1900. Many famous examples were invented later. *The Oxford Dictionary of Quotations* treats only one as substantiated: "The weight of rages will press hard upon the employer," for "the rate of wages." Spooner himself said that his only one, in 1879, was "The Kinquering Congs Their Titles Take," for the hymn "The Conquering Kings Their Titles Take."

Say it: SPOO-nuh-riz-um.

## Oxymoron

**An oxymoron is a compressed contradiction, two words that seem to cancel each other.**

Greek *oxys* means "sharp" or "keen," and *mōros* means "foolish" or "dull": a sharp-dull. *Deafening silence*. *Bittersweet*. *Living death*. In *Romeo and Juliet* (1.1), Romeo says, "O brawling love, O loving hate, ... O heavy lightness, serious vanity, ... feather of lead, bright smoke, cold fire, sick health." *Jumbo shrimp* is the everyday cousin, more playful than tragic.

Say it: ok-see-MOR-on.

## Euphemism

**A euphemism is a milder or indirect word for a harsher fact.**

Greek *eu* means "good" or "well," and *phēmē* means "speech": fair-speaking. *Passed away* for *died*. *Let go* for *fired*. *Restroom* for the toilet. The opposite habit, a harsher word where a plainer one would do, is a *dysphemism*. Euphemisms can be kind, and they can also hide what is happening. The job is to notice them.

Say it: YOO-fuh-miz-um.

## Neologism

**A neologism is a newly coined word, or a new sense of an old word.**

Greek *neo* means "new," plus *logos*, "word." *Podcast* began as a neologism (and is also a portmanteau). *Robot* comes from Karel Čapek's play *R.U.R.* (*Rossum's Universal Robots*), written in 1920 and first performed in Prague on 25 January 1921. Čapek's brother Josef suggested the word, from Czech *robota*, "forced labor" or "drudgery."

Shakespeare is often said to have "invented" thousands of English words. More carefully: the *Oxford English Dictionary* finds its first citation for many words in his plays, which is not the same as proving he coined them. *Lonely* is first recorded in *Coriolanus*; *swagger* in *A Midsummer Night's Dream*.

Say it: nee-OL-uh-jiz-um.

## Key facts

- A portmanteau packs two words into one. Carroll named the method in 1871 from a two-compartment suitcase.
- Onomatopoeia is a made name from a sound. Say on-uh-mat-uh-PEE-uh.
- A palindrome runs the same both ways. The Panama line is Leigh Mercer's, 1948. The Elba line is a later joke, not Napoleon's.
- Malapropism: the wrong word, often comic, named for Sheridan's Mrs. Malaprop (1775). Dogberry got there first.
- Eggcorn: a slip that still makes sense, named by Geoffrey Pullum in 2003. Mondegreen: a misheard line that makes a new scene, named by Sylvia Wright in 1954.
- Most spoonerisms attributed to the Rev. William Spooner are later inventions. One substantiated slip is "the weight of rages" for "the rate of wages."
- An oxymoron is a sharp-dull. Romeo's "cold fire" is the literary type. A euphemism is fair-speaking (*eu* + *phēmē*). A neologism is a new word. *Robot* is from Czech *robota*, via Čapek's *R.U.R.* (1920/21).
