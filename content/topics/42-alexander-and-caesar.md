## Alexander the Great (356–323 BC)
Plutarch opens with his line on character: "It is not histories I am writing, but lives." He shows Alexander through small moments:
- As a boy he tamed the horse **Bucephalus**, which no one could ride, by noticing it was **afraid of its own shadow** and **turning it toward the sun**. His father Philip said:

> "My son, look for a kingdom equal to yourself; Macedonia is too small for you."

- His tutor was **Aristotle**.
- Meeting the philosopher **Diogenes**, he offered him anything he wanted. Diogenes:

> "Stand a little out of my sun."

Alexander:

> "If I were not Alexander, I would be Diogenes."

- His rage: drunk at a banquet, he **killed his friend Cleitus** with a spear, then grieved for days.
- His soldiers **refused to go beyond India**. He **died in Babylon at 32**, worn out and drinking heavily, still planning new conquests.

## Julius Caesar (100–44 BC)
- Kidnapped by **pirates** as a young man, he told them to demand a **higher ransom**, joked he'd come back and **crucify** them, and later did.
- In Spain he **wept before a statue of Alexander**: at his age Alexander had conquered the world, and Caesar had done nothing memorable. This is the hinge of the pair: Caesar measured himself against Alexander, and Plutarch measures them against each other.
- He crossed the **Rubicon**: "Let the die be cast."
- He let himself be named **dictator for life**; senators who feared it stabbed him **23 times**, and he fell **at the foot of the statue of Pompey**, the rival he had beaten.
- He ignored the warnings about the **Ides of March**, which Shakespeare took straight from Plutarch.

## The lesson: ambition
Both men were ruined by what made them great: **ambition**, an appetite for glory with no limit. The Greek word for its dark side is ***pleonexia***, **"always wanting more."** The quality that lifts a man is often the one that ruins him, like Milton's Satan:

> "Better to reign in Hell, than serve in Heav'n."

## What is the Rubicon?
A **small river in northeast Italy** flowing into the **Adriatic near Rimini**. In Caesar's day it marked the **border between his province, Cisalpine Gaul, and Italy proper**. Roman law forbade a general to bring his army across: doing so was **treason** and meant civil war.

In **January 49 BC** Caesar stopped at the bank with **one legion**. Plutarch says he hesitated a long time, weighing the disaster he was about to cause, then crossed saying, **in Greek**:

> "Let the die be cast."

The civil war that followed ended the Republic. **"Crossing the Rubicon"** now means passing a point of no return. The name likely comes from Latin ***rubeus***, **"red,"** for its reddish, muddy water. Nobody is sure which modern stream it was: in **1933** Italy officially named the **Fiumicino** the **Rubicone**, but historians still argue. A good mapping puzzle.

## Key facts
- Alexander 356–323 BC; tutor Aristotle; horse Bucephalus; died in Babylon at 32.
- Caesar 100–44 BC; crossed the Rubicon January 49 BC; 23 stab wounds; fell at Pompey's statue.
- *Pleonexia*: "always wanting more."
- Rubicon: Cisalpine Gaul / Italy border near Rimini; *rubeus* "red"; Fiumicino renamed Rubicone in 1933.
