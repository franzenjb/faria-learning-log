The 1974 paper was about judgment under uncertainty. The 1979 *Econometrica* paper, "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk," is about choice. Several later ideas on this page were pursued by Kahneman, by Tversky, or by colleagues, and are gathered in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011). The [peak-end rule](../120-peak-end-rule/) already belongs to that later group.

## Prospect Theory, and Loss Aversion

Prospect theory is one account of how people choose when the outcome is uncertain. Two points from the 1979 paper are enough for a refresh. People treat outcomes as gains or losses from a reference point, not as final wealth. And they feel losses more sharply than gains of the same size. That second point is loss aversion. A sure gain is often preferred to a risky chance with the same average. A sure loss is often avoided by taking a risk.

Example: Many people will take a sure $100 rather than a coin flip between $200 and nothing. Facing a sure loss of $100, many of the same people will take a coin flip between losing $200 and losing nothing. The facts are mirrored. The choices are not.

Spot it: Ask, "Am I treating this as a gain or as a loss, and from what starting point?"

Self-test: Restate the same choice as a gain, then as a loss. If your preference flips, loss aversion and framing are both in play.

## Framing, and the Asian Disease

In 1981, Tversky and Kahneman showed that the wording of a choice can reverse it while the facts stay the same. Students at Stanford and at the University of British Columbia answered short questionnaires in class. Imagine that the United States is preparing for the outbreak of an unusual Asian disease, which is expected to kill 600 people. Two programs are proposed.

Of 152 people who read the "lives saved" wording, 72 percent chose Program A: 200 people will be saved. The rest chose Program B: a one-in-three chance that all 600 are saved, and a two-in-three chance that no one is saved.

Of 155 people who read the "lives lost" wording, only 22 percent chose Program C: 400 people will die. 78 percent chose Program D: a one-in-three chance that no one dies, and a two-in-three chance that all 600 die.

A and C are the same fact: 200 live, 400 die. B and D are the same gamble. The "saved" frame pulled people toward the sure option. The "die" frame pulled them toward the risk. The paper also reports the same reversal among university faculty and physicians.

Spot it: Ask, "If I described this with the opposite words — saved instead of lost, or lost instead of saved — would I still choose the same way?"

Self-test: Without looking back, would you take A or B? Now C or D? If the two answers disagree, you have just met framing.

## The Endowment Effect

Once a thing is yours, you often demand more to give it up than you would have paid to acquire it. Richard Thaler named the endowment effect. Kahneman, Jack Knetsch, and Thaler tested it in 1990, at Cornell, with coffee mugs. People who had just been given a mug asked roughly twice as much to sell it as people without a mug were willing to pay. Ownership had shifted the reference point. Giving up the mug is coded as a loss.

Spot it: Ask, "Would I pay this much to get it, if I did not already have it?"

Self-test: Name something you own and would not sell at a price you would not pay to buy it again. That gap is the endowment effect.

## Sunk Cost

You let a cost that cannot be recovered decide the next step, as if spending more could un-spend the past.

This is not a Kahneman and Tversky paper. The classic study is by Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer, in 1985: people who had paid more for a theater series attended more plays, and people who had paid for a weekend trip were reluctant to skip it for a trip they would actually enjoy more. The pattern sits near loss aversion: walking away makes the past payment feel like a loss.

Example: "We have already spent so much on this plan that we have to finish it." The money is gone either way. The only question is whether the next dollar, or the next month, is still well spent.

Spot it: Ask, "If I arrived today with no past spending, would I start this now?"

Self-test: Cover the sunk cost with your hand. Does the next step still look like the best one?

## The Planning Fallacy

People understate how long a job will take, and how much it will cost, when they look at it from the inside. Kahneman and Tversky described this planning fallacy in 1979, in "Intuitive Prediction: Biases and Corrective Procedures." The remedy they pointed to is an outside view: how long did similar jobs actually take? In *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Kahneman tells of a team, including himself, that set out to write a textbook and estimated about two years. A colleague who had seen similar projects said that about 40 percent never finished, and that those that did took seven to ten years. The book took eight years, and was never used.

Spot it: Ask, "What is the outside view? How long did jobs like this actually take?"

Self-test: Name one plan you have now. What would a similar-case history say, before you listen to the inside story?

## The Halo Effect

A single impression of a person or a thing — good or bad — spills onto traits you have not observed. Edward Thorndike named the halo effect in 1920, in a paper on officers' ratings of their men. Kahneman uses it in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* as a way the mind keeps a story consistent: if someone is impressive in one way, we fill in the rest. Example: a clear, confident speaker is also judged more competent on the substance, before the substance has been checked. The reverse halo is the [ad hominem](../139-fourteen-classic-fallacies/). An [appeal to authority](../139-fourteen-classic-fallacies/) can be a halo in the other direction.

Spot it: Ask, "Which trait did I actually see, and which traits did I fill in?"

Self-test: Write down three traits you believe about a person you have only seen once. How many of those did you observe?

## Hindsight, and Outcome Bias

Hindsight is the feeling, after the fact, that the outcome was more predictable than it was. Baruch Fischhoff documented it in 1975: once you know what happened, the past looks as if it pointed that way all along. "I knew it all along" is the everyday sentence. Outcome bias, named by Jonathan Baron and John Hershey in 1988, is a close neighbor: you judge a decision by how it turned out, not by what was known when it was made. A careful choice can end badly. A careless one can end well. The result is not a verdict on the choice.

Spot it: Ask, "What did this person know at the time, and would I have called this a good decision before I knew the result?"

Self-test: Take a decision that turned out badly. Can you describe the information as it stood *before* the result? If the story only works with the ending in view, hindsight is doing the writing.

## Overconfidence

People are often too sure of their judgments, especially when the story is clear and the evidence is thin. Confidence tracks the coherence of the story more than the completeness of the file. Griffin and Tversky, in 1992, found overconfidence in some conditions and underconfidence in others: the pattern is not "people are always too sure." Confidence is a later chapter, as [the 8 November note](../85-kahneman-tversky-the-first-evening/) already recorded, and it belongs after the shortcuts and after prospect theory.

Spot it: Ask, "How complete is the evidence, and how complete does the story feel?"

Self-test: Give a range you are "almost sure" contains the right figure. Then ask whether, in a set of ten such ranges, about nine would actually contain it. If your past ranges have missed more often than that, the confidence was ahead of the evidence.

WYSIATI is again in the background: a tidy story feels like a sufficient one.

## Two Questions

1. Without looking back, would you take Program A or Program B in the Asian-disease problem? Now Program C or Program D? If your answers flipped, you have just met framing.
2. Name one plan you have now that might be a planning fallacy. What would the outside view — similar jobs, actually finished — say?

## Key facts
- Prospect theory (1979) treats outcomes as gains or losses from a reference point. Losses loom larger than same-size gains. People often play safe with a sure gain and take a risk to avoid a sure loss.
- In the 1981 Asian-disease problem, 72 percent chose the sure "200 will be saved" option, and 78 percent chose the risky option when the same facts were worded as deaths (400 will die).
- The endowment effect (Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler, 1990): people given a mug asked roughly twice as much to sell it as others would pay to buy it.
- Sunk cost is a related pattern, documented by Arkes and Blumer in 1985, not a Kahneman and Tversky paper.
- The planning fallacy (1979) is the inside view running short. Kahneman's textbook team estimated about two years; similar projects took seven to ten; theirs took eight.
- Hindsight (Fischhoff, 1975) makes the past look more predictable than it was. Outcome bias (Baron and Hershey, 1988) judges the decision by the result. Griffin and Tversky (1992) found overconfidence in some cases and underconfidence in others.
