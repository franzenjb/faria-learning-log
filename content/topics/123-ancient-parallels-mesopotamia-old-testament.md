On 3 December 1872, George Smith, a self-taught assistant at the British Museum, was sorting broken clay tablets from Nineveh when he came upon a dove that flew out, found no resting-place, and came back. He was not reading Genesis. He was reading a story already old when the Hebrew Bible was being written. That day he told the Society of Biblical Archaeology what he had found: Noah had a neighbor.

The usual mistake is to say that Genesis copied Gilgamesh. A fairer way to put it is that the Hebrew writers lived in the same world of stories, and they recast what they inherited. The boat, the birds, and the sacrifice stay. The God, and the meaning, change.

The Hebrew Bible as we have it was shaped, in large part, during and after Judah's exile in Babylon, about 587 to 539 BCE, when these older stories were close at hand.

## The Key Ideas

- **The flood.** In Gilgamesh Tablet XI, Utnapishtim builds a boat and sends out a dove, a swallow, and a raven. In Genesis 8, Noah sends a raven, then a dove three times. After the sacrifice, in R. Campbell Thompson's 1928 translation, "the gods did assemble like flies o'er him making the off'ring." In the King James Version, "the LORD smelled a sweet savour" and made a covenant. Older tellings name Atrahasis and Ziusudra. Gilgamesh did not invent the flood.
- **Creation.** *Enuma Elish*, usually dated to the late second millennium BCE, has Marduk split Tiamat "like a flat fish" to make heaven (L. W. King, 1902). Genesis 1 begins with a watery deep—*tehom*, a distant echo of Tiamat, say many scholars—but the deep does not fight. God speaks: "Let there be light." In the completed sixth tablet, humans are made from a rebel's blood, to labor for the gods. In Genesis they are made in God's image.
- **Law.** Hammurabi's code, from late in his reign, about 1750 BCE, and the Covenant Code in Exodus 21–23 share the "if a man..." form, the goring ox, and "eye for eye." Hammurabi fines the owner of a known gorer, by class. Exodus can put that owner to death, treats free persons more alike, and still prices a slave differently. One is a king's law under Shamash; the other is a covenant from God.
- **Wisdom, and a voice from Egypt.** *Ludlul bel nemeqi* ("I Will Praise the Lord of Wisdom") and the Babylonian Theodicy stand near Job, but Job argues: "I desire to reason with God" (Job 13:3, KJV). The Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope* was shown in 1924 to run beside Proverbs 22:17–24:22.

**Question.** If the stories share a boat, birds, and a sacrifice, what actually changes when one God makes a covenant, instead of many gods gathering like flies?

## A Later Voice: Omar Khayyam

The story does not end in the exile. Omar Khayyam of Nishapur, in Persia, living about 1048 to 1131, was a mathematician, an astronomer, and a physician. He wrote his mathematics in Arabic and his poetry in Persian.

The mathematics of that world did not begin with him. Babylonian scribes already solved field problems without symbols: find two numbers whose product is six hundred, and one is ten times the other. Call the width *x*—a short name for the unknown. The length is ten times *x*. Then *x* times *x*—that is, *x* squared, the number multiplied by itself—equals 60, so the width is about 7.75 and the length about 77.5. The PDF walks through every step. The scribes used a memorized recipe, and they counted in sixties, the same habit that left us sixty minutes in an hour.

![A field of 600, drawn to scale: ten times as long as it is wide. Width about 7.75, length about 77.5, area 600.](../../img/Babylonian_Field_Ten_Times_as_Long_as_Wide_Area_600_Faria_2026-11-24.svg)

Khayyam went a step further, from squares to cubes. About 1070, in his *Treatise on Demonstration of Problems of Algebra*, he solved cubic problems with a drawing: a parabola and a circle, the unknown length appearing where the curves crossed—about five hundred years before Cardano's *Ars Magna* (1545). About 1079 he was one of the astronomers who produced the Jalali calendar for the sultan Malik-Shah.

![How Khayyam found a cubic's answer: a parabola and a circle, sized from known numbers; the crossing marks the length he needed. An illustration of the idea, not a working plate from his treatise.](../../img/Omar_Khayyam_Parabola_and_Circle_Cubic_Construction_Faria_2026-11-24.svg)

The West knows him best through Edward FitzGerald's *Rubáiyát* (first edition, 1859, which opened "Awake! for Morning in the Bowl of Night"). These two quatrains are from the fifth edition, 1889, numbers I and LXXI:

> Wake! For the Sun, who scatter'd into flight
> The Stars before him from the Field of Night,
> Drives Night along with them from Heav'n, and strikes
> The Sultán's Turret with a Shaft of Light.

> The Moving Finger writes; and, having writ,
> Moves on: nor all your Piety nor Wit
> Shall lure it back to cancel half a Line,
> Nor all your Tears wash out a Word of it.

In plain English, the second says: what's written is written. No prayer, no cleverness, and no tears can unwrite it. FitzGerald made an English poem, not a line-by-line crib. Scholars still debate which Persian quatrains Khayyam actually wrote.

**Question.** FitzGerald made a Victorian English poem from Persian verses of debated authorship. What, then, still belongs to Khayyam?

## The Last Word

The Flood Tablet and the stele tell us the stories were shared. Genesis and the Covenant Code tell us they were not simply copied. The Moving Finger, in FitzGerald's English, says a line cannot be unwritten. The Hebrew writers could not unwrite the old stories either. What they could do—and did—was write a different meaning beside them.

- On 3 December 1872, George Smith presented the Babylonian flood story, from Gilgamesh Tablet XI, to the Society of Biblical Archaeology.
- In Gilgamesh the birds are a dove, a swallow, and a raven; in Genesis 8, a raven, then a dove. After the sacrifice, the gods gather "like flies"; in Genesis, God smells a "sweet savour" and makes a covenant.

- *Ludlul bel nemeqi* and the Babylonian Theodicy stand near Job; Egypt's Amenemope stands near Proverbs 22:17–24:22.
