"All men by nature desire to know." That is the first sentence of the *Metaphysics*, in W. D. Ross's 1908 translation. Aristotle does not start with a doctrine. He starts with a hunger you can feel.

**The full lesson is the PDF:** [Aristotle: Master of Those Who Know (PDF)](/downloads/Aristotle_Master_of_Those_Who_Know_Faria_Lesson_2026-11-24.pdf). It has his life and method, the ethics of the mean, the four causes, the break from Plato, the soul, the *Poetics* and the *Rhetoric*, the sky he got wrong, six questions, and a section on how authority froze his major errors.

He was not the first to feel that hunger. His teacher, Plato, had felt it, and [Socrates](../124-socrates-gadfly-of-athens/) before him. In Raphael's *School of Athens*, painted in the Vatican about 1509 to 1511, the two of them walk side by side. Plato points up, toward a realm of Forms. Aristotle's hand is level, toward the world in front of him. For Aristotle, the form of a thing lives in the thing, not in a separate heaven.

## A Life, and a Way of Looking

![Aristotle, 384 to 322 BCE. A linear scale. Marks: birth at Stagira in 384; joins Plato's Academy about 367; leaves after Plato's death in 347; tutors Alexander about 343; founds the Lyceum about 335; leaves Athens in 323; dies at Chalcis in 322.](../../img/Aristotle_Life_Timeline_Faria_2026-11-24.svg)

Aristotle was born in 384 BCE at Stagira, on the peninsula of Chalcidice, in the orbit of Macedonia. His father, Nicomachus, was a physician to the Macedonian court. At about seventeen he joined Plato's Academy and stayed nearly twenty years. After Plato's death, in 348 or 347, he left. He worked at Assos and then on Lesbos, studying the animals of the lagoon at Pyrrha. About 343 he became tutor to the young [Alexander](../42-alexander-and-caesar/). About 335 he returned to Athens and founded a school at the Lyceum. In 323, after Alexander's death, he left for Chalcis. Later tradition has him saying he would not let Athens "sin twice against philosophy." The wording is almost certainly not his; Socrates had already been executed there. He died at Chalcis in 322.

He looked first. In the *History of Animals*, in D'Arcy Wentworth Thompson's 1910 translation, the dolphin "has been seen asleep with his nose above water," and dolphins "are provided with milk, and suckle their young." They have lungs and a blow-hole; they are not fish in the ordinary sense. He described the cuttlefish's eggs and classified some hundreds of kinds of animals. Then he reasoned in a form that could be checked. The syllogism later teachers made famous is simple: all men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal.

## Ethics, Politics, and Four Ways to Ask Why

In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, "human good turns out to be activity of soul in accordance with virtue," in a complete life: "one swallow does not make a summer" (Ross). The Greek word is *eudaimonia*—a life that is going well, not a passing mood. Virtue "is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean... relative to us." Courage stands between cowardice and rashness; liberality between meanness and prodigality.

![A virtue between two vices. Three rows from the Nicomachean Ethics, in W. D. Ross's wording: cowardice, courage, rashness; meanness, liberality, prodigality; insensibility, temperance, self-indulgence.](../../img/Aristotle_Golden_Mean_Virtue_Between_Vices_Faria_2026-11-24.svg)

In Benjamin Jowett's translation of the *Politics*, "man is by nature a political animal." Constitutions are classed by who rules, and whether they rule for the common good or for themselves. He thought a large middle class was a stabilizer. He was not a modern democrat, and he took slavery as he found it. The useful tool is the question: who rules, and for whom?

To know a thing, he thought, you need four causes. Worked on a bronze statue: the bronze (material), the shape (formal), the sculptor (efficient), and the purpose—a memorial, a gift (final). The same four questions work on a house. The soul, in R. D. Hicks's 1907 translation of *On the Soul*, is "the form of a natural body having in it the capacity of life": nutritive in plants, sensitive in animals, rational in us.

The *Poetics*, in S. H. Butcher's translation, makes plot "the soul of a tragedy," and "a whole is that which has a beginning, a middle, and an end." Tragedy works "through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation of these emotions"—catharsis. The *Rhetoric*, in W. Rhys Roberts's 1924 translation, gives three ways a speech persuades: "the personal character of the speaker," "putting the audience into a certain frame of mind," and "the proof... provided by the words of the speech itself." Later shorthand: ethos, pathos, logos.

**Question.** In Raphael's picture, Plato points up and Aristotle's hand is level. What does that difference say about where each thinks the form of a thing lives?

## Where He Was Wrong, and Why It Lasted

Some of what he taught lasted because it was true. Some lasted because authority froze it.

| Error | Why it lasted | When it fell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The geocentric universe | Everyday experience; no visible stellar parallax; Ptolemy; later, church teaching | Copernicus, 1543; then Galileo and Kepler |
| Spontaneous generation (eels from mud; later, flies from rotting meat) | It fit what people saw; no microscopes; his name carried weight | Redi, 1668; Pasteur, 1859–62 |
| Women as incomplete males | Embedded in medicine (Galen) and theology (Aquinas) | 19th– and 20th-century biology, and social change |

He placed the Earth at rest in the center, with the heavens on nested spheres, moved by an unmoved mover that "produces motion as being loved." On eels, in Thompson's translation, they "are derived from the so-called 'earth's guts' that grow spontaneously in mud." On women, in Arthur Platt's 1910 translation of *Generation of Animals* II.3: "For the female is, as it were, a mutilated male." The PDF walks through each error, and why it stuck, at full length.

The physics and the cosmology were overturned. Much of Aristotle had reached the Latin West through Arabic readers, above all Ibn Sina (Avicenna) and Ibn Rushd (Averroes), "the Commentator." Aquinas called him "the Philosopher." Dante, in Longfellow's translation of the *Inferno*, saw "The Master... of those who know." The looking, the ethics of the mean, the analysis of plot and of persuasion, the sense that we are political animals—those did not have to fall with the spheres.

The same habit of asking why is the thread of [Why?: Questioning Authority from Socrates to Darwin](../125-why-questioning-authority-socrates-to-darwin/), written for this same day.

**Question.** Authority froze some of Aristotle's errors long after looking could have undone them. What in his own method would have been the way to thaw them?

## The Last Word

He was wrong about the sky, about eels, and about women. He was not wrong that we want to know. The looking he practiced is what, in the end, undid the doctrines that had hardened around his name. The hunger in the first sentence of the *Metaphysics* outlasted the spheres.

## Key facts
- Aristotle was born in 384 BCE at Stagira, joined Plato's Academy at about 17, left after Plato's death in 348/347, tutored Alexander from about 343, founded the Lyceum about 335, left Athens in 323, and died at Chalcis in 322. The "sin twice" farewell is later tradition.
- He studied animals in detail (dolphins that breathe and nurse; cuttlefish and their eggs) and set out the syllogism as a checkable form of argument.
- In Ross's *Nicomachean Ethics*, human good is "activity of soul in accordance with virtue" in a complete life (*eudaimonia*); virtue lies in a mean, as courage between cowardice and rashness.
- In Jowett's *Politics*, "man is by nature a political animal." Four causes, on a statue: bronze, shape, sculptor, purpose. Form lives in the thing. The soul is the form of a living body (Hicks).
- Three durable errors: a geocentric sky (to Copernicus, then Galileo and Kepler); spontaneous generation (Redi 1668; Pasteur 1859–62); women as "a mutilated male" (Platt), a view that passed through Galen and Aquinas.
- Averroes was "the Commentator," Aquinas called him "the Philosopher," and Dante saw "The Master... of those who know." The ethics, the looking, and the rhetoric outlasted the spheres.
