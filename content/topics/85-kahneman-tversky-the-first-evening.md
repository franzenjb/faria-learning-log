## Who was named

**Daniel Kahneman** was born in Tel Aviv on 5 March 1934 and died on 27 March 2024. He was a psychologist. In 2002 the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences gave him the economics prize for bringing psychological research into economics, especially research on human judgment and decision-making under uncertainty. In his own Nobel account, the long collaboration with Amos Tversky began in 1969, after Kahneman asked Tversky to speak to a seminar and the two met to study how people really judge chances.

**Amos Tversky** was born in Haifa on 16 March 1937 and died on 2 June 1996. He was a psychologist of judgment and decision-making. When the prize was announced, Kahneman called Tversky his close friend and colleague and said the award was largely for work they had done together years before.

The field named that evening is the psychology of judgment and of decisions. That work shaped what is now called behavioral economics. The lives, the friendship, and the questions the two men shared were not told. That is where the next conversation begins.

## The check that was actually done

A bat and a ball cost $1.10 together. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?

At 7:03 Jeff's first answer was 10 cents.

Ten cents does not work. A ball at 10 cents, and a bat costing one dollar more than that ball, would be a bat at $1.10. Ten cents plus $1.10 is $1.20. The pair has to cost $1.10, not $1.20.

The prices that do work are a ball at 5 cents and a bat at $1.05. Together they are $1.10. And $1.05 is one dollar more than 5 cents.

![The ball is 5 cents. The bat is $1.05. Together they are $1.10. The bat is one dollar more than the ball.](../../img/Kahneman_Bat_and_Ball_Five_Cents_Faria_2026-11-08.svg)

This puzzle is Shane Frederick's. It is the first item of the Cognitive Reflection Test in his 2005 paper in the *Journal of Economic Perspectives*. Kahneman and Tversky did not invent it. Frederick writes that 10 cents is the answer that springs to mind, and that 5 cents is the answer that survives a check.

An answer can arrive on its own, before anyone has tested it. A slower pass can test that answer. Jeff was asked whether the immediate answer is the one to trust. He had not answered. A fast answer is not always wrong, and a slow check is not always right. Ten cents was a fast answer that failed this one check. The wider question stays open. Nothing that evening said that a person has two brains.

## Not yet studied

The next conversation starts with the two men, their friendship, and the questions they shared. Jeff asked for that start the next day, by talking. No hour was chosen.

After that conversation has actually happened, the later path follows the order of the work. None of this has been learned.

- **Heuristics, and base rates with them.** The shortcuts are the subject of Tversky and Kahneman, "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," *Science*, 1974. Base rates belong there, not later. Kahneman and Tversky, "On the Psychology of Prediction," *Psychological Review*, 1973, is the base-rate paper, and the 1974 paper takes up the same point under representativeness. The 1974 paper says the shortcuts are highly economical and usually effective, and that they also lead to systematic errors. A fast answer is not a wrong answer by default.
- **Risk and loss, together.** Kahneman and Tversky, "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk," *Econometrica*, 1979, is one account of risky choice. Gains, losses, and the different ways people take risks are in that same paper. They are not two later discoveries, one after the other.
- **Confidence.** Griffin and Tversky, "The Weighing of Evidence and the Determinants of Confidence," *Cognitive Psychology*, 1992, is the direct later paper. It finds overconfidence in some cases and underconfidence in others. It comes after the shortcuts and after prospect theory because that is when confidence was taken up on its own.

Everyday examples, and any link to disaster response, wait until an idea has been talked through. They are not written on this page. The older disaster pages in this log are about other subjects.

Still open from 8 November: is the immediate answer the one to trust?
