A sextant is a hand-held tool for measuring one angle: how high a star or the Sun stands above the sea horizon.

Look at the horizon through the clear half of the horizon mirror. Swing the index arm until the star's image, seen in the mirror half, just touches the horizon. Read the angle off the arc. That angle is the star's height.

The name comes from the scale. The graduated arc is one-sixth of a circle, 60°. It still reads up to about 120°, because the two mirrors double the angle the arm turns. The arc gives whole degrees. A drum adds the fine part, in minutes, where one minute is 1/60 of a degree.

## Polaris's height is about your latitude

Latitude is how many degrees you are north or south of the equator. Polaris is so far away that its light arrives almost parallel to Earth's axis, so the angle up to Polaris from your flat horizon matches your latitude. The same idea is on [Looking Up](../45-looking-up-polaris-planets-ptolemy-copernicus/) and [Polaris](../48-polaris/).

Polaris sits about 0.6° from the exact pole today, so navigators added a small correction from a printed table.

By day, measure the Sun at its highest point, local noon. Latitude is 90° minus that height, then adjusted by the Sun's seasonal position from a printed almanac.

Latitude was routine. Longitude, how far east or west, needed an accurate clock, which came later. See [Time to longitude](../70-time-to-longitude/).

## Key facts
- A sextant measures how high a star or the Sun stands above the sea horizon.
- The arc is one-sixth of a circle, but the two mirrors let it read up to about 120°.
- For Polaris, that height is about your latitude, with a small table correction because Polaris is about 0.6° from the true pole today.
- Daytime latitude uses the Sun at noon: 90° minus its height, then a seasonal adjustment from the almanac.
- Longitude needed an accurate clock, which came later than routine latitude sights.
