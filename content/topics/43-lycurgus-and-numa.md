## "Nothing can be said that is not disputed"
Plutarch opens the *Life of Lycurgus* with an honest warning:

> "Nothing can be said that is not disputed."

His birth, travels and death are all contested, and some scholars doubt he existed at all.

## Lycurgus of Sparta
Lycurgus lived **around the 800s BC**, if he lived at all; he may be **legendary**.

**Laws from Delphi.** He got his laws from the **Delphic oracle**, and the Pythia greeted him as

> "more god than man."

![Lycurgus Consulting the Pythia (detail), Eugène Delacroix](../../img/Delacroix_Lycurgus_Consulting_the_Pythia.jpg?v=1)
*Lycurgus Consulting the Pythia (detail), Eugène Delacroix. Eugène Delacroix (1798–1863), Public domain, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Eug%C3%A8ne_Delacroix_-_Lycurgus_Consulting_the_Pythia_-_Google_Art_Project_4_(detail).jpg).*

**What he built:**
- **Land divided equally** among the citizens.
- **Gold and silver banned**, replaced with **heavy iron bars**. A small fortune needed an **ox cart** to move it, so nobody bothered to steal, bribe or hoard.
- **Common mess halls**, where every citizen ate together, and the infamous **black broth**. A visitor who tasted it said:

> "Now I know why Spartans don't fear death."

- The **agoge**, the state training every boy entered **from age seven**.
- The story of the **boy who hid a stolen fox under his cloak** and let it **chew into him** rather than be caught.

**His exit.** He made the Spartans swear an **oath to keep his laws until he returned**. Then he went to Delphi and **starved himself**, so he **never returned** and the oath could never expire.

## Numa Pompilius of Rome
Numa (traditionally **715–673 BC**) was **Rome's second king, after Romulus**. He was a **Sabine philosopher** who **refused the crown at first** and had to be persuaded.
- He founded the **priesthoods**, the **Vestal Virgins** and the **calendar**, adding **January and February**.
- He claimed counsel from the **nymph Egeria**, whom he met in a **sacred grove**. Plutarch suspected she was a **useful fiction**, a way to give laws divine weight.
- The doors of the **temple of Janus**, opened in war and shut in peace, **stayed shut for all 43 years of his reign**.

## The comparison
**Lycurgus built a city for war; Numa built one for peace.** Sparta's laws lasted **about 500 years**. Rome **dropped Numa's peace after his death**. Plutarch's verdict leans on endurance: the harsher system lasted, the gentler one didn't.

## Words from Sparta
- **Laconic**, "using few words," from **Laconia**, Sparta's region. Philip of Macedon threatened:

> "If I enter Laconia, I will raze Sparta."

The Spartans' whole reply:

> "If."

- **Spartan**, "austere, without comfort," from the mess halls, iron money and agoge.

## The Frankenstein link
These are the lawgivers the creature admired most when he read Plutarch: not conquerors, but men who shaped how people live together. He studied founders of cities while having no city of his own.

## Question to think about
Lycurgus said his laws came from Apollo; Numa said his came from a nymph. **Why would a lawgiver claim his laws came from a god?**

## Key facts
- Plutarch: "Nothing can be said that is not disputed."
- Lycurgus, c. 800s BC (possibly legendary); the Pythia: "more god than man"; iron money, mess halls, black broth, agoge from age 7.
- Numa, trad. 715–673 BC; Vestals, priesthoods, calendar (January, February); Egeria; Janus closed 43 years.
- Sparta's laws ~500 years; Rome abandoned Numa's peace after him.
- "Laconic" < Laconia; "If."
