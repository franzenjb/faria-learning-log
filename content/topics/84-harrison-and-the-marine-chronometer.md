This page gathers the clock. The arithmetic that turns a time difference into degrees stays on the [time-to-longitude lesson](/topics/70-time-to-longitude/), which already holds the longer account of H4, the radio checks, and GPS. The [Cook lesson](/topics/53-captain-james-cook/) holds the voyages. What belongs here is which machine went to sea, and what the reward was.

This is a reconstructed lesson from museum and statutory accounts, not a transcript.

Earth turns 360 degrees in 24 hours of solar time, so one hour of time is 15 degrees of longitude. A chronometer carries the time of a named meridian, Greenwich in the usual modern example. Local noon is when the Sun is highest, not when a civil clock says 12. The difference, in hours, times 15, is the longitude. Worked numbers, including 14:30 at Greenwich and 37.5 degrees west, are on the longitude page. So is the warning about the equation of time, up to about 16 minutes, which an almanac removes. This page does not repeat the tables.

John Harrison's fourth marine timekeeper, H4, is dated 1759 by the Royal Museums Greenwich. Work on it had started in 1755. "Finished in 1761" is the wrong year for the building. 1761 is the first sea trial. The Commissioners let his son William take H4 toward Jamaica. A further trial, to Barbados, followed in 1764. In February 1765 the Board agreed that H4 had kept time within the strictest limit of the 1714 Longitude Act, and it asked Parliament to pay 10,000 pounds once Harrison explained the watch, and the rest only after other makers could build the same kind of thing. Harrison thought the full reward was already due. That argument became the Longitude Act of 10 May 1765.

Later tests at Greenwich went badly in Nevil Maskelyne's report, and Harrison disputed the report. In 1773 he went to Parliament. The Act of that year pays a further reward for the invention and for showing how it was made. It is not worded as the Board handing over the lump prize of 20,000 pounds. Accounts put the further sum at 8,750 pounds. Royal Museums Greenwich says he was rewarded, but not by the Board, and not with everything he believed he was owed.

The watch Cook used was a copy. In 1766 the Board asked Larcum Kendall to copy H4. Kendall finished K1 in 1769. The plate is engraved with his name and that year. Royal Museums Greenwich records that K1 was issued to Cook for the second voyage, 1772 to 1775, in Resolution, and that it also went on the third voyage. H4 stayed the original. William Wales sailed as astronomer on Resolution to tend the trial. The National Museum of Australia states that appointment. The copy is the machine under test. Calling it "Harrison's watch, on Cook's ship" collapses the two.

## Key facts
- One hour of time is 15 degrees of longitude. The worked examples live on the longitude lesson.
- H4 is dated 1759. The first sea trial was 1761, then Barbados in 1764. The 1765 Act and the 1773 further reward are not a single payment of 20,000 pounds by the Board.
- K1 is Kendall's copy, finished 1769, issued to Cook. It is not H4.
