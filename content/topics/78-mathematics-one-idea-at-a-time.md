## Algebra: an empty place

Picture a shelter with three rooms, and imagine, only for the arithmetic, that each room holds 40 people. Let C be the total. The letter is a name for the empty place, not a special object. The sign × means times. The sign = means the two sides have the same value.

C = 3 × 40 = 120 people.

Check: 40 + 40 + 40 = 120. The sign + means add. Real shelter capacity depends on space, access, staffing, and safety. The 40 is invented.

A truck is to carry a 3,000-pound load. Suppose each loaded pallet weighs 500 pounds. Let P be the number of pallets. Then P × 500 pounds = 3,000 pounds. The sign ÷ means divided by. Division undoes multiplication. P = 3,000 ÷ 500 = 6 pallets. Check: 6 × 500 = 3,000. Dividing both sides of an equality by the same number, other than zero, keeps them equal. "lb" means pounds. This is weight arithmetic, not a loading plan.

## Geometry: the 3-4-5 triangle

People measured fields before Euclid. Around 300 BCE he organized much earlier knowledge in the *Elements*. BCE means before the common era. Geometry studies shapes, sizes, and positions. A proof is reasoning that shows why a claim must hold.

A full turn is 360 degrees. A square corner is 90 degrees. A right triangle has one square corner. "Right" means 90 degrees, not the right-hand side of the page. The hypotenuse is the side across from that corner, and it is the longest side. An equilateral triangle has three equal sides. On flat paper each of its angles is 60 degrees.

Pythagoras's theorem says the area of the square on the hypotenuse equals the areas of the squares on the other two sides, added. Area is surface covered. A square 3 feet on a side covers 3 × 3 = 9 square feet. For sides 3, 4, and 5: 3 × 3 + 4 × 4 = 9 + 16 = 25, and 5 × 5 = 25. If the long side were unknown, you would ask what length times itself gives 25. The answer is 5. The relation was known in Babylonian mathematics long before it carried Pythagoras's name.

Split an equilateral triangle of side 20 feet and you get the half that makes sine equal to one half at 30 degrees. That argument, the ladder, and Galileo's different lunar triangle are the [ladder lesson](/topics/77-ladder-sine-cosine-and-tangent/).

## Calculus: rate, and total

The shelter total and the triangle held still. A truck speeds up. A planet changes direction. Calculus describes continuous change, and what builds up from it.

In the late 1600s Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz developed calculus independently, on earlier work. Newton tied it to motion. It was not one person's invention out of nothing.

A rate of change compares a change in one quantity with a change in another. Speed is how much distance changes as time passes. Accumulation means adding up over an interval. From a distance record, find the speed. From the speed, recover the distance. Those are partner questions.

Suppose a truck travels 60 miles in two hours. Average speed is 60 ÷ 2 = 30 miles per hour. That does not mean it held 30 the whole way. Instantaneous speed is the speed at one moment. Estimate it over a short time. Calculus asks what happens as that interval shrinks.

On a graph, time runs across and distance runs up. Slope is rise compared with run. A tangent line follows the curve at one point. Its slope is the rate there. Finding that rate is differentiation. The rate is a derivative. This tangent line is not the triangle ratio tan. Both use slope, rise divided by run. The graph in the sheet is a picture of the idea, not the log of that 60-mile trip.

Turn the question around. At 20 miles per hour for one hour, the truck covers 20 miles. At 40 miles per hour for the next hour, it covers 40. The sum is 60 miles. On a speed-against-time graph, a rectangle's height is the speed and its width is the time. Their product is the distance. The area under the speed graph is distance. When speed changes smoothly, cut the trip into short pieces, estimate each distance, and add. Making that precise is integration. The total is an integral. Under suitable conditions, differentiation and integration undo each other.

Velocity is speed with a direction. Acceleration is a change in velocity, so a turn counts even at constant speed. Gravity, in Newton's account, is the attraction between masses. Forward motion plus the Sun's inward pull curves a planet's path. Without the pull it would continue straight. Many orbits are ellipses, oval paths, not circles. The drawing in the sheet is a schematic. Sizes are not to scale.

The Moon pulls harder on the near side of the Earth than on the center, and less on the far side. That difference helps raise tides. The Sun contributes. Local water levels also depend on depth and coastline. NOAA's tide notes are the place for that geography. Calculus does not erase it.

## Coffee, and a derivative

A fresh cup cools quickly, then more slowly as it nears room temperature. A differential equation ties a quantity to its rate of change. Newton's law of cooling is a simple model.

T is the coffee's temperature in degrees Fahrenheit. t is elapsed time in minutes. T with the label "room" is the steady room temperature. The word room is a label, not a multiplication. The gap is T minus the room temperature. The sign − means subtract.

dT/dt reads as the rate of temperature change, degrees per minute. Think of dT as a tiny temperature change and dt as a tiny time change. The derivative is the limiting rate. k is a positive cooling coefficient, a multiplier for how fast the gap shrinks. Its units are per minute.

The equation is dT/dt = −k times the gap. The cooling rate's size is k times the gap. The minus sign means a hot cup's temperature is falling. A smaller gap means slower cooling.

The time constant, named tau and written τ, is 1/k. One divided by k. Because k is per minute, tau is in minutes. No particular number of minutes is chosen here. The cup decides it.

After one time constant, about 37 percent of the original gap remains, so about 63 percent has closed. After three time constants, about 5 percent of the gap remains. Percent means out of a hundred. These percents describe the gap, not the room.

Example: coffee at 170°F in a 70°F room. The gap is 100 degrees. °F means degrees Fahrenheit. After one time constant the coffee is about 107°F. After three, about 75°F. Check the first: 37 percent of 100 is 37, and 70 + 37 = 107. The model assumes a steady room and a roughly constant k. It approaches room temperature and does not reach it in a finite time, in the ideal.

On the temperature graph, mark instant A and a later instant B. The slope of the line joining them is the temperature change divided by the time change. The run is positive. The rise is negative, because the cup cools. Hold A and slide B closer. The ratios approach a limit. That limit is the derivative at A. You never divide by zero. As the curve flattens, the slopes become less negative. The limit is exact for the smooth model. A real thermometer only estimates it.
