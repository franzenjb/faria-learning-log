## The sky turns around a point
Watch the night sky for an hour and every star wheels around one point, the **celestial pole**, the spot Earth's axis points to. In the north, a moderately bright star sits almost on it: **Polaris**, the North Star (today it's within about **0.7°** of the true pole, so it barely moves while everything else circles it).

## Polaris tells your latitude
**The height of Polaris above the horizon equals your latitude.**
- At the **North Pole** (90°N) it is **straight overhead**.
- At the **equator** it sits on the horizon.
- In **Florida** (about 25°–31°N) it stands roughly **27°** up, about three fists held at arm's length.

That's why sailors could always find latitude on a clear night. Longitude was another matter (see [Galileo](/topics/46-galileo/)). For the full story of Polaris itself — the triple star, Cepheids, precession, every name, and Shakespeare's Sonnet 116 — see [Polaris: the North Star, explained](/topics/48-polaris/).

## The wanderers
The stars keep their patterns; five bright lights don't. The Greeks called them ***planētai***, **"wanderers,"** which gave us **planet**.

**Mars's retrograde motion.** Mars normally drifts eastward against the stars night after night. But every two years or so it **slows, stops, runs backward (westward) for a couple of months, then turns forward again**, tracing a loop or zigzag.

## Ptolemy's answer: circles on circles
**Claudius Ptolemy** of Alexandria (about **AD 150**) kept **Earth at the center** and explained the loops with **epicycles**: each planet rides a small circle whose center rides a big circle around Earth. When the small circle carries the planet backward faster than the big one carries it forward, the planet appears to reverse. His great book, the ***Almagest*** (Arabic *al-majisti*, "the greatest"), predicted planetary positions well enough to **dominate for about 1,400 years**. It is **the universe of Dante and Milton**: nested spheres around a central Earth.

## Copernicus: the Sun in the middle
In **1543**, the year he died, **Nicolaus Copernicus** published ***De revolutionibus orbium coelestium*** with the **Sun at the center**. Retrograde motion became simple: **Earth, on a faster inner orbit, overtakes Mars**. It's like **passing a slower car on the highway**: for a moment the other car seems to slide backward against the distant hills, though both are moving forward.

## Star words
- **Disaster**: from Italian *disastro*, from *dis-* ("bad, ill") + *astro* (Latin *astrum*, "star"): **a "bad star,"** an ill-starred event.
- **Consider**: from Latin *considerare*, **possibly** *con-* + *sidus, sideris* ("star, constellation"), "to examine the stars" closely. The etymology is plausible but not certain.
- **Influenza**: Italian for **"influence"**, the **influence of the stars**, once blamed for epidemics.

## "A correct wrong model is bad news"
The Socratic question: why is a wrong model that gives correct answers dangerous? Jeff's answer:

**A wrong model that predicts well hides its own error.** As long as it gets the answers right, nobody has a reason to question the structure underneath. It's the **forecasting** problem: a model that verifies well for the wrong physical reasons will fail exactly when conditions change, and you won't see it coming.

Ptolemy fell not because his predictions were bad, but through three blows:
1. **Copernicus's simplicity** (1543): the same motions with fewer contrivances.
2. **Kepler's ellipses** (1609): orbits are ellipses, not circles, and the predictions became far more accurate.
3. **Galileo's evidence** (1610): Jupiter's moons and the phases of Venus, which Ptolemy's arrangement could not produce.

## Key facts
- Polaris's altitude = your latitude (about 27° in Florida; 90° at the pole).
- *Planētai* = "wanderers."
- Ptolemy, c. AD 150: Earth-centered, epicycles, the *Almagest*; dominant ~1,400 years.
- Copernicus, 1543: Sun-centered; retrograde = Earth overtaking Mars.
- Disaster = "bad star"; influenza = influence of the stars.
