## The word
**Disaster** comes from the Italian *disastro*: *dis-* (bad, ill, apart) plus *astro* (star). It reached English through French *désastre* in the late 1500s.

The idea behind it is astrology. People believed the position of the stars and planets ruled what happened on Earth. A catastrophe meant the stars had lined up against you, an "ill-starred" event. So every time a disaster response team deploys, it is, in the oldest sense of the word, answering a bad star.

## The Greek and Latin root
The root goes back to Greek *astēr* / *astron* (star), which also gave Latin *astrum*. Words in the same family:

- **Astronomy**: *astron* + *nomos* (law, arrangement), the laws of the stars
- **Astrology**: *astron* + *logos* (word, study), reading the stars for meaning
- **Astronaut**: *astron* + *nautēs* (sailor), a star sailor (see the next topic)
- **Asterisk** (\*): *asteriskos*, "little star", the star-shaped mark on a page
- **Aster**: the flower named for its star-shaped bloom
- **Asteroid**: "star-like", because early telescopes showed them as points like stars
- **Disaster**: the bad star

## Shakespeare used it this way
In *King Lear*, Edmund mocks people who "make guilty of our disasters the sun, the moon, and the stars." He means it literally: blaming the stars. See [Edmund's star speech](../03-lear-edmund-star-speech/).

## Key facts
- Disaster: Italian dis-astro, bad star
- Astro words share Greek astron (star): astronomy, astrology, astronaut, asterisk, aster, asteroid
- "Ill-starred" carries the same astrology idea in plain English
