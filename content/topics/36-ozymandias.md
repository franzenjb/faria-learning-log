## The poem (1818)
> I met a traveller from an antique land,
> Who said—"Two vast and trunkless legs of stone
> Stand in the desert. . . . Near them, on the sand,
> Half sunk a shattered visage lies, whose frown,
> And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command,
> Tell that its sculptor well those passions read
> Which yet survive, stamped on these lifeless things,
> The hand that mocked them, and the heart that fed;
> And on the pedestal, these words appear:
> My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings;
> Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!
> Nothing beside remains. Round the decay
> Of that colossal Wreck, boundless and bare
> The lone and level sands stretch far away."

## Who was Ozymandias?
**Ozymandias** is the Greek form of a name of **Ramesses II**, the great Egyptian pharaoh (reigned about 1279–1213 BC), from his throne name *User-maat-re*. The Greek historian Diodorus Siculus recorded the inscription on his statue: "King of Kings am I, Ozymandias. If anyone would know how great I am and where I lie, let him surpass one of my works."

In **1818** a huge granite head and chest of Ramesses II, called the **Younger Memnon**, arrived at the **British Museum** in London, where it still is. The news of it inspired the poem.

## The irony
- "**Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!**" The king meant: despair, because you can never equal me. Now the meaning **reverses**: despair, because this is what all your power comes to. Nothing beside remains.
- The sculptor "well those passions read": the artist saw through the king and carved his cruelty. The sneer survives; the empire doesn't.
- The final irony: the only things that last are **words**, the inscription, and the poem itself. That links it to [Sonnet 18](../31-shakespeare-sonnet-18/), where the poem promises to outlive Time.

## A sonnet contest
Shelley wrote it in friendly competition with his friend **Horace Smith**; each wrote a sonnet on the same subject, and both were published in *The Examiner* in early 1818. Smith's poem is long forgotten. Shelley's is one of the most famous poems in English.

## Shelley and tyrants
**Percy Bysshe Shelley** (1792–1822) was a radical who hated tyranny in every form. He wrote in the years just after **Napoleon**'s fall (1815), when Europe had just watched an emperor who conquered a continent end on a small island. The poem is about every ruler who believes his works will last forever.

## Key facts
- Ozymandias (Shelley, 1818): "Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!"
- Ozymandias is the Greek name for Ramesses II
- The British Museum's giant head of Ramesses II, the Younger Memnon, inspired it
- "Despair" reverses meaning: only the words survive, as in Sonnet 18
- Written in a sonnet contest with Horace Smith, whose poem is forgotten
