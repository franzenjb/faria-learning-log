English land measure still carries the shape of a plowed field. The odd numbers — 660, 5,280, 43,560 — are not accidents. They come from a furrow, a Roman habit of counting, and a queen's statute that kept the farmers' unit and stretched the mile to fit it.

## 1. A furrow long

A **furlong** is 660 feet. The word itself is the explanation: in Old English it is *furh* plus *lang*, "furrow long."

The traditional story is that this was about as far as a team of oxen could plow before the plowman turned the team, or let the animals rest. Turning a heavy plow at the end of a strip was hard work, so the furrow was made long, and the field was laid out in narrow strips. That length was later fixed at 40 rods, each rod 16½ feet, which is 660 feet.

You do not need a surveyor's tool to hold the idea. A furlong was the standard plow length. Later, when governments wanted to compare rents and prices from one farm to the next, they standardized that length, rather than leaving every village to its own furrow.

## 2. The Roman mile, and an odd English one

The word **mile** comes from the Latin *mille passus*, "a thousand paces." A Roman pace was five Roman feet, so a Roman mile was 5,000 Roman feet. In modern feet that is about **4,850** — a little short of 5,000 of our feet, because a Roman foot was slightly shorter than ours.

England inherited the word, and the habit of thinking of a mile as a thousand paces, or as eight shorter parts. But English miles were not all the same. They varied by region and by custom, and many of the old local miles were longer than either the Roman mile or the mile we use now.

It is tempting to say that everybody was still using the old Roman mile and getting it wrong. The closer story is this: people kept the Roman habit of counting a mile in eight parts, but the part that mattered on English ground was the farmers' furlong, not a Roman pace. Eight of those furlongs would not fit into 5,000 feet.

## 3. Why the mile is 5,280 feet

In **1593**, in the reign of Elizabeth I, a statute settled the argument, at least on paper. The act was called *An Acte againste newe Buyldinges*. Its main business was to forbid new building within three miles of the gates of the City of London. Tucked into it was a definition, so those three miles would be definite:

> a mile shall contain eight furlongs, every furlong forty poles, and every pole sixteen feet and a half.

That is 8 × 40 × 16½ = **5,280 feet**. The law kept the farmers' furlong and stretched the mile to eight of them. It did not shrink the furlong to restore a tidy 5,000-foot Roman mile. Land deeds, rents, and fields already ran in furlongs. Changing the furrow would have unsettled too much. Changing the mile was cheaper.

That is why the mile is the odd number 5,280, and not a round 5,000. The statute did not wipe out every local mile overnight. Customary miles, often longer, lingered on maps and in the countryside for a long time. The 5,280-foot mile became the legal mile throughout Britain only in 1824. But 1593 is when the number we still use was written into law.

## 4. Gunter's chain, and how to skip it

In **1620** the English mathematician Edmund Gunter described a surveyor's chain 66 feet long — four rods, or exactly one-tenth of a furlong — divided into 100 links. Ten square chains make one acre, which is 43,560 square feet. An acre is a rectangle one furlong long by one chain wide: 660 × 66.

*Drawn for this lesson. Eight furlongs make a mile. One of those furlongs, enlarged, is shown as an acre: 660 feet by 66 feet.*

The same arithmetic gives the larger counts. Eighty chains make a mile. A square mile is 80 chains by 80 chains, which is 6,400 square chains, and that is **640 acres**.

For a first telling, you can skip the chain altogether. Say a furlong was the standard plow length. Say an acre is a furlong long by a tenth of a furlong wide. The chain is the surveyor's way of making that tenth easy to measure on the ground. It is a tool, not the idea.

## 5. Tens for area, eights for length

Here is the odd joint in the system, and it is worth sitting with.

**Length**, when you walk a mile, is counted in eights: eight furlongs to the mile.

**Area**, when you measure a field, is counted in tens: ten chains to the furlong, ten square chains to the acre.

Gunter chose 66 feet, and 100 links, so that a surveyor could work in tens for area without giving up the eight-furlong mile that the statute had already fixed. The two number systems meet, but they do not match.

A few questions, before the numbers are handed to you:

- If ten chains make a furlong, how many chains make a mile of eight furlongs?
- A square mile is that many chains on each side. How many square chains is that, and how many acres?
- Why did no one simply make the mile ten furlongs, so that length and area would both run in tens?

(Ten chains to the furlong times eight furlongs is 80 chains to the mile. A square mile is then 80 × 80 = 6,400 square chains, and 6,400 ÷ 10 = 640 acres. The furlong was already locked into land law and into the acre, so stretching the mile to eight furlongs was easier than inventing a ten-furlong mile.)

## Key facts
- A furlong is 660 feet. The name is Old English *furh* + *lang*, "furrow long," traditionally the length an ox team plowed before turning or resting, later fixed at 40 rods of 16½ feet.
- A furlong was the standard plow length. Governments later standardized that measure so rents and prices could be compared.
- The Roman mile, *mille passus*, was 1,000 paces of 5 Roman feet, or 5,000 Roman feet, about 4,850 modern feet.
- English miles varied by region and custom, and many old local miles were longer than either the Roman mile or the later statute mile.
- In 1593, Elizabeth I's *Acte againste newe Buyldinges* defined a mile as 8 furlongs of 40 poles of 16½ feet, which is 5,280 feet. It kept the farmers' furlong rather than restoring a 5,000-foot Roman mile.
- That statute did not abolish every local mile at once. The 5,280-foot mile became the legal mile throughout Britain in 1824.
- Edmund Gunter's chain, described in 1620, is 66 feet long: 4 rods, or one-tenth of a furlong, divided into 100 links.
- An acre is one furlong by one chain (660 × 66), or 10 square chains, or 43,560 square feet. In a simple telling, an acre is a furlong long by a tenth of a furlong wide.
- 80 chains make a mile. A square mile is 80 × 80 = 6,400 square chains = 640 acres.
- Length is counted in eights (8 furlongs to the mile). Area is counted in tens (10 chains to the furlong, 10 square chains to the acre).
