## The poem
Published in **1915** (written mostly in 1910–11, when Eliot was barely in his twenties), it is the inner voice of a timid, aging man going to a society tea, wanting to ask a woman an "overwhelming question" and never daring to.

## The opening
> Let us go then, you and I,
> When the evening is spread out against the sky
> Like a patient etherized upon a table;

A shocking image in 1915: the sunset compared to a patient numbed on an operating table. It sets the mood: everything is anesthetized, waiting.

## Coffee spoons
> I have measured out my life with coffee spoons;

A whole life counted in tiny, polite, repeated social moments.

## Not Prince Hamlet
> No! I am not Prince Hamlet, nor was meant to be;
> Am an attendant lord, one that will do
> To swell a progress, start a scene or two,
> Advise the prince; no doubt, an easy tool,
> Deferential, glad to be of use,
> Politic, cautious, and meticulous;
> Full of high sentence, but a bit obtuse;
> At times, indeed, almost ridiculous—
> Almost, at times, the Fool.

He isn't even the hero of his own story, just a minor courtier like Polonius, or at worst the Fool.

## The peach
> I grow old ... I grow old ...
> I shall wear the bottoms of my trousers rolled.
> Shall I part my hair behind? Do I dare to eat a peach?
> I shall wear white flannel trousers, and walk upon the beach.

Why a **peach**? It's soft, juicy, **messy and sensual**: you can't eat one gracefully. Daring to eat a peach in public means risking dripping juice and looking undignified. It stands for his **fear of looking foolish**, and of desire itself. The white flannel trousers are the fashion of a young man at the seaside, which he worries he is too old for.

## The mermaids
> I have heard the mermaids singing, each to each.
> I do not think that they will sing to me.

> We have lingered in the chambers of the sea
> By sea-girls wreathed with seaweed red and brown
> Till human voices wake us, and we drown.

He can imagine beauty but believes it isn't for him, and when real life ("human voices") breaks in, the dream drowns.

## The name
**Prufrock** almost certainly came from the **Prufrock-Littau Company**, furniture dealers in St. Louis, Eliot's hometown, which advertised there in the 1910s. Asked about it later, Eliot said he had no memory of taking the name that way, "but I think that it must be assumed that I did, and that the memory has been obliterated."

"**Prude in a frock**" is a **readers' interpretation**, not something Eliot ever said.

## Key facts
- Prufrock (1915) opens with the evening "like a patient etherized upon a table"
- "I have measured out my life with coffee spoons"
- "I am not Prince Hamlet... an attendant lord... almost, at times, the Fool"
- "Do I dare to eat a peach?": the messy, sensual peach stands for fear of looking foolish
- The name came from the Prufrock-Littau furniture company of St. Louis
- "Prude in a frock" is a readers' reading, not Eliot's
