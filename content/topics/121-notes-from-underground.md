Two men spend years underground. One comes out speaking several languages, carrying a map to a fortune, and ready to act. The other comes out with a grudge, a notebook, and the best argument against the perfectly rational person ever written. The first is Edmond Dantès, taught by the [Abbé Faria](../44-the-real-abbe-faria/) in the Château d'If. The second is the nameless narrator of Fyodor Dostoevsky's *Notes from Underground*, published in the spring of 1864 in *Epoch*, the magazine he ran with his brother Mikhail. This page is about the second man, and about why the first one keeps showing up beside him.

## The Shape of the Book

The point first: Part I is an argument, Part II is the evidence, and the evidence is the arguer's own life.

- **Part I, "Underground"** (11 short chapters). The narrator is forty, retired, alone in "my corner" in St. Petersburg. He talks to imaginary "gentlemen" who believe in reason and progress, and he tries to wreck their faith.
- **Part II, "Apropos of the Wet Snow"** (10 chapters). Wet snow reminds him of what happened when he was twenty-four. Three episodes follow: an officer, a farewell dinner, and a young woman named Liza.

![Part I argues it. Part II proves it on himself. Each claim in Part I is joined to the scene in Part II that acts it out.](../../img/Notes_from_Underground_Part_I_Claims_Part_II_Scenes_Faria_2026-11-22.svg)

**Question.** Why would Dostoevsky put the argument first and the story second, when the story happened sixteen years earlier? What does the order do to how you judge the man?

## A Sick Man, a Spiteful Man

The first line is a diagnosis he gives himself:

> I am a sick man.... I am a spiteful man. I am an unattractive man. I believe my liver is diseased.

Within a page he takes it back: "I was lying when I said just now that I was a spiteful official. I was lying from spite." That is the key to him. Every statement is followed by a second thought about the statement, and then a third. He cannot become anything, he says, "neither spiteful nor kind, neither a rascal nor an honest man, neither a hero nor an insect."

The facts are plain. He was a **collegiate assessor**, a middle rank (eighth of fourteen) in the Russian civil service. A distant relative left him 6,000 roubles, so he retired and "settled down in my corner." The underground is not a cellar. It is a state of mind: watching yourself so closely that you can no longer move.

He names the disease himself:

> I swear, gentlemen, that to be too conscious is an illness—a real thorough-going illness.

He even blames the city, Petersburg, "the most theoretical and intentional town on the whole terrestrial globe."

### The Mouse and the Man of Action

His picture of himself is a mouse. Against it he sets the "direct" man, the man of action, whom he calls, in French, *l'homme de la nature et de la vérité*, the man of nature and truth. The phrase is usually read as a dig at Rousseau.

When the man of action is insulted, he charges "like an infuriated bull with its horns down" and believes his revenge is justice. The mouse is just as insulted, maybe more, but it doubts. It piles questions on the one question until there is "a fatal brew," then creeps "ignominiously into its mouse-hole," where it nurses the injury for forty years.

> It may be an acutely conscious mouse, yet it is a mouse.

The cruelest part is that no one forces the mouse to be a mouse. "He himself, his very own self, looks on himself as a mouse; no one asks him to do so."

### The Toothache Pleasure

Then the strangest claim. "Well, even in toothache there is enjoyment," he says. Not in the pain itself, but in the moaning: the malicious moan of an educated man who knows it does no good, keeps the household awake, and enjoys the fact that they see through him. Humiliation, fully understood, turns into "a sort of shameful accursed sweetness."

He draws the conclusion that runs through the book: "the direct, legitimate fruit of consciousness is inertia, that is, conscious sitting-with-the-hands-folded."

**Question.** Have you met the toothache pleasure, in yourself or in a meeting: the complaint that is really a performance? What is the difference between that moan and an honest one?

## The Argument: Twice Two Makes Five

The point first: people do not always act in their own rational interest, and sometimes they choose against it **on purpose**, just to show that they can.

His target is a confident idea of the 1860s: that people do wrong only because they do not know their true interests, and that once science shows them, they will be good automatically. He mocks it as a child's idea: "Oh, the babe! Oh, the pure, innocent child!"

The lists of human advantages, he says, all leave one out:

> One's own free unfettered choice, one's own caprice, however wild it may be, one's own fancy worked up at times to frenzy—is that very "most advantageous advantage" which we have overlooked.

And more bluntly: "What man wants is simply *independent* choice, whatever that independence may cost and wherever it may lead."

### The Piano Key and the Organ Stop

Suppose science really did find "a formula for all our desires and caprices." Then, he says, a person would be "transformed from a human being into an organ-stop or something of the sort." His answer is a dare. Even if you proved to a man that he was a piano key, he would do something perverse, even go mad, to disprove it:

> the whole work of man really seems to consist in nothing but proving to himself every minute that he is a man and not a piano-key!

So mathematics becomes the enemy. "Twice two makes four without my will. As if free will meant that!" And then the famous line:

> I admit that twice two makes four is an excellent thing, but if we are to give everything its due, twice two makes five is sometimes a very charming thing too.

He is not saying arithmetic is wrong. He is saying a life run entirely by arithmetic would leave no room for a self. From there he goes further: "suffering is the sole origin of consciousness," and people will never fully give up suffering, because it proves they are not finished.

**Question.** Is choosing what is bad for you really freedom, or just another kind of compulsion? When the Underground Man refuses a doctor "from spite," is he free, or is spite driving?

## The Crystal Palace, the Ant-Heap, and the Hen-House

The point first: the Crystal Palace is the book's symbol for a society so rational that no one could object to it, and that, for him, is exactly what is wrong with it.

![The Crystal Palace in Hyde Park, London, built for the Great Exhibition of 1851: iron and glass, designed by Joseph Paxton. Color lithograph from Dickinson's Comprehensive Pictures of the Great Exhibition of 1851 (Dickinson Brothers, published 1852–54). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.](../../img/Crystal_Palace_Hyde_Park_1851_Dickinson_Brothers.jpg)

The real palace was Joseph Paxton's glass hall for the Great Exhibition of 1851, later rebuilt at Sydenham in south London. Dostoevsky saw it there in the summer of 1862. In *Winter Notes on Summer Impressions* (1863) he described it as a kind of Baal, a modern idol that crowds stood before in silence.

The palace in the novel comes from a book. In 1863 Nikolai Chernyshevsky, writing in a prison cell in the Peter and Paul Fortress, published the novel *What Is to Be Done?*. In the heroine Vera Pavlovna's fourth dream, the future is a huge building of iron and glass, modeled openly on the Sydenham palace, where people live in rational harmony. The book preached **rational egoism**: understand your true interest and you will do good. It became a bible for young radicals. (Lenin later borrowed its title for a 1902 pamphlet.)

The Underground Man answers it in three images:

- **The tongue.** "You believe in a palace of crystal that can never be destroyed—a palace at which one will not be able to put out one's tongue or make a long nose on the sly." A world you cannot mock is a world you cannot be free in.
- **The ant-heap.** Ants have "a marvellous edifice of that pattern which endures for ever—the ant-heap." People, he suspects, love building and fear finishing. "Perhaps he only loves building it and does not want to live in it."
- **The hen-house.** "If it were not a palace, but a hen-house, I might creep into it to avoid getting wet, and yet I would not call the hen-house a palace out of gratitude to it for keeping me dry." Shelter is not the same as a reason to live.

Note the personal stake. In the 1840s Dostoevsky had been part of the Petrashevsky circle, which read the French utopian Charles Fourier. He was arrested in 1849 and spent four years in a Siberian prison camp. He was arguing against ideas he had once shared. He also complained, in a letter to his brother, that the censors cut a passage in Part I where he drew from all this the need for faith; what survived is the attack without the answer.

**Question.** Jeff, you build systems for disaster response: shelters, dashboards, models. Where does a well-designed system run into the person who simply will not do the sensible thing? Is that person a flaw in the system or information about it?

## Part II: Apropos of the Wet Snow

Part II opens with an epigraph from the poet Nikolai Nekrasov, about a "fallen woman" redeemed by the poet's "words of passionate exhortation." In Garnett's text the verse is translated by Juliet Soskice. It is meant ironically: the story will run the redemption scene backward.

### The Officer in the Billiard Room

The accurate version matters. He is not slapped. One night he wanders into a tavern hoping for a fight. He is standing by the billiard table, in the way:

> he took me by the shoulders and without a word—without a warning or explanation—moved me from where I was standing to another spot and passed by as though he had not noticed me. I could have forgiven blows, but I could not forgive his having moved me without noticing me.

"I had been treated like a fly." The insult is not violence; it is being unseen. (In Part I he had imagined being slapped, and finding a despairing pleasure even in that.)

His revenge is pure underground. He writes a satirical story about the officer and sends it to a magazine, which does not print it. He drafts a letter challenging him to a duel and never sends it. Then, for a long time, he plans not to step aside when they pass on **Nevsky Prospect**, Petersburg's main street. He draws an advance on his salary for black gloves and a decent hat, sells his raccoon collar, and borrows from his superior to buy a beaver one like an officer's. He loses his nerve again and again. Finally: "I closed my eyes, and we ran full tilt, shoulder to shoulder, against one another! I did not budge an inch." The officer seems not to notice. The narrator goes home "triumphant and sang Italian arias."

### Zverkov's Farewell Dinner

He forces himself onto a dinner for Zverkov, a former schoolfellow now an officer, whom he despises and envies. He arrives at five, as he was told; the hour had been moved to six and no one told him. He insults the guests, is ignored, and then, to show he does not need them, walks the room "from eight o'clock till eleven, in the same place, from the table to the stove and back again." It is Part I's "conscious inertia" turned into a picture: three hours of motion that goes nowhere.

### Liza

After the dinner he follows the party to a brothel and wakes beside Liza, about twenty. In the dark he lectures her on her future: a coffin he had seen carried up out of a cellar in the Haymarket, a grave with water in it, a life ending in "filth and mud." He knows he is doing it for the power. She answers with the line that cuts him: "Why, you ... speak somehow like a book." He goes on anyway, and she breaks down. He gives her his address.

Days later she comes, and finds him in a torn dressing gown, shouting at his servant. He turns on her and tells her the truth: he wanted power, he wanted "to wring out your tears," and "the world may go to pot for me so long as I always get my tea." Then something he did not foresee happens:

> Liza, insulted and crushed by me, understood a great deal more than I imagined. She understood from all this what a woman understands first of all, if she feels genuine love, that is, that I was myself unhappy.

She embraces him. He cannot bear to be pitied, takes her to bed in what he admits was "simply revenge, a fresh humiliation," and as she leaves he pushes money into her hand. He refuses to excuse it: "I opened her hand and put the money in it ... from spite."

After she goes, he finds "a crumpled blue five-rouble note" on the table. She had left it. He runs into the snow after her and stops at the crossroads. At home he consoles himself with a theory: "Resentment—why, it is purification." And then the question of the book:

> which is better—cheap happiness or exalted sufferings? Well, which is better?

He never sees her again. He calls himself an anti-hero, using the word himself: "a novel needs a hero, and all the traits for an anti-hero are *expressly* gathered together here." The last word is not his. An unnamed editor adds:

> [The notes of this paradoxalist do not end here, however. He could not refrain from going on with them, but it seems to us that we may stop here.]

**Question.** Who is the freest person in Part II? The narrator talked about free will for eleven chapters. Liza, who has almost no freedom, makes one silent choice with a five-rouble note. What does that choice say?

## Against the Sublime and Beautiful

The point first: the book takes the Romantic ideal of the noble heart and shows what it can turn into, a costume for vanity.

"The sublime and beautiful" is a stock phrase of the age. It echoes Edmund Burke's 1757 *Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful*, and in Russia it carried the scent of Schiller, whose lofty "beautiful soul" young Russians of the 1840s admired. (Dostoevsky's later hero Dmitri Karamazov still recites Schiller at length.) The Underground Man always puts the phrase in quotation marks.

His complaint is precise. At the very moment he most feels "every refinement of all that is 'sublime and beautiful'," he does his ugliest things. In his daydreams he is the Romantic hero to the hilt: he comes "in for countless millions and immediately devoted them to humanity," confesses his sins "in the Manfred style" (Byron's brooding hero), and everyone weeps.

So the Romantic said: noble feeling makes a noble man. The Underground Man answers: noble feeling can sit comfortably beside cruelty, because it costs nothing. His speech to Liza is the proof. It is full of beautiful feeling and it is a weapon.

## The Hinge Between Romanticism and Modernism

The point first: much of what we call modern literature starts here.

![What the book answers, and what answered it: a timeline from Dumas in 1844 to Kahneman and Tversky in 1979.](../../img/Notes_from_Underground_Timeline_1844_to_1979_Faria_2026-11-22.svg)

- **An unreliable narrator who says so.** "There is not one thing, not one word of what I have written that I really believe."
- **The anti-hero,** by name.
- **Consciousness as a disease,** not a crown.
- **A confession that knows confessions lie.** He cites Heine's view that Rousseau lied in his *Confessions* out of vanity, and admits he may do the same.

Friedrich Nietzsche found the book by chance in a French translation in 1887 and wrote to his friend Franz Overbeck of an instinct of kinship. In 1956 Walter Kaufmann opened his anthology *Existentialism from Dostoevsky to Sartre* with Part I. Treat "the first existentialist" as a label later readers gave him, not one he would have used; Dostoevsky was a Christian writer, and the cut passage suggests where he meant to go.

**Question.** Part I says the man is diseased. Part II shows him hurting someone. Is the book a portrait of a sick man, or a warning about a sickness in an age? Can it be both?

## Cross-References

### Kafka: The Insect Self

"I have many times tried to become an insect. But I was not equal even to that." Fifty years later, Franz Kafka's Gregor Samsa, a traveling salesman, wakes up as a monstrous vermin in *The Metamorphosis* (1915). Kafka read Dostoevsky closely; in a 1913 letter to Felice Bauer he named him among four writers he considered his "true blood relations." Whether he had this exact passage in mind is not documented, so call it an echo, not a source.

The closer cousin may be Kafka's late story **"The Burrow"** (written 1923–24, published 1931). An animal builds an elaborate underground home for safety, then cannot rest because of a faint noise it can never locate. It is the underground as Dostoevsky drew it: a refuge that becomes the trap, run by a mind that will not stop analyzing.

The difference: the Underground Man talks to us and argues. Kafka's people rarely argue; they are processed.

### Chekhov: The Clerk, Told With Mercy

Anton Chekhov's "The Death of a Government Clerk" (1883) is only a few pages long. A minor clerk sneezes on a general's bald head at the theater, apologizes, apologizes again, is finally shouted out of the room, goes home, lies down on the sofa in his uniform, and dies. It is the Underground Man's hypersensitivity to rank, cut down to a joke that stops being funny.

The difference is tone. Dostoevsky lets his clerk talk for a hundred pages. Chekhov stands back, says little, and lets compassion come through restraint. In an 1889 letter to his publisher Aleksei Suvorin, Chekhov described a young man who "squeezes the slave out of himself drop by drop." The Underground Man cannot do that; he keeps the slave and argues for it.

### Shakespeare: Thought That Stops the Hand

Hamlet is the Underground Man's oldest ancestor:

> Thus conscience does make cowards of us all,
> And thus the native hue of resolution
> Is sicklied o'er with the pale cast of thought.

Here "conscience" includes what we would call consciousness, the thinking that stalls action. Hamlet also says he could be "bounded in a nutshell, and count myself a king of infinite space, were it not that I have bad dreams." That nutshell is an underground.

Two villains fit his spite. Richard III decides, "since I cannot prove a lover," he is "determined to prove a villain." Iago tells us "I am not what I am," and offers so many reasons for his hatred that Coleridge called it "the motive-hunting of motiveless malignity." The Underground Man says the same of himself: his spite needs no cause, "precisely because it is not a cause."

And Edmund, in [King Lear](../03-lear-edmund-star-speech/), mocks those who blame their faults on the stars, "as if we were villains by necessity." The Underground Man refuses the modern version of the stars: the laws of nature and the piano key.

The difference: Shakespeare's men act in the end; Hamlet kills, Richard and Iago scheme. The Underground Man's whole revenge is a shoulder bump.

### Dumas: The Cell as School, the Underground as Trap

Here the site's own name comes in. In [The Count of Monte Cristo](../14-monte-cristo-and-napoleon/) (1844–46), Edmond Dantès spends fourteen years in the Château d'If. The [Abbé Faria](../44-the-real-abbe-faria/) tunnels into his cell and turns it into a school: languages, history, science, and the reasoning that reveals who betrayed him. Dantès comes out to act.

Set the two side by side:

| | Dantès | The Underground Man |
|---|---|---|
| The underground | A prison he did not choose | A corner he chose |
| What it gives him | A teacher, a method, a fortune | Spite and a notebook |
| The insult | Betrayal, prison, a stolen life | Being moved aside without being noticed |
| The revenge | Carried out, over years | Imagined, drafted, and one shoulder bump |
| Belief in justice | He calls himself an agent of Providence | "I see no justice in it" |

Part I describes Dantès almost exactly: the man of action revenges himself "because he sees justice in it," and so "he carries out his revenge calmly and successfully." The Underground Man's daydream of coming into "countless millions" and giving them to humanity is a Monte Cristo fantasy without a Faria. One small coincidence: he says of the officer, "I have not seen him now for fourteen years," the same span as Dantès's imprisonment. It is a coincidence, not a clue.

And the endings mirror each other. Dumas ends on "Wait and hope." Dostoevsky ends on a question: cheap happiness or exalted sufferings?

**Question.** What did Dantès have that the Underground Man lacked: a teacher, a real injury, or a purpose outside himself? Which matters most?

## Kahneman and Tversky, Foreseen

This thread began on [the note of one evening](../85-kahneman-tversky-the-first-evening/). In 1979 Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky published **prospect theory**, showing that people depart from rational self-interest in regular, predictable ways: losses loom larger than gains, and the way a choice is framed changes the choice. That is 115 years after the Underground Man mocked the "averages of statistical figures and politico-economical formulas."

They are not saying the same thing. Kahneman and Tversky found biases that are **predictable**, and so can be modeled. The Underground Man claims that if you predicted him, he would do the opposite just to prove you wrong. The closer match in psychology may be **reactance**, Jack Brehm's 1966 term for the push back people feel when their freedom is threatened.

The book also uses the [peak-end rule](../120-peak-end-rule/) on its reader. Its most intense moment is the five-rouble note, and its end is someone else's voice cutting the man off. Dostoevsky designed the last moment.

**Question.** If a model could predict your "irrational" choices, would you feel less free? Would you, like him, be tempted to break the model?

## The Last Word

Dantès comes out of his cell to act, and Faria is the reason. The Underground Man stays in his corner to explain, and has no Faria. The one teacher who comes to his door is Liza, and she teaches him by leaving the money on the table. Twice two makes four. A crumpled five-rouble note left on a table makes something no formula predicts.

## Key facts
- *Notes from Underground* appeared in 1864 in *Epoch*, the magazine Dostoevsky ran with his brother Mikhail. Part I, "Underground," is an argument; Part II, "Apropos of the Wet Snow," is the story of the narrator at twenty-four.
- It opens: "I am a sick man.... I am a spiteful man. I am an unattractive man." The narrator is a forty-year-old retired collegiate assessor; the underground is a state of mind, "to be too conscious is an illness."
- Its central claim: people do not always act in their rational self-interest, and will choose against it to prove they are "a man and not a piano-key." "Twice two makes five is sometimes a very charming thing too."
- The Crystal Palace (London, 1851; rebuilt at Sydenham, seen by Dostoevsky in 1862) stands for the rational utopia of Chernyshevsky's *What Is to Be Done?* (1863), whose ethic was rational egoism.
- In Part II an officer moves him aside in a billiard tavern "without noticing me"; his revenge is a shoulder bump on Nevsky Prospect. It is not a slap.
- Liza leaves the five-rouble note he pushed into her hand; the narrator asks, "which is better—cheap happiness or exalted sufferings?" An editor's note ends the book.
- The book turns the Romantic "sublime and beautiful" into a mask for vanity, and its self-declared anti-hero and unreliable confession mark a hinge toward modernism. Nietzsche found it in 1887; Kaufmann opened his 1956 existentialism anthology with it.
- Echoes: Kafka's insect and "The Burrow," Chekhov's clerks told with restraint, Hamlet's "pale cast of thought," and Dantès, whose cell with the Abbé Faria was a school where the Underground Man's corner is a trap.
- 115 years later, Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory (1979) showed predictable departures from rational choice; the Underground Man claims something wilder, a will that breaks any prediction.
