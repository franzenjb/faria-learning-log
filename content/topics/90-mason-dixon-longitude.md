Yes, Mason and Dixon used Galileo's moons, specifically Io, the innermost one. They did not use them to lay out the boundary itself.

The [Mason-Dixon line](../58-mason-dixon-survey/) is mostly a line of latitude, a fixed distance north of the equator. For the boundary they mainly needed latitude, which they got by timing stars crossing overhead with a zenith sector. They measured distances on the ground with chains and wooden rods, and kept direction using stars and their clocks. They did not carry a chronometer like the K1 that Cook later took to sea. See [Time to longitude](../70-time-to-longitude/) and [Harrison's chronometer](../84-harrison-and-the-marine-chronometer/).

Longitude came in as a side project for the Royal Society. They wintered at John Harlan's farm on the Brandywine in Chester County, from December 1766 to June 1767. There they set up an observatory and timed the moments Jupiter's first moon slipped into Jupiter's shadow. Comparing their local times against the Paris Observatory's predicted times gave the difference in longitude. Mason worked out that Harlan's backyard was 5 hours, 12 minutes and 54 seconds of time west of Paris, which turned out to be only about eight miles off.

They also timed the end of an eclipse of the Moon, a second, rougher way to find longitude. Both appear in their report in the Royal Society's *Philosophical Transactions* for 1768.

There is a link to their earlier work at the Cape. The Astronomer Royal, Nevil Maskelyne, sent them the same Royal Society pendulum clock he had taken to St. Helena and Barbados. They tested how its swing changed in Pennsylvania compared with Greenwich, a measure of how strong gravity is there. That is the same kind of careful astronomy they had done in 1761, when they timed the transit of Venus at the Cape.

The moons themselves are on [Galileo's moons](../64-galileo-jupiters-moons/).

## Key facts
- The Mason-Dixon line is mostly a line of latitude, measured with a zenith sector, chains, and rods.
- They did not carry a marine chronometer like Cook's K1.
- They timed eclipses of Io at John Harlan's farm, from December 1766 to June 1767, as a Royal Society project, not to stake the boundary.
- Mason found Harlan's farm 5 hours, 12 minutes and 54 seconds west of Paris, about eight miles off.
- They also timed the end of a lunar eclipse. Both results are in the *Philosophical Transactions* for 1768.
