Willebrord Snel van Royen, who published as Snellius, was born in Leiden on 13 June 1580 and died there on 30 October 1626. His father, Rudolph Snellius, taught mathematics at the university. Willebrord was pushed toward law and went to mathematics. After his father's death in 1613 he succeeded him in the chair.

Two results carry his name. They are not the same result.

The first is a survey. In 1615 he measured a stretch of meridian in the Netherlands by triangulation: one measured base, then a chain of triangles whose angles fix the other sides. The book is *Eratosthenes Batavus*, the Dutch Eratosthenes. The idea is the one [Eratosthenes](/topics/18-eratosthenes-measures-the-earth/) used for a degree of the Earth, rebuilt with triangles instead of a single paced road. [Picard](/topics/73-picard-triangulation-1669/), half a century later, did the same job between Malvoisine and Amiens with telescopic sights. A network of such triangles, shared from country to country, is the [geodesy lesson](/topics/82-geodetic-networks/).

The law of sines, which that survey uses, is not Snell's law. In a triangle, a side divided by the sine of the opposite angle is the same for all three sides. That is plane trigonometry. Snell's optical law is about a ray crossing from one material into another.

The second result: in 1621 he found that when light passes from one medium into another, the sine of the angle in the first medium, divided by the sine of the angle in the second, is a constant for those two media. Angles are measured from the line perpendicular to the surface, the normal, not from the surface itself. He did not publish the law. Christiaan Huygens printed Snell's result in 1703. René Descartes had already published a form of it. The constant is often indexed as n, a name you may skip until the sines are clear.

The relation was older than Leiden. Ibn Sahl, in Baghdad, wrote the sine law of refraction around 984. Calling the law Snell's is a European habit of naming. It is not a claim that the tenth century had no optics. Say both.

A small numerical picture, invented to show the constant, not taken from a Dutch notebook: if the constant from air into glass is about 1.5, and the sine of the angle in air is 0.75, then the sine in glass is 0.75 divided by 1.5, which is 0.5. An angle whose sine is 0.5 is 30 degrees. So a ray that met the normal at about 49 degrees in air would run at 30 degrees to the normal in that glass. The 1.5 is a round teaching value for common glass. It is not a universal number, and it is not Snell's field note.

## Key facts
- Born in Leiden on 13 June 1580, died there on 30 October 1626. He followed his father in the mathematics chair in 1613.
- *Eratosthenes Batavus* is a triangulation of a Dutch arc, begun from the campaign of 1615.
- The sine law of refraction, angles measured from the normal, was written by Ibn Sahl around 984. Snell found it in 1621 and did not publish it. Huygens printed it in 1703.
- That optical law is not the law of sines inside a survey triangle.
