## The Conjunction Fallacy, and Linda

A combination of two facts cannot be more likely than one of those facts alone. That is the conjunction rule. In 1983, Tversky and Kahneman showed that people often break it, and named the break the conjunction fallacy.

Linda is 31 years old, single, outspoken, and very bright. She majored in philosophy. As a student she was deeply concerned with issues of discrimination and social justice, and also participated in anti-nuclear demonstrations. Which is more likely?

1. Linda is a bank teller.
2. Linda is a bank teller and is active in the feminist movement.

In the most direct version of the problem, 85 percent of 142 undergraduates chose the second statement. The second cannot be more likely. Every feminist bank teller is already a bank teller. The longer description fits the story better, so representativeness makes it feel more probable.

Here is the same point as a count. Imagine 100 women who fit Linda's description. Suppose 8 of them are bank tellers, and 6 of those 8 are also active in the feminist movement. Six cannot be more than eight. The story makes option 2 *feel* larger. The count will not let it be.

Spot it: Ask, "Is this a subset of that? If it is, it cannot be more likely."

Self-test: Cover the story and keep only the two labels, "bank teller" and "bank teller and feminist." Which set can possibly be larger?

This is the tightest pairing in the [hub chart](../138-logical-fallacies-and-kahneman-tversky-biases/): a formal fallacy and a documented bias at once.

## Base-Rate Neglect

You pay too little attention to how common a category is, and too much attention to a specific resemblance or a single witness.

Kahneman and Tversky developed this in "On the Psychology of Prediction" (1973), and returned to it in 1974. A later, widely taught version is the cab problem, from work collected in 1982.

A city has 85 green cabs and 15 blue cabs, out of every 100. A witness says a cab in an accident was blue. The witness is right 80 times out of 100, and wrong 20 times out of 100. How likely is it that the cab was blue?

Many people say "about 80 in 100," as if the witness's accuracy were the whole story. It is not. Start with the 100 cabs. About 85 are green and 15 are blue. Of the 85 green cabs, the witness will wrongly call 20 in 100 of them blue: that is 17 green cabs called blue. Of the 15 blue cabs, the witness will rightly call 80 in 100 of them blue: that is 12 blue cabs called blue. So when the witness says "blue," we have 12 true blues and 17 false blues, 12 out of 29. That is a little over 40 in 100, not 80 in 100.

The witness still matters. The base rate matters too. WYSIATI leaves those 85 green cabs out of view.

Spot it: Ask, "Before I heard this witness or this description, how common was the category?"

Self-test: Can you start from 100 cases and split them into the common group and the rare group, before you apply the witness?

## The Law of Small Numbers

You expect a small sample to look just like the whole, as if the law of large numbers also governed a handful of cases. Tversky and Kahneman named this belief in 1971, and restated it in 1974.

A town has two hospitals. About 45 babies are born each day in the larger one, and about 15 in the smaller one. For a year, each hospital recorded the days on which more than 60 percent of the babies born were boys. Which hospital recorded more such days: the larger, the smaller, or about the same?

Most people say about the same. The smaller hospital will have more of those days. A small count swings more. A large count stays closer to half and half. That is why hasty generalization is a cousin: two rude clerks are a small hospital.

Spot it: Ask, "Is this a big enough sample to look like the whole?"

Self-test: Would you trust a batting average after four at-bats the same way you trust one after four hundred?

## The Gambler's Fallacy, and the Hot Hand

After a run of one outcome, people often feel that the other outcome is "due." That is the gambler's fallacy, and it is already in the 1974 paper. If a fair coin has come up heads five times, the next flip is still one in two. The coin does not keep a tab.

The hot hand is the feeling in the other direction: after a run of hits, the next one feels more likely because the person is "hot." In 1985, Thomas Gilovich, Robert Vallone, and Amos Tversky studied basketball shooting and concluded that, in the records they examined, the hot hand was largely an illusion. Later work, especially by Joshua Miller and Adam Sanjurjo in 2018, has argued that the original analysis was tilted against finding a real hot hand. The 1985 claim is the starting point. The question is not fully closed.

Spot it: Ask, "Is this next event independent of the last ones, or is there a real reason the chance has changed?"

Self-test: After five heads, would you bet more on tails than you would have on the first flip? If yes, the gambler's fallacy is in play. After five baskets, would you raise the chance for the sixth without any other information? That is the hot-hand feeling.

A fair coin has no memory. A tired shooter, or a changed defense, might. The bias is in treating a short run as if it told you more than it can.

## Regression to the Mean, and the Flight Instructors

An extreme result is often followed by a more ordinary one, just because extremes are unusual. That is regression to the mean. It needs no reward and no punishment to happen.

Kahneman tells the story of Israeli flight instructors in *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, and in his 2002 Nobel lecture. The instructors found that praise after a good landing was often followed by a worse one, and that criticism after a poor landing was often followed by a better one. They concluded that praise hurts and criticism helps. A more ordinary landing was likely either way: extremes tend to be followed by something closer to the average.

Spot it: After a peak or a slump, ask, "Would I have expected the next one to be closer to average anyway?"

Self-test: A student scores 98, then 88. Is that a decline you must explain, or the usual settling after an unusually high score?

The instructors were not foolish to want a cause. The tidy story — praise spoils, criticism corrects — is representativeness again. The count does not need a villain.

## Two Questions

1. In the Linda problem, which statement did you pick first, and can you now say why the other statement has to be at least as likely?
2. Think of a great day that was followed by a merely good one. Was that a slump you needed to explain, or regression to the mean?

## Key facts
- In the most direct Linda test, 85 percent of 142 undergraduates judged "bank teller and active in the feminist movement" more probable than "bank teller" (Tversky and Kahneman, 1983). The second statement is a subset of the first, so it cannot be more likely.
- In the cab problem, a witness who is right 80 times in 100 still leaves a little over 40 in 100 once the 85 green cabs are counted. The 80 is not the answer.
- A small hospital will have more days with unusual birth ratios than a large one. Small samples swing more. That is the law of small numbers (1971, 1974).
- The gambler's fallacy treats the next flip as "due." The hot hand treats a streak as a raised chance. The 1985 basketball paper found little support for a hot hand in its records; later work has partly reopened the question.
- Israeli flight instructors saw praise followed by worse landings and criticism by better ones. Kahneman's account is regression to the mean: extremes settle toward the average either way.
