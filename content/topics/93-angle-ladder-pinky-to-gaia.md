Angles on the sky form a ladder, and each rung is sixty times finer than the one above it. That factor of sixty is the old Babylonian way of dividing a circle, the same heritage that gives us sixty minutes in an hour.

A pinky held at arm's length covers about one degree. That is 1°.

A dime, seen face on, at about 200 feet (about 60 meters) covers one arcminute. An arcminute, written 1′, is 1/60 of a degree.

A dime at about 2.3 miles (about 4 kilometers) covers one arcsecond. An arcsecond, written 1″, is 1/60 of an arcminute, or 1/3,600 of a degree.

One arcsecond already reaches outside the solar system. A star whose parallax is one arcsecond sits at a distance of one parsec, about 3.26 light-years.

The European space telescope Gaia measures much finer than that: for bright stars, down to a few tens of microarcseconds. A microarcsecond is one millionth of an arcsecond. That is about the width of a dime as seen from the Moon.

## Key facts
- 1° is about a pinky at arm's length.
- 1′ (one arcminute, 1/60 of a degree) is a dime at about 200 feet, or about 60 meters.
- 1″ (one arcsecond, 1/60 of an arcminute, or 1/3,600 of a degree) is a dime at about 2.3 miles, or about 4 kilometers.
- Each of those three steps is a factor of sixty, from the Babylonian division of the circle.
- A parallax of 1″ is a distance of one parsec, about 3.26 light-years, already outside the solar system.
- Gaia measures tens of microarcseconds for bright stars, about the width of a dime as seen from the Moon.
