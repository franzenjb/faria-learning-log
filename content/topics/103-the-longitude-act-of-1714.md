In the summer of 1714, the British Parliament did something new. It offered a large public reward for solving a scientific problem: how to find a ship's longitude at sea. The law is usually called the Longitude Act. Its full title was "An Act for Providing a Publick Reward for such Person or Persons as shall Discover the Longitude at Sea." It ran for more than a century and shaped the story of the marine clock.

## The Problem

Latitude was easy enough. A sailor could measure the height of the Sun at noon, or of the North Star at night. Longitude was the hard one. You need to know the time at home and the time where you are. One hour of difference is 15 degrees of longitude. The arithmetic is on the [time-to-longitude lesson](../70-time-to-longitude/). In 1714 there was no clock that could keep good time on a rolling ship for weeks.

## The Petition

The push came from two mathematicians, **William Whiston** and **Humphry Ditton**. Whiston had followed Isaac Newton as Lucasian Professor at Cambridge, until he was dismissed in 1710 for his religious views. Ditton taught mathematics at Christ's Hospital in London.

They had a scheme of their own. Signal ships would be anchored at known places at sea. At set times each ship would fire a shell high into the air. A navigator who saw the flash, or heard the bang, could compare times and work out where he was. It was never practical. The open ocean is too deep to anchor in, and you would need ships everywhere.

But they were good at politics. A petition about longitude went to the House of Commons in the spring of 1714. Historians think Whiston and Ditton were behind it. On 10 June 1714 they put out a flyer, "Reasons for a Bill, Proposing a Reward for the Discovery of the Longitude." It came out the day before Members of Parliament were due to discuss the committee's report.

## Newton's Testimony

The Commons committee asked experts for advice. The most famous was Isaac Newton, then about 71 and President of the Royal Society. On 11 June 1714 he went through the possible methods, including the one by a clock. As usually quoted, he said:

> "One [method] is by a Watch to keep time exactly. But, by reason of the motion of the Ship, the Variation of Heat and Cold, Wet and Dry, and the Difference of Gravity in different Latitudes, such a watch hath not yet been made."

The spelling and capitals vary a little between printed versions. The meaning does not. Newton named the other methods too: the eclipses of Jupiter's moons, the position of our Moon, and Whiston and Ditton's plan. He said each method was right in theory but hard in practice.

## What the Act Offered

Queen Anne gave royal assent in July 1714. Royal Museums Greenwich gives the date as 9 July. Some sources give 8 or 20 July. Britain still used the Old Style calendar then.

The Act set three prizes, based on how close the method came to the true longitude:

| Accuracy | In nautical miles | Prize |
|---|---|---|
| Within 1 degree | 60 | £10,000 |
| Within ⅔ of a degree | 40 | £15,000 |
| Within ½ a degree | 30 | £20,000 |

The Act measured distance in "geographical miles," which are what we now call nautical miles, about 1.15 ordinary miles each. A degree of longitude is 60 nautical miles only at the equator. Farther north it is shorter, but the prize was set in degrees.

The test was a voyage from Great Britain to a port in the **West Indies**, chosen by the Commissioners. The method had to work on that trip. The Act also allowed up to **£2,000** to help promising inventors pay for experiments.

## The Commissioners

The Act named a group of **Commissioners for the Discovery of the Longitude at Sea**. They included naval officers, politicians, and scientists such as the Astronomer Royal and the Lucasian Professor. Later they were called the **Board of Longitude**.

For years they hardly met. There is no clear record of a meeting until about 1737, when John Harrison came forward with his first sea clock, H1. Parliament later changed the rules several times. In 1774 a new Act cut the top prize to £10,000 and made the tests stricter. The Board was abolished in 1828.

## What Was Actually Paid

No one ever received the full £20,000 prize as written in 1714. But a good deal of money was paid out.

- **John Harrison** got the most. He received many payments over the years, from the Board for his clocks and from Parliament. In 1765 an Act provided £10,000 once he explained how his watch H4 worked. In 1773, after a long fight, Parliament gave him a further £8,750. One common total for all his rewards is about £23,000. It was still not the prize itself. See [Harrison and the marine chronometer](../84-harrison-and-the-marine-chronometer/).
- **Tobias Mayer**, a German astronomer, made lunar tables good enough to find longitude from the Moon. He died in 1762. His widow received £3,000.
- **Leonhard Euler** received £300 for the math that helped Mayer's tables.
- Many smaller sums went to instrument makers and astronomers for experiments, tables and the *Nautical Almanac*.

## Why It Matters

The Act did not solve longitude by itself. Two methods won out in the end: Harrison's clock and the lunar method. The Board paid for both. It was an early case of a government paying for science with prize money. The [Scilly disaster of 1707](../102-scilly-1707/) is often called its cause, but historians debate that link.

## Key facts
- The Longitude Act received royal assent in July 1714. Royal Museums Greenwich gives 9 July.
- The prizes were £10,000 for 1 degree, £15,000 for ⅔ of a degree, and £20,000 for ½ a degree.
- The test voyage was from Great Britain to a port in the West Indies.
- William Whiston and Humphry Ditton pushed for the Act. Their own signal-ship plan never worked.
- Newton told the committee on 11 June 1714 that "such a watch hath not yet been made."
- The Commissioners became known as the Board of Longitude. It was abolished in 1828.
- No one ever received the full £20,000 prize. Harrison received about £23,000 in many payments; Mayer's widow received £3,000.
