The question comes up in every class: if the British capital is London, why is zero longitude called the Greenwich meridian? The short answer is that Greenwich *is* London. It is a borough on the south bank of the Thames, about five miles east of the city center. The line takes its name from the building it is measured from, not from the city around it.

## A King's Observatory for Longitude

In 1674 a Frenchman, the Sieur de St. Pierre, told Charles II's court that he could find longitude at sea from the Moon's position against the stars. A royal commission looked at the claim. Its youngest member, John Flamsteed, pointed out the flaw: nobody had star positions or Moon tables good enough to make the method work.

The King's answer was to pay for them. On 4 March 1675 a royal warrant made Flamsteed the King's "astronomical observator," the first Astronomer Royal. His job was "to find out the so much-desired longitude of places for the perfecting the art of navigation." A second warrant, on 22 June 1675, ordered "a small observatory within our park at Greenwich, upon the highest ground." Christopher Wren designed it on the foundations of an old castle on the hill. Flamsteed laid the foundation stone on 10 August 1675.

So the Royal Observatory was built for one problem: longitude. Every star it measured was measured from its own telescopes, and so from its own meridian.

## How a Building Became the World's Zero

- **Star tables.** For two centuries the Astronomers Royal published positions measured at Greenwich. Anyone using them was counting from Greenwich.
- **The almanac.** Nevil Maskelyne, the fifth Astronomer Royal, published the *Nautical Almanac* for 1767. Its lunar distances were given in Greenwich time. A navigator who used it was finding longitude east or west of Greenwich. See [lunar distances](../105-lunar-distances-werner-to-maskelyne/).
- **The charts.** British Admiralty charts, sold to ships of every nation, counted longitude from Greenwich.
- **The vote.** By 1884 most of the world's shipping already used Greenwich. The [International Meridian Conference](../106-the-1884-meridian-conference/) in Washington made official what sailors were already doing.

## Which Line, Exactly?

Greenwich has had more than one meridian. Each new Astronomer Royal who installed a new main telescope set a new line a few yards from the last. Halley's and Bradley's lines are still marked in the observatory. The one the world adopted runs through the cross-hairs of the **Airy Transit Circle**, the telescope George Airy installed in 1851. The brass strip in the courtyard, where tourists stand with one foot in each hemisphere, marks that line.

And there is one more twist. A GPS receiver standing on the brass strip does not read 0°. The modern zero is about 335 feet east. That story is on [Plumb Line or Center of Mass?](../112-plumb-line-vs-center-of-mass/).

## Key facts
- Greenwich is a London borough on the south bank of the Thames, about five miles east of the city center.
- A claim by the Sieur de St. Pierre to find longitude by the Moon led to a royal commission in 1674–1675.
- Charles II appointed John Flamsteed the first Astronomer Royal on 4 March 1675, "to find out the so much-desired longitude of places."
- A second warrant, 22 June 1675, ordered the observatory built in Greenwich Park; Christopher Wren designed it, and the foundation stone was laid 10 August 1675.
- The Nautical Almanac for 1767 gave lunar distances in Greenwich time, which spread Greenwich longitude to ships everywhere.
- The prime meridian runs through the Airy Transit Circle of 1851, not through earlier Greenwich lines.
