Most of us were taught that virtue sits on one side and vice on the other. Aristotle thought that was too simple. Vice comes in pairs. Courage is not the opposite of cowardice alone. Recklessness is a vice too. The virtue is the proportion between them.

He set this out in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Books II through IV. It is often called the doctrine of the mean. The popular name "golden mean" came later, and it has nothing to do with the golden ratio in geometry. The idea itself is practical. These are tools for a Tuesday afternoon, not ornaments for a shelf. A short account of the same idea sits in [Aristotle: Master of Those Who Know](../126-aristotle-master-of-those-who-know/). This page is the standalone lesson.

## Not a Midpoint

The mean is not a mathematical middle. Aristotle says it is "the mean relative to us," in W. D. Ross's 1925 translation. His example is a meal. A huge portion may be right for a champion wrestler and far too much for a beginner. The amount halfway between too much and too little is not, by itself, the right meal for either person. What is courageous for a trained rescuer is not what is courageous for someone untrained. The measure is the person, the moment, and the task.

The mean is also often closer to one extreme than to the other. Recklessness — Ross's word is rashness — is "liker and nearer to courage," and cowardice more unlike it. We also drift more naturally toward some errors than others: toward comfort, toward applause, toward the outburst or the silence that comes easily to us. Hitting the mean is hard, he says, because "men are good in but one way, but bad in many."

## Both Extremes Are Lies

Here is the plain-English framing. Both extremes are lies. The virtue is proportion. The coward tells a lie about the danger: that it cannot be faced. The reckless person tells the opposite lie: that the danger need not be measured. Courage tells the truth about the danger and still goes, with a plan. The same pattern holds for each of the seven virtues below. This phrasing is the lesson's, not a sentence from Aristotle.

## The Seven Virtues

**Courage**, between cowardice and recklessness. In disaster response, the coward stays in the truck when a family is still in the water. The reckless responder rushes into a current without a line or a plan, and becomes a second victim. Courage is going in with a plan, a partner, and a way back.

**Temperance**, between insensibility and self-indulgence. At the table, self-indulgence is another helping because the food is there, or another drink after you have had enough. Insensibility is treating every ordinary pleasure as a trap. Temperance is appetite in proportion: enough, and then you stop.

**Generosity**, which Aristotle also calls liberality, between stinginess and wastefulness. A colleague's child is in the hospital. Stinginess looks away when you could easily help. Wastefulness empties the account and then cannot cover the rent. Generosity gives what you can, to the right person, without ruining yourself.

**Magnificence**, between cheapness and vulgarity. This is generosity at a large public scale: a shelter dedication after a storm, or a workplace ceremony that ought to honor the work. Cheapness makes a public gesture so thin it signals contempt. Vulgarity makes a splash that is really about the giver. Magnificence spends what the occasion actually calls for, and does it with taste.

**Proper pride**, also called magnanimity, between undue humility and empty vanity. At work, undue humility shrinks from credit you earned, and hides the work from people who need to know it was done. Empty vanity takes credit for a team effort, or inflates a title. Proper pride is a clear, accurate sense of what you have done, and what you still owe.

**Good temper**, between a spiritless response and a short temper. A late contractor, a missed delivery, a volunteer who did not show. A short temper blows up and poisons the room. A spiritless response lets the same harm happen again. Good temper is anger in proportion: you name the problem, hold the line, and do not enjoy the heat.

**Truthfulness**, between self-deprecation and boastfulness. Aristotle's word for the first extreme is *eironeia*, mock-modesty. On a résumé, or in a briefing after a storm, boastfulness pads the numbers. Mock-modesty pretends you did less than you did, which is also a lie. Truthfulness says what happened, no more and no less.

**Question.** Think of a recent decision at work or at home. Which extreme were you leaning toward, and what would the proportion have looked like?

**Question.** Aristotle says the mean is often closer to one extreme, and that we drift more naturally toward some errors than others. Which of these seven is hardest for you, and which extreme do you tend to?

## The Last Word

The mean is not a bland middle. It is the right amount, for this person, in this moment. That is not a theory to admire from a distance. It is a tool you can use today, at the table, at work, and in the next hard conversation. Both extremes are lies. The truth is proportion.

## Key facts

- The doctrine of the mean is set out in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Books II–IV. Quotations follow W. D. Ross, 1925, public domain in the United States.
- The mean is "relative to us," not a mathematical midpoint, and is often closer to one extreme than to the other.
- The seven virtues treated here are courage, temperance, generosity (liberality), magnificence, proper pride (magnanimity), good temper, and truthfulness.
- Both extremes are lies; the virtue is proportion. That phrasing is the lesson's, not a direct quotation.
- "Golden mean" is a later popular name. It is not Aristotle's phrase, and it is not the golden ratio.
