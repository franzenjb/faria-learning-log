Every map we use today hangs on a grid: latitude north and south, longitude east and west. That grid was not always there. Two Greek astronomers, about 300 years apart, built it. Hipparchus said where a place is should be settled by the sky, not by travelers' tales. Ptolemy then wrote down thousands of places as pairs of numbers. His book was lost to western Europe for centuries, and its return in the 1400s helped shape the voyages that followed.

## Hipparchus Asks for Proof From the Sky

Hipparchus worked on Rhodes, roughly 160 to 125 BC. He is the astronomer who measured the Moon's distance on the [lunar parallax](../63-hipparchus-lunar-parallax/) page. He also wrote a book attacking the map of Eratosthenes, the man who [measured the Earth](../18-eratosthenes-measures-the-earth/). That book is lost. We know it mainly through the geographer Strabo, who quotes it.

Hipparchus's complaint was simple. Eratosthenes had placed many towns from reported distances and directions. Hipparchus said a map should rest on observation of the sky.

- **Latitude** could come from the height of the pole, or from the length of a noon shadow. Strabo preserves a list of Hipparchus's parallels, the east-west bands, with their distances in stadia.
- **Longitude** was harder. Strabo reports Hipparchus saying that the only way to learn the longitudes of places is from eclipses.

## Why a Lunar Eclipse Is a Clock

A lunar eclipse happens at the same instant for everyone who can see it. Earth's shadow touches the Moon once, not once for each town. But the local clock time is different in each town, because local time follows the Sun. Earth turns 15° every hour. So if the eclipse starts at 9 p.m. local time in one city and 11 p.m. in another, the second city is two hours, or 30°, farther east.

It was a correct idea. It was almost never carried out. You needed two careful observers, far apart, both watching the same eclipse and both keeping good local time. That rarely happened.

Ptolemy, writing later, gives the one ancient example he had: an eclipse seen at Arbela, in what is now Iraq, at the fifth hour of the night, and at Carthage at the second hour. That eclipse is usually identified with the one 11 days before Alexander the Great's battle near Arbela, in 331 BC. Three hours means 45°. The true gap is closer to 34°, or about 2¼ hours. Some scholars doubt the two timings were ever really made.

Historians also warn against giving Hipparchus too much. He argued for fixing places by astronomy, and he used latitude bands. Whether he gave individual towns numbered coordinates in degrees is not clear from what survives. The full grid, with numbers for every place, is first clearly seen in Ptolemy.

## Ptolemy's Geography

Claudius Ptolemy worked in Alexandria, in Egypt, around AD 150. He is best known for the *Almagest*, his astronomy. His *Geography* is a guide to drawing a map of the known world. It has two parts:

- Instructions for map projections, ways to draw a round Earth on a flat sheet.
- A long list of places with a latitude and a longitude for each. The usual figure is **about 8,000** places, though counts differ with what is counted: towns, river mouths, capes, mountains.

Latitude was counted from the equator, as now. Longitude needed a starting line, a **prime meridian**. Ptolemy chose the **Fortunate Isles**, the westernmost land he knew, generally identified with the **Canary Islands**. Everything else was so many degrees east of them. His world ran about 180° of longitude, from the Canaries to China.

Only a small share of those coordinates came from real sky measurements. Most were worked out from itineraries, sailing times and earlier maps, especially the lost work of Marinus of Tyre. They look precise. Many are not.

## Ptolemy's Too-Small Earth

Eratosthenes had found the Earth to be about 252,000 stadia around, or 700 stadia to a degree. Ptolemy instead used 500 stadia to a degree, so 180,000 stadia around, a figure usually traced back to Posidonius. That is roughly 30% smaller than Eratosthenes. Because ancient stadia were not all the same length, the exact size of the error in miles is still argued.

The small degree did harm. When a known distance in stadia was turned into degrees, it covered too many degrees. Asia stretched too far east. The Mediterranean came out too long, by roughly 20°.

## The Return to Europe

The *Geography* survived in Greek, in the Byzantine world. Around 1400 a Greek manuscript reached Florence. The scholar Jacopo Angeli translated it into Latin, finishing around 1406 to 1410. Printed editions followed: Vicenza in 1475, without maps; Bologna in 1477, with maps; Ulm in 1482, with a full set of maps.

Suddenly European scholars had a whole world on a grid of numbers, with a small Earth and a long Asia. That picture fed the argument that Asia could be reached by sailing west. Columbus took it further, preferring even more stretched estimates of Asia and an even smaller Earth. The real story of what educated people believed in 1492 is on [the flat-Earth myth and Columbus](../21-flat-earth-myth-and-columbus/).

The grid itself outlived the errors. Prime meridians moved, first to many national lines and then, from 1884, to Greenwich. But a place named by a latitude and a longitude is still Ptolemy's idea.

## Key facts
- Hipparchus, around 150 BC, argued that places should be fixed by observing the sky, and that lunar eclipses could give longitude.
- A lunar eclipse happens at one instant for everyone. The difference in local times, at 15° per hour, gives the difference in longitude.
- Ptolemy's one example, Arbela and Carthage, gives 3 hours, or 45°. The true difference is about 34°.
- Ptolemy's Geography, around AD 150, lists about 8,000 places by latitude and longitude.
- Ptolemy's prime meridian ran through the Fortunate Isles, usually identified with the Canary Islands.
- Ptolemy used 500 stadia per degree, 180,000 stadia around, against Eratosthenes' 252,000. His Earth was too small and his Asia too long.
- Jacopo Angeli's Latin translation, around 1406 to 1410, and printed editions from 1475 brought the Geography back to western Europe.
