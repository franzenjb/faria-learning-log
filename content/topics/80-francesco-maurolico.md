Francesco Maurolico was born in Messina, in the Kingdom of Sicily, on 16 September 1494, and died there on 22 July 1575. The family was Greek. His father, Antonio, had left Constantinople, studied with Constantine Lascaris in Messina, practiced as a physician, and became master of the Messina mint. Francesco learned Greek, mathematics, and astronomy at home, and grammar from a priest in the city. He was ordained in 1521. Disease then killed his father, two brothers, and his sister. He left the city for a time.

He was not the eldest son at birth. Older brothers died, and he inherited enough to study, though printing still needed patrons. In 1528 he published a grammar dedicated to Ettore Pignatelli, and the governor of Messina, Giovanni Marullo, asked him to lecture on Sacrobosco's Sphere and Euclid's *Elements*. He finished a rearrangement of part of Euclid on 9 July 1532. It waited until the *Opuscula Mathematica* of 1575 to be printed.

In *Cosmographia*, written as three dialogues and published in 1543, he gave methods for measuring the Earth that later workers, including Picard, could use. He also refused a moving Earth. He wrote that fickleness had grown so far that some would say the Earth turns while the heavens stand still. In a later address to the reader of *De Sphaera* he said Copernicus, who held the Sun still and the Earth in circular motion, deserved a whip rather than a refutation. That sentence is his. It is not a modern verdict. A nineteenth-century Sicilian writer, Domenico Scinà, said plainly that Maurolico had not escaped the common view of his time. Report both. Do not sand the insult off, and do not pretend the insult is an argument.

He became a Benedictine in 1550. Simone Ventimiglia gave him the abbey of Santa Maria del Parto at Castelbuono. He observed from a tower on that estate. He returned to Messina, taught mathematics at the Jesuit college from 1569, the contract including music as a branch of mathematics, and served the mint and the fortifications. He wrote a history of Sicily, published in 1562.

The mathematical work is the reason he is here. He translated and restored Theodosius, Menelaus, Autolycus, Euclid, Apollonius, and Archimedes from incomplete texts. In 1547 he restored books of Apollonius's *Conics* from the preface's hints. They were printed in 1654, long after his death. In the *Arithmeticorum libri duo*, written in 1557 and printed with the *Opuscula*, he proves facts about triangular and square numbers by showing a step from one whole number to the next. MacTutor records that the sum of the first n odd numbers equals the nth square, and that the proof steps from n to the next number. That pattern is mathematical induction, written before the name was common.

In the *Theodosii* of 1558 he gives a table of secants and the relations secant equals radius divided by cosine, and secant equals radius times tangent, divided by sine. Delambre once credited him with the first secant table. MacTutor notes that a secant table had already appeared in Copernicus. The identities are still his to teach.

*Photismi de lumine*, mostly written in 1554 and left with Christopher Clavius in 1574, treats light and shadow, the rainbow, and lenses. He takes the crystalline humor of the eye to be a lens with two outward curves. On the rainbow he asks why the altitude is a little under 45 degrees, and he offers, as a guess he does not claim to know, that the drops may not be perfect spheres. That is a question, not a measurement of a drop.

He saw the new star in Cassiopeia in 1572, the star Tycho made famous. A manuscript dated 6 November 1572, five days before Tycho's recorded observations, was found in 1960 by C. Doris Hellman. Christopher Clavius had published some of Maurolico's notes. The full sheet had waited. The star is the same star. The [Tycho lesson](/topics/74-tycho-brahe-uraniborg-and-stjerneborg/) tells the Danish observations. It does not erase the Sicilian ones.

The *Opuscula* carries a publisher's dedication dated 26 July 1575, four days after Maurolico died. Much else waited longer: mechanics in 1613, a center-of-gravity study in 1685, more manuscripts in 1876 and even 1995. A life of books is not the same as a life of books in print.

## Key facts
- Born in Messina on 16 September 1494, died there on 22 July 1575. Ordained in 1521, a Benedictine from 1550.
- He restored Greek mathematics, including Apollonius on conics, and proved number facts by a step from n to the next number.
- He rejected a moving Earth in harsh words. That rejection is part of the record.
- He observed the 1572 star in Cassiopeia. A manuscript of 6 November 1572 was identified in 1960.
