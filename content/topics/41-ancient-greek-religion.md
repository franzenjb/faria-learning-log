## A religion with no name
It had **no name**. The Greeks didn't treat "religion" as something separate from life, so they had no word for their own. The closest were ***ta theia***, "the divine things," and ***eusebeia***, "piety," proper reverence for the gods. There was **no holy book, no creed and no single church**: only the rituals, festivals and stories each city kept, with **Homer and Hesiod** as the shared reference.

Scholars today call it **ancient Greek religion** or **Hellenic polytheism** (*Hellēn* "Greek," *poly* "many," *theos* "god"). The Roman version is called Roman religion.

## "Pagan," "heathen" and "myth"
**Pagan** comes from Latin ***paganus***, "country person, villager," from ***pagus***, "rural district." Christianity spread first in the cities, so those who kept to the old gods were the country folk. In effect, "pagan" first meant "hick." **Heathen** may have the same story in English: people of the heath, the open countryside. The only name the old religion ever got was given by its rivals, and it meant "the people out in the sticks."

**Myth** comes from Greek ***mythos***, which first meant simply "word" or "story." Only later did it come to mean "a story that isn't true," largely because Christians used it against the old religion.

## How old was it?
- **Before the Greeks, around 1400 BC:** Mycenaean traces at Delphi. Myth says Delphi first belonged to **Gaia**, the Earth, guarded by the serpent **Python**; **Apollo killed Python** and took over, which is the origin of the name **Pythia**. Many scholars read this as an older earth cult being replaced.
- **Linear B** clay tablets from Mycenaean palaces already name **Zeus, Hera, Poseidon and Dionysus**.
- **The 700s BC to about 390 AD:** the oracle of Apollo at its height, about **1,100 years**.
- The religion overall lasted about **1,800 years**, close to the time from Jesus to today.

## Greek or Roman? Both
It was Greek first and became both. Delphi was always a Greek sanctuary, run in Greek, but for its last five centuries Rome ruled and honored it.
- From about 500 BC Rome absorbed the Greek gods: **Zeus as Jupiter, Hera as Juno, Aphrodite as Venus**. **Apollo** is the exception: Rome kept his Greek name.
- Rome sent delegations to Delphi as early as the **500s and 400s BC**.
- After Rome conquered Greece in **146 BC**, emperors like **Augustus** and **Hadrian** paid for repairs and visited.
- **Plutarch** was both a Greek priest at Delphi and a Roman citizen: the "both" in one man. One religion seen through two languages, which is the idea behind the *Parallel Lives*.

## Plutarch's own beliefs
Delphi is far older than Christianity, but Plutarch lived in the first century AD, while **Paul was preaching in Greece** and the **Gospels were being written**. He **never mentions Christianity** once. For him Apollo and Zeus were the living religion of his world. He was a **Platonist** who believed in **one supreme, perfect divine being**, and saw the stories of the gods as partly poetic veils over it. He said outright that some myths were **crude** and not to be taken literally.

Question: Plutarch believed in one perfect God behind a crowd of stories. How far is that from the Christianity that was about to replace his world?

## Will Christianity be called a myth?
Jeff's point: **"In a thousand years some might consider Christianity a myth."**

History gives that weight. Every religion of the ancient Mediterranean that once seemed permanent is now called "mythology." **Egypt's gods lasted about 3,000 years**, longer than Christianity has existed, and are now in museums and cartoons.

But religions are **absorbed as well as dying**. Christianity took over Greek philosophy, Roman festivals and even sacred sites; Delphi's ruins have Christian buildings among them. A thousand years from now Christianity might be called a myth, or live on inside whatever comes next, the way Plato lives on inside it.

> **The word "myth" usually just marks whose story lost.**

Question: which lasts longer, what a religion teaches about how to live, or its story about the gods? Plutarch would say the first: his moral essays are still read; nobody prays to his Apollo.

## Does every religion become a myth?
Not every one, but most. **Those tied to a fallen state did:** Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece and Rome, the Norse, the Aztecs. **The survivors:**
- **Hinduism**: the Vedas from about 1500 BC; about a billion people.
- **Judaism**: about 3,000 years; it moved its center from the Temple to a book and a way of life.
- **Zoroastrianism**: about 100,000–200,000 followers, still alive after about 3,000 years.

**The pattern:** text plus community plus daily practice outlasts temples and kings. Christianity, Islam and Buddhism have those survival traits, but the future is unknown. (Continued on the [Nov 3 session](/sessions/2026-11-03/).)

## Key facts
- No native name; *ta theia* ("divine things"), *eusebeia* ("piety"); no book, creed or church.
- "Pagan" < Latin *paganus*, "villager"; "myth" < *mythos*, "word, story."
- Linear B names Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Dionysus; ~1,800 years overall.
- Rome: Zeus → Jupiter, Hera → Juno, Aphrodite → Venus; Apollo stayed Apollo.
