Ariel is not human. In the last act of *The Tempest*, this is the spirit who has to remind Prospero how a human being ought to feel. The prisoners are in tears. Ariel, who is "but air," says that seeing them, "your affections would become tender." Then: "Mine would, sir, were I human." Prospero, who can raise a storm and hunt men with invisible hounds, answers, "And mine shall." The line the play has been walking toward comes next: "The rarer action is in virtue than in vengeance."

The storm that opens the play is the easy part. What the play is about is the price of power, and the harder act of laying it down.

Prospero was Duke of Milan. He buried himself in his books, and his brother Antonio, with help from the King of Naples, stole the dukedom and set him adrift with little Miranda, then three years old. They washed up on a remote island, with food and books slipped to them by a kind counselor, Gonzalo. Twelve years later, the same enemies sail within reach. Prospero is ready for them. He is not the first magician to have ruled the place.

## Sycorax and the pine

We never meet Sycorax. We hear her only through Prospero, in Act 1, Scene 2, when Ariel starts asking about freedom. She was a witch from Argier — Algiers — banished and left on the island already with child. Ariel was her servant and refused her "earthy and abhorred commands," so she imprisoned the spirit in a cloven pine. She died and left Ariel there "a dozen years." Prospero's art "made gape the pine and let thee out." Freedom, though, is not the same as being free. Ariel now serves Prospero. The magician promises to discharge him in two days, and threatens, if the spirit murmurs, to rend an oak and peg him in it for twelve winters. The same prison, a different tree.

## Caliban, and a claim that will not go away

Caliban is Sycorax's son. He says, almost as soon as he enters, "This island's mine by Sycorax, my mother, which thou tak'st from me." He remembers a kinder beginning: Prospero stroked him, taught him to name the sun and the moon, and was shown in return the springs of the isle. Then, he says, he was used and confined to a rock. Prospero's reason is just as plain. He lodged Caliban in his own cell "till thou didst seek to violate the honor of my child." Caliban does not deny it. He wishes it had been done, and says he would have "peopled else this isle with Calibans."

In the First Folio, it is Miranda who answers, "I pitied thee, took pains to make thee speak." Some later editors gave that speech to her father. Caliban's reply is the line no one forgets: "You taught me language, and my profit on 't is I know how to curse."

Both things can be true at once. Caliban was there first, and he was treated kindly, and he did try to assault Miranda. The play will not let you pick only the part that makes one of them simple. He is also the speaker of one of the play's most beautiful speeches — "Be not afeard. The isle is full of noises" — and, later, the conspirator who takes a drunk butler for a god, follows Ariel's music through briers into a filthy pool, and falls for a trap of shining garments. At the end he sees it: "I'll be wise hereafter and seek for grace." Prospero, presenting him to the lords, says, "This thing of darkness I acknowledge mine." What becomes of Caliban when the ships leave, the play does not say.

## Ariel's jobs, and the one he gives his master

Follow Ariel through the five acts and the plot becomes a list of assignments. The spirit raises the storm, then reports that not a hair has perished, and that the King's ship is safely in harbor. Invisible, Ariel sings Ferdinand to Miranda. In Act 2, when Antonio and Sebastian draw swords over the sleeping King and Gonzalo, Ariel sings in Gonzalo's ear and the murder plot fails. In Act 3 the spirit helps lay a banquet, makes it vanish, and appears as a harpy to name "three men of sin." In Act 4 Ariel presents a wedding masque for the lovers, then leads Caliban's crew into the pool, hangs out the glittering clothes, and hunts them with hounds.

In Act 5 the spirit does something that was not on the list. After reporting Gonzalo's tears, Ariel tells Prospero that his affections, seeing the prisoners, would become tender — "Mine would, sir, were I human." Only then does Prospero abjure "this rough magic," promise to break his staff and drown his book, have the ship brought back, promise "calm seas, auspicious gales," and set Ariel "to the elements" free.

He releases Ariel because he said he would, because the spirit has earned more than the bargain, and because the staff, the book, the servant, and the revenge are one apparatus. He cannot drown the book and keep the spirit on a chain. Then he turns to us. In the Epilogue his charms are "all o'erthrown," and he asks the audience, "Let your indulgence set me free."

It is often said that this is Shakespeare saying goodbye to the stage. The reading is attractive, and it is not a fact. *The Tempest* was probably written in 1610 or 1611 and stands first in the 1623 Folio. Shakespeare almost certainly still had a hand, after this, in *Henry VIII* and *The Two Noble Kinsmen*. He may have felt something of what Prospero says. We do not know that he wrote the Epilogue in order to tell us so.

One later reading deserves a fair place. From the mid-twentieth century especially, Caliban has been seen as a figure of the colonized subject: his island taken, his language imposed, his labor extracted. George Lamming (1960) and Aimé Césaire (1969) are the central later writers. The play gives that reading real lines to stand on. It also gives the assault on Miranda, and a geography that is Mediterranean, not, on its face, the New World. The pattern of dispossession is in the text. It is not the only thing the play is about.

Miranda's first sight of the shipwrecked lords, and the "brave new world" she thinks she sees, has its own short page: [Miranda's First Sight of Mankind — The Tempest](../130-mirandas-first-sight-of-mankind-the-tempest/).

**Question.** When Prospero says, "This thing of darkness I acknowledge mine," is he claiming a slave, admitting a responsibility, or recognizing something in himself?

**Question.** If you have used someone well and then used them, what would have to be true for their release to count as justice, and not only as a convenient ending?

## The Last Word

The rarer action was not the storm. It was letting the spirit go.

## Key facts

- *The Tempest* is usually dated 1610 or 1611. It was performed at court on 1 November 1611 and stands first in the 1623 First Folio.
- Sycorax, from Argier (Algiers), imprisoned Ariel in a cloven pine for twelve years. Prospero freed the spirit and then commanded him, promising discharge and threatening an oak if he complained (1.2).
- Caliban, Sycorax's son, claims the island by his mother. Prospero enslaves him after the attempted assault on Miranda, which Caliban does not deny. "You taught me language, and my profit on 't is I know how to curse" (1.2).
- In the Folio, the "Abhorred slave" speech is Miranda's. Some later editions gave it to Prospero.
- The turn of the play is Ariel's "Mine would, sir, were I human" (5.1). Prospero then chooses virtue over vengeance, drowns his book, and sets Ariel free. The Epilogue asks the audience to do the same for him.
