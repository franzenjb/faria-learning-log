## The navel of the world
Delphi was the holiest site in ancient Greece. It sits high on the slopes of **Mount Parnassus** and held the **temple of Apollo** and his oracle. The Greeks called it the ***omphalos***, the "navel" of the world. In the myth, Zeus released two eagles from opposite ends of the earth, and they met there.

The temple carried three short sayings. The best known is the Delphic maxim:

> **"Know thyself"** (*gnōthi seauton*)

That suits Plutarch, who was a priest there: the *Lives* are one long exercise in knowing yourself by studying other people.

**Etymology:** the name comes from *delphys*, "womb," and is related to *delphis*, "dolphin." In one myth Apollo took the form of a dolphin to bring his first priests there.

## The Pythia
The oracle was not one woman who lived forever. **"The Pythia" was a job title**, held by a succession of real women over **about 1,000 years**; when one died, another was chosen. Plutarch, who worked beside them, says that in his day she was a **local peasant woman of good character**, not educated or high-born. At first the Pythia was young; later she was a **woman over fifty dressed as a girl**. At Delphi's busiest, **three Pythias** took turns.

## A visit to the oracle
- She answered only on certain days: at first **once a year**, later **once a month**, and **never in winter**, when Apollo was said to be away.
- You paid a **fee** and offered a sacrifice, usually a **goat**. The priests sprinkled it with **cold water**; if it didn't **shiver**, the god was unwilling and you went home.
- The Pythia purified herself in the **Castalian spring**, went down into the ***adyton*** (the "do-not-enter" room) under the temple, and sat on a **bronze tripod** over a **crack in the rock**, holding **laurel**, Apollo's plant.
- You asked your question **through a priest**. She went into a trance and answered, and the priests often **put her words into verse**.

## The vapor and the faults
Ancient writers said a sweet-smelling vapor, the ***pneuma***, rose from the crack and brought on the trance. Plutarch himself thought the vapor was **real and weakening**, which he gave as one reason the oracle was declining.

In **2001**, geologists found **two faults crossing right under the temple**, and traces of **ethylene**, a sweet-smelling gas that can cause a trance-like state, in a nearby spring. The ethylene is still debated; the faults are real. The most famous holy place in Greece may have been set by a fault line.

Question: if the priests shaped her words into verse, who really spoke for Apollo, the woman in the trance or the men writing it down?

## The great stories
**Croesus tests the oracles.** Croesus, king of Lydia ("rich as Croesus"), tested several oracles by having messengers ask, on the **hundredth day**, what the king was doing at that moment. Only Delphi got it right: he was **boiling a tortoise and a lamb together in a bronze pot**. So he trusted Delphi, sent fabulous gold, and was told that if he attacked Persia **"a great empire would fall."** One did: **his own**. Herodotus tells this story.

**The wooden wall.** In **480 BC** Xerxes invaded Greece. Athens got a terrifying answer, then a second: trust in the "wooden wall," and

> "divine Salamis, you will destroy the children of women."

The old men said it meant the wooden fence around the Acropolis. **Themistocles** argued it meant **ships**. Athens abandoned the city, put everything on the fleet, and crushed the Persian navy at **Salamis**. One reading of one sentence may have saved Western history. Plutarch tells it in his *Life of Themistocles*.

**Socrates, the wisest man.** A friend asked whether anyone was wiser than Socrates. The Pythia answered no one. Socrates, who knew he knew nothing, questioned people who claimed to be wise and found they didn't know either. He decided his only wisdom was knowing that he didn't know. That questioning is the **Socratic method**, and it may have started with this answer.

**Oedipus.** The oracle tells Oedipus he will kill his father and marry his mother. He runs away to avoid it, and running away is exactly how it comes true. That is the Greek idea of fate: trying to escape it brings it about.

**The treasuries.** Cities built small marble treasuries along the Sacred Way to show off their gifts, a kind of competitive generosity. The **Athenian treasury** still stands, rebuilt, and holds the **earliest notated music** we have: **two hymns to Apollo** carved in stone with notes above the words.

The pattern: the oracle rarely told you what to do. It handed you a riddle and made you reason, which is why it lasted. Question: if its answers were so ambiguous, why did the smartest Greeks keep coming back for a thousand years?

## The decline
- Rome sacked Delphi more than once: **Sulla** plundered its treasures in **86 BC**, and **Nero** is said to have carried off **500 bronze statues**.
- Plutarch's essay **"On the Obsolescence of Oracles"** asks why the shrines had grown so quiet. In it he tells of the ship's pilot **Thamus**, who sailing past the island of Paxi heard a voice telling him to announce that **"Great Pan is dead."** When he did, a great wailing rose from the shore. Later Christians read it as the old gods dying at Christ's coming.
- **Theodosius** banned pagan worship in **391–392 AD**, and the oracle fell silent. A late legend gives its last answer:

> "Tell the king the fair-wrought hall has fallen... the speaking water is quenched."

- **Earthquakes**, from the same faults, brought the temples down, and **landslides** from Parnassus buried them.

## The village on top
A Greek village, **Kastri**, grew over the ruins, its houses built from the **old marble**; ancient inscriptions ended up in garden walls and goat pens. Travelers in the 1600s and 1700s came looking for Delphi and found peasants living on it. **Lord Byron** visited in **1809** and **carved his name** on a column in the gymnasium.

## The rediscovery
In **1891 France won the right to dig**, partly by agreeing to **buy Greek currants**. Greece moved the **entire village about 1 km west**; the new Kastri is the modern town of Delphi. The **French School at Athens** dug from **1892 to 1903**, the **"Great Excavation,"** finding:
- the Athenian treasury (rebuilt) and the hymns with musical notes
- the temple foundations and the theater
- the **Charioteer of Delphi**, a life-size bronze from about **470 BC**, buried by the **373 BC earthquake**. That burial is why it survived when nearly every other ancient bronze was melted down. His inlaid eyes still look at you.

**The earthquakes that destroyed Delphi also preserved its greatest treasure**, a pattern any disaster person will recognize.

## Key facts
- Delphi: Mount Parnassus; temple of Apollo; the *omphalos*, "navel" of the world.
- "Know thyself" (*gnōthi seauton*).
- The Pythia: a title held by many women over ~1,000 years; up to three at once.
- 2001: two faults under the temple; ethylene traces in a spring (debated).
- Salamis, 480 BC: the "wooden wall" read as ships.
- Silenced by Theodosius, 391–392 AD.
- Kastri moved ~1 km west; Great Excavation 1892–1903; Charioteer c. 470 BC, buried 373 BC.
