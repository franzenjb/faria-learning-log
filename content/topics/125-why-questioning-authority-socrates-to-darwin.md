In 399 BCE, an Athenian jury sentenced Socrates to drink poison, because he would not stop asking why. Twenty-two centuries later, a cautious English naturalist asked why living kinds should stay as they were first made. Between those two questions stand a Dominican who thought faith and reason could be allies, a Dutch scholar who laughed the Church's abuses into the open, a German monk whose protest spread on the printing press, and an Italian mathematician made to kneel for saying the Earth moves. This lesson follows one word, why?, from Socrates to Darwin.

## The Key Ideas

- **Socrates, 399 BCE.** The fuller story is [Socrates: The Gadfly of Athens](../124-socrates-gadfly-of-athens/). He wrote nothing himself; we know him mainly through Plato, and also through Xenophon and Aristophanes. He tested what people thought they knew. The Greek name for that test is *elenchus*. In Benjamin Jowett's translation of the *Apology*, he says, "I neither know nor think that I know." The familiar short form, that he knew only that he knew nothing, is later shorthand. In 399 BCE he was tried for impiety and for corrupting the young, and executed by hemlock.
- **Aquinas, 1265–1274.** Thomas Aquinas, about 1225–1274, spent his last decade on the *Summa Theologiae*, left unfinished at his death. Aristotle had come back into the Latin universities, often through Arabic commentators. Aquinas treated faith and reason as allies. In the 1920 Dominican translation: "grace does not destroy nature but perfects it." Each article of the *Summa* states the objections first. The question "Whether God exists?" opens, "It seems that God does not exist."
- **Erasmus, 1511.** Erasmus of Rotterdam, about 1466–1536, wrote *The Praise of Folly* in 1509; it was printed in 1511. Folly walks through courts, universities, and the Church. Of people who trusted purchased pardons, John Wilson's 1668 English has her mock those who "hugg themselves with their counterfeit Pardons" and have "measur'd Purgatory by an Hour-glass." A saying of the time ran that Erasmus laid the egg that Luther hatched. The line is not his, and he did not accept it as a compliment. He wanted reform from inside, not a break.
- **Luther, 1517 and 1521.** On 31 October 1517, Luther sent the Ninety-five Theses on indulgences to Archbishop Albert of Mainz. The picture of him nailing them to the Castle Church door is traditional but debated; it comes down from later telling, especially from Melanchthon, who was not in Wittenberg in 1517. What is not in doubt is that the theses were printed and traveled. In the 1915 Holman translation, thesis 27 attacks the claim that "so soon as the penny jingles into the money-box, the soul flies out." At the Diet of Worms, in April 1521, he refused to recant. "Here I stand, I can do no other" appears in later printed versions of the speech and is probably a later embellishment. The refusal was real.
- **Galileo, 1633.** In 1632 he published the *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems*. In 1633 he was tried, forced to recant the moving Earth, and placed under house arrest at Arcetri. The line *Eppur si muove*—"And yet it moves"—is almost certainly a legend; it is first printed by Giuseppe Baretti in 1757. The fuller story is in [Galileo](../46-galileo/) and [Bruno and Galileo](../23-bruno-and-galileo/).
- **Darwin, 1859.** On 24 November 1859, John Murray in London issued *On the Origin of Species*. The idea that living kinds might change was not brand new; Darwin's own grandfather had speculated about it, and so had others, including Lamarck. What Darwin offered was evidence, and a mechanism. In the first edition he wrote, "I am fully convinced that species are not immutable," and that natural selection is "the main but not exclusive means of modification." The book ends with the "grandeur in this view of life." Later editions added "by the Creator" to that closing; those words are not in the 1859 text.

**Question.** Aquinas put the objections first, including "It seems that God does not exist." What does it do to an argument when the writer gives the other side a fair start?

**Question.** "Here I stand" is probably a later embellishment, and "And yet it moves" is almost certainly a legend. Why do we keep repeating lines that were probably never spoken?

## The Last Word

The authority being questioned changes—the city, the Church, the sky, the living world. The habit does not. Two of the most famous sentences in this story are probably later additions. The real courage is quieter, and better documented. The last word is not an answer. It is the question, still open: why?

## Key facts

- Socrates, about 470–399 BCE, wrote nothing himself and was executed in 399 BCE. In Jowett's *Apology*, "I neither know nor think that I know."
- Aquinas, about 1225–1274, wrote the *Summa Theologiae* about 1265–1274. Faith and reason are allies: "grace does not destroy nature but perfects it." The objections come first.
- Erasmus published *The Praise of Folly* in 1511. The saying that he laid the egg Luther hatched is not his own.
- Luther sent the Ninety-five Theses on 31 October 1517. The door-nailing is debated. At Worms, in April 1521, he refused to recant. "Here I stand" is probably a later embellishment.
- Galileo's *Dialogue* appeared in 1632; he was tried in 1633 and held at Arcetri. *Eppur si muove* is first printed in 1757 and is almost certainly a legend.
- Darwin's *Origin* was published on 24 November 1859. Species are not immutable; natural selection is "the main but not exclusive means of modification."
