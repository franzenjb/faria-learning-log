A friend of Socrates once made the climb to Delphi and asked the god a blunt question: was anyone wiser than Socrates? The priestess said no one. Socrates spent the rest of his life trying to prove her wrong. In failing, he found a kind of wisdom that still has his name on it.

It has his life and method, the trial of 399 BCE, the refusal to escape, the last words, a short look at the cycle of governments in Plato's *Republic*, six questions, and a note on why our picture of him is hard to pin down.

He was born in Athens about 470 or 469 BCE and died there in 399. He was the son of Sophroniscus and of Phaenarete, a midwife. Later tradition makes the father a stonemason, and sometimes gives Socrates the same trade; Plato does not say that. He served as a hoplite, an armored infantryman, at Potidaea, Delium, and Amphipolis—the three campaigns he names in the *Apology*, usually dated to about 432–429, 424, and 422 BCE.

## The Key Ideas

- **The method.** He did not lecture. He asked, and he kept asking until a confident answer came apart. The Greeks called this test an elenchus. In the *Euthyphro*, a man sure he knows what piety is says it is what the gods love. Socrates asks "whether the pious or holy is beloved by the gods because it is holy, or holy because it is beloved of the gods." The first makes "loved by the gods" a result, not a definition. The second makes piety only a matter of approval. They try other answers. None of them holds.
- **What he claimed to know.** The familiar slogan "I know that I know nothing" is a later paraphrase. In Benjamin Jowett's translation of the *Apology*, Socrates says, "I neither know nor think that I know." After the oracle's answer he tested the politicians, the poets, and the artisans, and decided that his only edge was this: he did not think he knew what he did not know.
- **The gadfly.** He told the jury he was attached to the city like a gadfly to a large, sleepy horse, "arousing and persuading and reproaching you" (*Apology* 30e, Jowett).
- **The trial.** In 399 BCE, Meletus, Anytus, and Lycon charged him with impiety and with corrupting the youth. A jury of about 500 citizens heard him. After the guilty vote he said that if thirty votes had gone the other way he would have been acquitted (*Apology* 36a). That is close, but we do not know the exact tally, or even whether the jury numbered 500 or 501. Asked for a penalty, he first proposed the honor given to Olympic victors: free meals in the Prytaneum. He also told the jury that "the unexamined life is not worth living" (38a). They voted for death.
- **The end.** Friends offered him a way out. In the *Crito* he refuses. The Laws of Athens, in a speech he imagines, ask whether a state can stand "in which the decisions of law have no power, but are set aside and trampled upon by individuals." In the *Phaedo* he drinks the hemlock, and his last words are, "Crito, I owe a cock to Asclepius; will you remember to pay the debt?"

He wrote nothing himself. We know him through Plato, through Xenophon, and through the comic Socrates of Aristophanes' *Clouds*. Those portraits do not agree. That gap is the Socratic problem. The early Platonic dialogues are often taken as closer to the man; in later ones, Socrates often speaks for Plato.

One of those later works is the *Republic*. In Book VIII, Plato describes a cycle of constitutions: timocracy, the rule of honor, gives way to oligarchy, the rule of wealth; oligarchy to democracy; and democracy, when freedom is pursued without limit, to tyranny. Excess freedom, he says, breeds a popular "protector" who turns into a tyrant. "This and no other is the root from which a tyrant springs; when he first appears above ground he is a protector" (Jowett, about 565).

:::callout
Plato was describing a cycle, not a person. The mechanism is what matters — demagogues exploit frustration, and the crowd mistakes volume for vision.
:::

**Question.** If Socrates' only claimed advantage is that he does not think he knows what he does not know, why would a city find that so hard to live with?

**Question.** He wrote nothing. If Plato, Xenophon, and Aristophanes disagree, what can we still fairly say about the man?

## The Last Word

He wrote nothing. What lasted is a habit of asking whether we know what we think we know. The cup is famous. The question is the thing that still stings, which is what a gadfly is for.

## Key facts

- Socrates lived in Athens about 470 or 469 to 399 BCE. Son of Sophroniscus and of Phaenarete, a midwife. The stonemason trade is later tradition.
- Hoplite at Potidaea, Delium, and Amphipolis, usually dated to about 432–429, 424, and 422 BCE.
- The elenchus tests a claim until it comes apart. In the *Euthyphro*: is the holy loved because it is holy, or holy because it is loved?
- Jowett, *Apology*: "I neither know nor think that I know." The slogan "I know that I know nothing" is a later paraphrase. Chaerephon asked the Delphic oracle whether anyone was wiser; the priestess said no one.
- The gadfly of Athens (*Apology* 30e). Trial in 399 BCE: Meletus, Anytus, and Lycon; a jury of about 500; thirty votes the other way would have acquitted (36a), though the exact tally is unknown. He proposed free meals in the Prytaneum, and said "the unexamined life is not worth living" (38a).
- He refused to escape (*Crito*). Last words (*Phaedo*): a cock owed to Asclepius. He wrote nothing. Plato, Xenophon, and Aristophanes' *Clouds* (423 BCE) disagree; that is the Socratic problem.
- *Republic* Book VIII: timocracy to oligarchy to democracy to tyranny; a popular "protector" becomes a tyrant (Jowett, about 565).
