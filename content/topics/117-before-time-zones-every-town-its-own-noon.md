Every Greek and Roman town kept its own time by the Sun. Noon was simply the moment the Sun stood highest over that town. A town a little to the west had a later noon. That was not a problem. Nobody needed to share a clock with a city days away.

## Twelve Hours of Daylight

Greeks and Romans split the daylight, from sunrise to sunset, into twelve hours, and the night into twelve more. An hour was not a fixed 60 minutes. It was one twelfth of that day's light, or of that night's darkness. Only at the equinoxes, when day and night are about equal, was an hour about 60 minutes.

At Rome, a little under 42° N, the difference is easy to feel. Ludwig Ideler's table for 45 BC, the year after Julius Caesar reformed the calendar, gives a summer-solstice day of **15 hours 6 minutes** and a winter-solstice day of **8 hours 54 minutes**. Divide each by twelve:

- a summer daylight hour in Rome was about **75 minutes**
- a winter daylight hour was about **45 minutes**

The night hours swap. A summer night hour is about 45 minutes; a winter night hour is about 75. The sixth daylight hour always ends at noon, when the Sun is highest, in every season.

## Time Changes as You Go East or West

The Greeks also worked out that clock time itself shifts as you travel east or west. [Hipparchus](../104-hipparchus-ptolemy-and-the-grid/), working on Rhodes around 150 BC, argued that the way to measure how far apart two cities were in longitude was to time the same **lunar eclipse** from both. A lunar eclipse begins at one instant for everyone who can see it. But the local hour is different in each city, because local time follows the Sun. Earth turns **15° every hour**. If the eclipse starts at the third hour of the night in one city and the fifth hour in another, the second city is two hours, or 30°, farther east.

It was the right idea. It was almost never carried out. You needed two careful observers, far apart, both watching the same eclipse and both keeping good local time.

## Hours East or West of Alexandria

[Ptolemy](../104-hipparchus-ptolemy-and-the-grid/), writing in Alexandria around AD 150, put that idea into a book. Most of his *Geography* lists places in degrees, counted east of the Fortunate Isles, usually identified with the Canary Islands. Book 8 does it another way. For about **360 noteworthy cities**, the *poleis episemoi*, he gives the difference in hours east or west of Alexandria, and the length of the longest day there. Those hours are equinoctial hours, the even 60-minute kind, not the stretching seasonal hours of a sundial. One hour still means 15°.

## Until the Train and the Telegraph

So the idea behind time zones is old: local time follows the Sun, and a difference in hours is a difference in longitude. For most of history no one needed to share a clock across cities. A railroad timetable is another matter, and the telegraph made it possible to send the same time signal to many clocks at once.

On **18 November 1883**, the "Day of Two Noons," U.S. railroads adopted standard time zones on their own authority. In some cities the clocks showed noon twice: once at the old local sun time, and again at the new standard time. Federal law followed only in 1918. The next year, in October 1884, the [International Meridian Conference](../106-the-1884-meridian-conference/) in Washington recommended Greenwich as zero longitude. The fuller story of the 15° fact, the noon that walks west across the United States, and how those zones were drawn is on [Every Town Its Own Noon](../116-every-town-its-own-noon/).
