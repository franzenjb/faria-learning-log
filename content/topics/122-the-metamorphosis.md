Gregor Samsa wakes up as an enormous insect, and his first worry is not his body. It is the train. "My train leaves at five," he thinks, and it is already half past six. Yesterday's lesson was a man who said he wished he could become an insect. Today's is the man it happened to.

## The Key Ideas

- **A word with no English match.** The German says Gregor became an *ungeheueres Ungeziefer*. *Ungeheuer* means monstrous; *Ungeziefer* is not one animal but a general word for vermin, the pests you want out of the house. Translators choose a loss: "gigantic insect" (Muir, 1933), "monstrous vermin" (Corngold, 1972), "horrible vermin" (Wyllie). No species is ever named.
- **"The insect itself cannot be drawn."** On 25 October 1915 Kafka wrote to his publisher, fearing the illustrator would draw Gregor (translated here): "Not that, please not that!... It cannot even be shown from a distance." Starke drew a man and an open door instead.
- **Written fast, published late.** Kafka wrote it in November and December 1912, at night, after his day job. It came out in October 1915 in the magazine *Die weißen Blätter*, then as a book that November.
- **The real change is in the family.** Gregor was the only earner, paying off his father's debt to his own boss. Once he cannot work, he becomes "it." The father goes back to work and wears his bank uniform even at home. The mother sews underwear for a shop. His sister Grete, seventeen, moves from caretaker to judge.
- **The impossible, told calmly.** There is no explanation and no dream. Kafka writes like a report: the time on the clock, the train he missed, how to turn a key with no hands. From 1908 to 1922 he was a legal official at the Workers' Accident Insurance Institute in Prague, sorting firms by risk and writing on accident prevention. He knew what a catastrophe looks like by the time it reaches the paperwork.

## Three Escapes From the Room

Each of the three parts turns on one attempt to leave the room, and each costs Gregor more.

![Three times out of the room, three times driven back. Each escape costs Gregor more and gives the family more reason.](../../img/Metamorphosis_Three_Escapes_From_the_Room_Faria_2026-11-23.svg)

1. **The chief clerk.** His firm sends the chief clerk to the flat shortly after seven. Gregor pleads through the door that he'll catch the eight o'clock train. "That was the voice of an animal," says the clerk. The father drives Gregor back with the clerk's own stick.
2. **The apple.** His mother faints at the sight of him. The father, back from work in a uniform with gold buttons, throws apples. One "lodged in his back" and stays there, rotting, for more than a month.
3. **The violin.** Grete plays for the family's three lodgers, who are bored. Gregor crawls out, moved: "Was he an animal if music could captivate him so?" The lodgers give notice, and Grete says it: "I don't want to call this monster my brother, all I can say is: we have to try and get rid of it."

He goes back to his room. He thinks of his family "with emotion and love," feeling "that he must go away even more strongly than his sister," and dies before dawn.

**Question.** Grete cared for Gregor more than anyone did, and she is the one who passes sentence. Is that betrayal, or exhaustion?

## The Ending

The charwoman finds the body and gets rid of it. The family takes the day off and rides the tram out into the country, in warm sunshine at the end of March. Their prospects, they decide, "were not at all bad." They will move to a smaller flat, not the one "chosen by Gregor." The last image is not Gregor. It is his parents noticing "how their daughter was blossoming," and Grete standing up to "stretch out her young body."

## From the Underground to Gregor's Room

In [Notes from Underground (topic 121)](../121-notes-from-underground/), Dostoevsky's narrator says: "I have many times tried to become an insect. But I was not equal even to that." Kafka makes the figure of speech literal. He named Dostoevsky as one of his "true blood relations" in a 1913 letter, though no one has shown he had this passage in mind. The difference: the Underground Man chose his corner and never stops talking. Gregor was put in his room, loves his family to the end, and cannot be understood at all.

## What Catastrophe Reveals

This is a reading for disaster work, not research. The transformation does not create the Samsas' troubles. It exposes them: the debt, the job Gregor hated, the mother's illness, the daughter's unaffordable dream. The person doing the most care, Grete, is the one who breaks. Gregor loses his standing the moment he can no longer earn. And the household recovers, quickly and in sunshine, faster than the person at the center. As with the [peak-end rule](../120-peak-end-rule/), they will probably remember the whole ordeal through that bright ending.

**Question.** In a disaster response, who is your Grete: the person carrying the most, quietly, until suddenly they cannot? What would it take to notice her a month earlier?

## The Last Word

Kafka asked that the insect never be drawn, and he was right. The story is not about what Gregor became. It is about what everyone else turned out to be once he stopped being useful.

## Key facts
- *Die Verwandlung* was written in November–December 1912 and published in October 1915 (*Die weißen Blätter*), then as a book by Kurt Wolff, Leipzig.
- *Ungeheueres Ungeziefer*: "monstrous vermin" (Corngold), "gigantic insect" (Muir), "horrible vermin" (Wyllie). No species is named.
- Kafka to his publisher, 25 October 1915: "The insect itself cannot be drawn. It cannot even be shown from a distance."
- Three escapes: the chief clerk and the stick; the apple lodged in his back; the violin, the lodgers, and Grete's "we have to try and get rid of it."
- Gregor was the sole earner; once useless he becomes "it," and the family goes back to work. After his death they ride the tram into the country and notice Grete has blossomed.
- Kafka worked from 1908 to 1922 at the Workers' Accident Insurance Institute in Prague.
- It makes literal the Underground Man's wish to "become an insect" (topic 121).
